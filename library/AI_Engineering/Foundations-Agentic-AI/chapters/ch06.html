Part II · Reasoning and Planning — Chapter 6

Search and Planning

A chain of thought follows one path; voting follows several and counts heads. Real problems branch, and the best way through them is often to try something, judge where it leads, and back up. This chapter brings the search algorithms of classical AI back into the language-model era, and asks the question that decides whether they help: how good is the judge?

Why this chapter

Chapter 5 showed that spending more computation on a decision raises its accuracy, through longer reasoning or through voting over independent attempts. Both have the same blind spot. A single chain cannot go back: once it commits to a wrong approach it rarely recovers. Voting cannot recognise quality: a single brilliant solution among many mediocre ones is outvoted, and on free-form outputs such as code or plans there is nothing to vote on. What is missing is an evaluator, something that can look at a partial or complete solution and say how good it is, and a search procedure that uses the evaluator to decide where to spend effort.

This is the combination that made AlphaGo work (§1.5): a learned policy proposing moves, a learned value function judging positions, and Monte Carlo tree search allocating simulations between them. It is also the structure of the classical planners of §1.3, with a heuristic in place of the value function. This chapter rebuilds that structure with language models in every role: as the proposer of candidate thoughts or actions, as the evaluator of their promise, and, in some designs, as the translator that hands a problem to a classical planner. We will see that the benefit of search is governed by a simple quantity, the gap between how well a model can generate solutions and how well something can verify them, and we will compute what search costs, which for agents acting in real environments can be prohibitive.

Learning objectives
  1. Analyse best-of-\(n\) sampling with a perfect and an imperfect verifier, and derive its accuracy ceiling.
  2. Explain reward-model over-optimisation and the KL cost of best-of-\(n\).
  3. Describe Tree of Thoughts and its breadth-first and depth-first variants, with the evidence for them.
  4. State the UCT rule of Monte Carlo tree search and explain how LATS applies it to agents.
  5. Compare plan-and-execute agents, LLM-generated plans checked by classical planners, and the evidence on whether LLMs can plan.
  6. Compute the cost of a search strategy, and state the conditions (reversibility, verifier quality, budget) under which search helps an agent.

We use pass@\(k\) (§1.10), the decision-process view (Chapter 2), and self-consistency and test-time scaling (§5.4, §5.7).

6.1 From sampling to search

Consider the coding agent facing a bug that has three plausible causes. A single chain of reasoning picks one, writes a fix, and runs the tests; if the fix fails, it has spent most of its budget on the wrong path. Self-consistency would have the agent generate several complete fixes and pick the most common, which is useless when the fixes are all different and only one is right. What a human engineer does instead is different from both: consider the three hypotheses, run a quick check for each (add a print statement, read the relevant function), drop the two that the checks rule out, and invest effort in the survivor. That is search: generate options, evaluate them cheaply, expand the promising ones, and abandon the rest.

Every search method in this chapter has the same three ingredients, and it helps to name them before meeting the variants. A generator proposes candidates: complete solutions, partial solutions (thoughts), or actions in an environment. An evaluator scores them: a test suite, a learned reward model, the language model itself asked "how promising is this?", or the environment's response. A search strategy decides which candidates to expand, given the scores and a budget: sample many and keep the best, explore a tree breadth-first or depth-first, or balance exploration and exploitation as Monte Carlo tree search does. The strategies differ in how they spend the budget, but the outcome of all of them is bounded by the evaluator: search can only find what the evaluator can recognise.

6.2 Best-of-n and the quality of the verifier

The simplest search is best-of-\(n\) (also called rejection sampling or sample-and-rank): sample \(n\) complete candidates independently and return the one the evaluator scores highest. Cobbe and colleagues introduced it for mathematics in 2021, training a verifier to judge solutions to GSM8K problems and finding that sampling 100 solutions and picking the verifier's favourite gave a boost comparable to a thirtyfold increase in model size[1]. Figure 6.1 shows the pipeline, which is the backbone of much of what follows.

With a perfect verifier, one that recognises a correct candidate whenever one exists, best-of-\(n\) succeeds exactly when at least one candidate is correct: its accuracy is pass@\(n\) \(=1-(1-p)^n\), where \(p\) is the per-sample success probability (§1.10). This is the upper bound for any selection method over \(n\) samples, and it grows quickly: a coding agent that resolves an issue on 30% of attempts resolves it with probability 0.97 given ten attempts and a reliable checker. When the hidden tests of a benchmark are available to the selector, best-of-\(n\) approaches this bound, which is why benchmark results quoted "pass@\(k\)" should never be compared with single-attempt results.

Real verifiers are imperfect. Model them by two error rates: a correct candidate is accepted with probability \(1-\epsilon_-\) (false-negative rate \(\epsilon_-\)) and an incorrect one with probability \(\epsilon_+\) (false-positive rate). Suppose we return a uniformly random accepted candidate (or any candidate if none is accepted). As \(n\) grows, there will almost surely be many accepted candidates of both kinds, and the chance that the returned one is correct tends to the verifier's precision on the candidate distribution:

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr[\text{best-of-}n\text{ correct}]=\frac{p(1-\epsilon_-)}{p(1-\epsilon_-)+(1-p)\,\epsilon_+}. \]

This ceiling, not pass@\(n\), is what an imperfect verifier can achieve, and it can be low. With \(p=0.3\), \(\epsilon_-=0.1\) and \(\epsilon_+=0.1\), it is \(0.27/0.34=0.79\); with \(\epsilon_+=0.3\), only 0.56. Figure 6.2 plots the exact accuracy as a function of \(n\) for these cases.

Figure 6.2 is the most important picture in this chapter. With a good verifier (blue), sampling more is an almost free path to reliability. With a mediocre one (orange), the curve flattens after a handful of samples at a level set entirely by the verifier, and the only way up is a better verifier. The asymmetry is worth stating plainly: false positives matter far more than false negatives. A verifier that rejects some correct candidates merely wastes samples, since another correct one will usually be accepted; a verifier that accepts wrong ones lets them through, and as \(n\) grows there are more wrong ones to let through. A coding agent's hidden test suite has a very low false-positive rate if the tests are thorough, which is why best-of-\(n\) works so well for code with tests. A model asked "is this patch correct?" may have a false-positive rate of 20% or more, which is why best-of-\(n\) with a model judge often disappoints.

Worked example — best-of-n for the coding agent

The coding agent resolves an issue on \(p=0.3\) of attempts at $1.15 per attempt (§1.10). The repository has its own test suite, which the agent may run, but the hidden tests that define success are unavailable. The agent's own tests accept a correct patch 90% of the time and a wrong patch 20% of the time.

Ceiling. \(0.27/(0.27+0.7\times0.2)=0.27/0.41=0.66\).

Cost. Eight attempts ($9.20) get close to the ceiling. Compared with one attempt: accuracy from 0.30 to about 0.64 for eight times the cost, $14.40 per resolved issue against $3.83 for single attempts ($1.15 / 0.30) if failed attempts are free to retry later, or a large gain if each unresolved issue costs an engineer an hour.

Improving the verifier. If the agent first writes a test that reproduces the bug (it should fail before the fix and pass after), the false-positive rate of its checks might fall to 5%. The ceiling rises to \(0.27/(0.27+0.035)=0.885\). A better verifier is worth more than any number of extra samples.

Selecting without a verifier: filtering and clustering

Sometimes there is no reliable verifier for the final output, but there are partial checks. DeepMind's AlphaCode (2022), which reached roughly the median of human competitors in programming contests, showed how far partial checks can be pushed[2]. It sampled up to a million candidate programs per problem, discarded those that failed the few example tests given in the problem statement (about 99% of them), and then, since it could submit only ten, clustered the survivors by their behaviour: it generated new test inputs, ran every surviving program on them, grouped programs that produced identical outputs, and submitted one program from each of the largest clusters. Clustering is self-consistency (§5.4) generalised to outputs that cannot be compared directly: two programs "agree" if they behave the same, and the most common behaviour is likely the correct one, for the same reason that the most common numerical answer is. For agents, the same two-stage pattern (a cheap filter that removes obviously bad candidates, then agreement among survivors) applies to patches, SQL queries and any output that can be executed. Its weakness is shared with voting: if most candidates share the same misconception, the largest cluster is wrong.

Over-optimisation and the KL cost of selection

When the evaluator is a learned reward model, a further effect appears. A reward model is a proxy for what we actually want, accurate on typical outputs and less so on unusual ones. Selecting the best of \(n\) by proxy pushes towards outputs the proxy scores highly, and as \(n\) grows these increasingly include outputs that exploit the proxy's errors. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured this carefully, using a large "gold" reward model as ground truth and smaller proxies for selection: as best-of-\(n\) selection intensified, gold reward first rose and then fell, while proxy reward kept rising[3]. This is Goodhart's law in quantitative form, and it applies to every search guided by a learned evaluator, including the RL training of Chapter 18.

A useful measure of how far selection moves the output distribution is its KL divergence from the base distribution. For best-of-\(n\) with a continuous score, the distribution of the selected sample has a closed form, and its divergence from the sampling distribution is \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\log n-(n-1)/n\) nats. Selection is therefore a gentle optimiser: best-of-16 moves the distribution by about 1.8 nats and best-of-256 by about 4.5. The logarithm explains both why best-of-\(n\) is relatively robust to over-optimisation compared with RL, which can travel much further in KL, and why it is an expensive way to optimise: each additional nat of movement multiplies the sample count by about \(e\).

Drill 6.1 — the verifier's ceiling

A research agent's candidate answers are correct with probability 0.5. A model judge accepts correct answers 95% of the time and incorrect ones 25% of the time. (a) What is the best-of-\(n\) ceiling? (b) What false-positive rate would be needed for a 95% ceiling?

(a) \(0.475/(0.475+0.125)=0.792\). (b) Need \(0.475/(0.475+0.5\epsilon_+)\ge0.95\), so \(0.5\epsilon_+\le0.025\), \(\epsilon_+\le0.05\). The judge must reject 95% of wrong answers, a much harder standard than accepting right ones.

Drill 6.4 — clustering candidate patches

An agent generates 20 patches; 12 fail the repository's existing tests and are discarded. On 30 generated inputs, the 8 survivors fall into behaviour clusters of sizes 5, 2 and 1. If patches are correct with probability 0.3 before filtering, and the filter never discards a correct patch, what is the chance that at least one survivor is correct? Which patch would you submit, and what could make that choice wrong?

Correct patches are expected to be \(0.3\times20=6\) of 20; all survive, so a survivor is correct unless all 6 expected correct ones are absent: \(1-0.7^{20}\approx0.9992\) that at least one exists. Submit one from the cluster of 5, since correct patches behave identically and are the most likely to agree. The choice is wrong if the 5 share a common mistake (the generated inputs miss the buggy case), so add inputs that exercise the reported bug.

6.3 Tree of Thoughts

Best-of-\(n\) evaluates only complete solutions. For problems where most attempts go wrong early, that is wasteful: if the first step of a solution is a bad choice, everything after it is wasted computation. Searching over partial solutions lets the evaluator prune bad starts before they are completed. In May 2023 two groups proposed this for language models at almost the same time; the formulation by Yao and colleagues, Tree of Thoughts (ToT), became the standard reference[4].

ToT treats problem solving as search in a tree whose nodes are partial solutions, sequences of "thoughts", each a coherent intermediate step such as a line of an equation or a paragraph of a plan. At each node the language model proposes several candidate next thoughts, and the language model (in a separate prompt) evaluates each resulting state, either by scoring it ("sure / likely / impossible to reach the goal from here") or by voting among siblings. A search algorithm, breadth-first with a beam of the best \(b\) states at each depth or depth-first with back-tracking when a state is judged impossible, decides what to expand. Figure 6.3 shows a breadth-first search with pruning.

Figure 6.3 shows why ToT can succeed where chain of thought fails. On the Game of 24, a puzzle in which four numbers must be combined with arithmetic to make 24, GPT-4 with chain-of-thought prompting solved 4% of problems in the paper's test set; with ToT and a beam of five, it solved 74%. The puzzle is ideal for tree search: each step (combine two numbers) is small, the evaluator can often see that a remaining set of numbers cannot reach 24 ("10, 13, 13: impossible"), and pruning early saves almost all the work. On tasks without such crisp intermediate evaluations, gains are smaller, and the cost is high: the Game of 24 results used on the order of a hundred model calls per problem, against one for chain of thought.

Evaluating steps, not just outcomes

Search over partial solutions needs an evaluator of partial solutions, and a verifier of final answers does not provide one. Lightman and colleagues at OpenAI (2023) compared two kinds of learned verifier for mathematics: an outcome reward model (ORM), trained to predict whether a complete solution's final answer is correct, and a process reward model (PRM), trained on human labels of each step of a solution as correct, incorrect or neutral[5]. Used to pick the best of many sampled solutions to problems from the MATH benchmark, the PRM chose a correct solution 78.2% of the time against 72.4% for the ORM. Step-level supervision helps for a reason the chapter has already met: a solution can reach the right answer by a wrong route, and an outcome verifier trained on final answers learns to accept such solutions, a source of false positives; a process verifier rejects them. A PRM also scores partial solutions directly, so it can guide beam search or MCTS at every step rather than only at the end.

For agents, the analogue of a process reward is a judgement about each action: was this the right file to open, was this edit sensible, was that question to the customer necessary? Such step-level signals are expensive to label by hand, and much current work derives them automatically, for example by estimating a step's value from the success rate of rollouts that continue from it, which is exactly the Monte Carlo evaluation of the next section. The deep difficulty is the same as in reinforcement learning's credit-assignment problem (Chapter 18): when an episode fails after forty steps, which step was to blame?

ToT made explicit something that reasoning models (§5.6) later internalised. A model trained by RL to reason at length explores alternatives, judges them and back-tracks within a single chain of thought, which is a tree search linearised into text, with the model as its own proposer and evaluator. Explicit tree search remains useful when the evaluator is external and reliable (a test, a checker, the environment), when the search must be parallelised, or when the tree is too large for one context. When the only evaluator is the model's own judgement, a strong reasoning model often matches explicit ToT at lower cost.

6.4 Monte Carlo tree search and LATS

Breadth-first search with a fixed beam spends the same effort at every depth, whatever the evaluator says. A better allocation spends more where the evaluator is optimistic but uncertain and less where it is confident, balancing exploitation of branches that look good with exploration of branches that might be better than they look. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), the algorithm at the heart of AlphaGo, does this with a rule from the theory of multi-armed bandits.

MCTS builds a tree incrementally by repeating four phases, drawn in Figure 6.4. Selection: starting at the root, descend by choosing at each node the child that maximises an upper confidence bound. Expansion: at a leaf, add one or more children by proposing actions. Evaluation: estimate the value of the new node, by a learned value function, by a heuristic, or by a rollout that plays the episode to the end with a fast policy and observes the outcome. Back-propagation: update the value estimates and visit counts of every node on the path to the root. The selection rule most often used is UCT (Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006)[6]:

\[ a^*=\arg\max_{a}\Big[\hat Q(s,a)+c_{\text{uct}}\sqrt{\frac{\ln N(s)}{N(s,a)}}\Big], \]

where \(\hat Q(s,a)\) is the mean value observed below child \(a\), \(N(s)\) and \(N(s,a)\) are the visit counts of the node and the child, and \(c_{\text{uct}}\) sets the strength of exploration. The second term is large for rarely visited children, so every child is eventually tried, but it grows only logarithmically, so effort concentrates on children whose observed value is high. This is the upper-confidence-bound algorithm for bandits applied recursively at every node, and it inherits that algorithm's guarantee of logarithmic regret.

Figure 6.4's evaluation step is where language-model agents differ from game-playing programs. In Go, a rollout is cheap (a fast policy plays to the end in microseconds) and the outcome is exact. In an agent environment, a rollout means actually running the agent: executing code, browsing pages, calling APIs. Language Agent Tree Search (LATS; Zhou et al., 2023) adapted MCTS to this setting[7]. Its nodes are states of an agent trajectory; expansion samples several candidate actions from the language model; evaluation combines a language-model score of the state with the environment's feedback (for code, test results); and when a trajectory fails, the model writes a reflection on why (as in Reflexion, Chapter 7), which is stored and used to guide later expansions. With GPT-4 on the HumanEval programming benchmark, LATS reported 92.7% pass@1 (one final submission, after search guided by the visible test cases), and it improved results on the WebShop shopping environment and on multi-hop question answering.

The phrase "after search" hides the main practical constraint, which applies to every method in this chapter that searches over actions in a real environment: search requires the ability to go back. MCTS revisits nodes; ToT expands several children of the same state; both assume that the agent can return to an earlier state and try a different action. In a game or a simulator, that is a matter of copying the state. In a code sandbox, it can be done with snapshots of the file system (Chapter 10). On the live web, in a customer conversation or against a production database, it is impossible: a sent message cannot be unsent and a refund cannot be un-issued. For such environments, search must happen over plans or in a model of the environment, not in the environment itself, which brings us to planning.

6.5 Planning

A plan is a decision about a sequence of actions made before executing them. Classical AI took planning to be the heart of intelligent agency (§1.3); reactive agents rejected it (§1.4); language agents have revived it in a pragmatic form. The case for planning in an agent is that it moves some of the search out of the irreversible environment and into the model's reasoning, where trying an alternative costs only tokens.

The simplest planning architecture is plan-and-execute. A planner call produces a list of steps ("1. look up the booking; 2. search Friday flights before noon; 3. compute the fare difference; 4. confirm with the customer; 5. change the booking"); an executor, which may be a cheaper model or a fixed workflow, carries them out one by one; and when an observation contradicts the plan (no Friday flights before noon), control returns to the planner to replan. Figure 6.5 shows the loop. Variants such as ReWOO plan all tool calls up front with placeholders for their results, saving the tokens of re-reading the history at every step[8]. The benefits are cost (one expensive planning call, many cheap execution calls), interpretability (the plan can be shown to a user or checked before anything happens) and commitment in the BDI sense of §1.4 (the agent does not reconsider everything at every step). The cost is brittleness: a plan made before observing the environment encodes guesses, and an executor that follows a stale plan faithfully does the wrong thing efficiently.

Worked example — what plan-and-execute saves

A ten-step support task. ReAct with the frontier model: each step reads a 10,000-token context (9,000 cached, 1,000 fresh) and writes 300 tokens. Per step: \(0.009\times\$0.30+0.001\times\$3+0.0003\times\$15=\$0.0027+\$0.003+\$0.0045=\$0.0102\); ten steps: $0.102. Decode time: \(10\times300/60=50\) s.

Plan-and-execute. One frontier planning call reads 10,000 tokens (8,000 cached) and writes a 3,000-token reasoned plan: \(\$0.0024+\$0.006+\$0.045=\$0.053\). Ten executor steps on the small model, each reading 10,000 tokens (cached reads assumed at a tenth of its $0.25 input price) and writing 200: about $0.00075 each, $0.0075 in all. Total $0.061, 40% less. Decode time: \(3{,}000/60+10\times200/150=50+13=63\) s, slightly more, because the plan is long and written by the slow model.

Replanning. If an observation contradicts the plan in 30% of tasks, each costing one more planning call, the expected cost rises by \(0.3\times\$0.053=\$0.016\) to $0.077, still a quarter cheaper.

Lesson. Plan-and-execute moves expensive judgement to one call and cheap execution to many, saving money; it does not save time unless the plan is short or execution is parallel. If surprises are frequent, replanning erodes the saving and a step-by-step loop may be better.

Can language models plan well? On this question there has been a sharp and productive argument. Valmeekam, Kambhampati and colleagues built PlanBench, a set of classical planning problems such as Blocksworld (stack blocks in a goal configuration) presented in natural language, and found that models of 2023, GPT-4 included, produced valid plans for only a small fraction of problems, the best of them (GPT-4) about 12% on average across their domains in the autonomous setting, and that performance collapsed when the domain was obfuscated by renaming objects and actions, suggesting retrieval of familiar patterns rather than planning[9]. Reasoning models improved these numbers substantially, but the critique's structural point stands: a language model's plan has no guarantee of validity, and on long or unfamiliar problems it often violates constraints it was told about.

The constructive response, which Kambhampati calls LLM-Modulo, is to pair the model with external verifiers or solvers, as in the right half of Figure 6.5. In LLM+P (Liu et al., 2023), the model translates a natural-language task into the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL), the descendant of STRIPS (§1.3), and an off-the-shelf classical planner finds a plan, which is sound and often optimal by construction[10]. The model does what it does well (understanding the task and formalising it) and the planner does what it does well (combinatorial search with guarantees). The weak point moves to the translation, which can be wrong, but a wrong translation is much easier to check, by a human or by validating against examples, than a wrong plan. For agents, the lesson generalises beyond PDDL: wherever a task has formal structure (scheduling, routing, constraint satisfaction, eligibility rules), let the model formalise and a solver solve.

6.6 What search costs

Search buys accuracy with computation, and in agents computation is expensive enough that the purchase must be justified. The arithmetic is simple. Best-of-\(n\) costs \(n\) complete attempts. A breadth-first tree search with branching factor \(b_{\text{prop}}\) proposals per node, beam width \(b\) and depth \(\Lambda\) makes about \(\Lambda\,b\,b_{\text{prop}}\) proposal calls and as many evaluation calls. MCTS with \(I\) iterations makes about \(I\) expansions and evaluations, and each evaluation by rollout costs a full episode from that node. Since a full agent episode costs on the order of a dollar (§1.10), a search over twenty rollouts costs twenty dollars, and must be worth it.

Whether it is worth it depends on what a failure costs. Let one attempt cost \(c\), let a failure cost \(F\) (an engineer's time, a lost customer), and let \(A(n)\) be the accuracy of best-of-\(n\). The expected total cost of using \(n\) attempts is \(nc+(1-A(n))F\). Figure 6.6 plots it for the coding agent of the worked example in §6.2, with \(c=\$1.15\), \(F=\$50\) and the three verifiers of Figure 6.2.

Figure 6.6 turns the qualitative lessons into a decision rule. The optimal number of attempts balances the marginal cost of one more attempt, \(c\), against its marginal reduction in failure cost, \((A(n+1)-A(n))F\). With a perfect verifier, accuracy keeps rising until it saturates, so search pays up to a point and then stops; with a weak verifier, accuracy plateaus early at the verifier's precision, and every attempt beyond the plateau is wasted. Raising \(F\) (higher stakes) moves the optimum to more search, and lowering \(c\) (a cheaper model for attempts) does the same. A frequently overlooked option is to spend the budget on the verifier instead: a reproduction test that halves the false-positive rate moves the whole orange curve down towards the blue one.

Drill 6.2 — the cost of a tree

A Tree-of-Thoughts search uses depth 3, beam width 5 and 5 proposals per state, with one proposal call and one evaluation call per candidate. Each call reads 3,000 cached tokens and writes 200. (a) How many calls and what cost? (b) Compare with a single chain of thought of 600 output tokens.

(a) Per depth, \(5\times5=25\) candidates, each with two calls: 50 calls; over 3 depths, 150 calls. Each costs \(0.003\times\$0.30+0.0002\times\$15=\$0.0009+\$0.003=\$0.0039\): total $0.585. (b) The chain: \(0.003\times\$0.30+0.0006\times\$15=\$0.0099\). The tree costs about 59 times as much, justified only if it turns failures into successes on problems that matter, as on the Game of 24.

6.7 When does search help an agent?

We can now state the conditions under which an agent should search rather than act once, and they follow from the pieces of this chapter.

A verifier much better than the generator. Search helps when it is easier to check a solution than to produce one, which is to say when the generator–verifier gap is large. Code with good tests, mathematics with checkable answers, puzzles with crisp constraints and formal problems with solvers all have large gaps. Open-ended writing, judgement calls and customer conversations have small ones: judging a report is nearly as hard as writing it, and model judges of such outputs have false-positive rates that cap best-of-\(n\) at modest levels (Figure 6.2). Where the gap is small, a stronger model or more reasoning usually beats search.

Reversibility or a model. Searching over actions requires returning to earlier states: snapshots in a sandbox, a simulator, or a dry-run mode for tools. In irreversible environments the search must move into planning (search over plans in the model's head or in a solver) followed by careful execution, or into a simulated copy of the environment.

Stakes that justify the budget. The cost arithmetic of §6.6 requires that the value of turning failures into successes exceed the cost of the search. For high-value, low-volume tasks (a production bug, a large refund decision, a research question for an executive) this is easy; for high-volume routine traffic it rarely is.

Problems in the "learnable" difficulty range. Search turns a small per-attempt success probability into a large one only if that probability is not zero. A model that never produces a correct candidate gains nothing from more candidates. Snell and colleagues' finding (§5.7) that test-time compute is most valuable on problems of intermediate difficulty for the model is the same observation.

A verifier that cannot be gamed. There is one more condition, easy to overlook because it concerns failure rather than success. Search is optimisation, and optimisation against an imperfect evaluator finds the evaluator's blind spots (§6.2). A coding agent searching for a patch that makes the tests pass will, given enough attempts, find patches that pass by special-casing the test inputs, deleting an assertion or catching and silencing the exception the test checks for. Each such patch is a false positive the verifier failed to reject, and the more search, the more likely one is selected. The defences are the ones a careful engineer would apply anyway: hold some tests back from the agent so that it cannot optimise against them, check the diff for edits to test files, review patches that pass suspiciously quickly, and prefer verifiers whose failure modes are understood. Chapter 18 meets the same phenomenon when the search is replaced by reinforcement learning, where it is called reward hacking and is, if anything, worse.

When these conditions hold, search is one of the most reliable ways to make an agent better without changing the model. When they do not, the budget is better spent on the verifier, the context, the tools, or a stronger model. In every case, the evaluator is the component to scrutinise first.

Worked example — planning a support conversation without search

The support agent cannot search over actions: once it tells the customer "your flight is changed", it cannot try something else. A customer wants to move a trip with two connecting flights and a hotel booked through the airline.

Plan in the head. The agent first calls tools that only read (look up the booking, search alternative flights, check the hotel's cancellation terms), then writes a plan in its reasoning, considering two alternatives: change both flights to Friday morning (fare difference $140, hotel night lost, $95 non-refundable) or change only the second flight (fare difference $60, a six-hour layover).

Check before committing. A fare-calculation tool confirms both numbers; a policy tool confirms both changes are allowed. The agent presents both options with their costs, and the customer chooses.

Execute. Only now does it call the tools that write, and it reads the booking back to verify.

Structure. All the search happened over plans, using read-only tools as a model of the environment; the irreversible actions were executed once, after a check and the customer's confirmation. Separating read tools from write tools is what makes this possible, a design principle Chapter 8 develops.

Drill 6.3 — UCT by hand

A node has been visited \(N(s)=20\) times. Child A has \(\hat Q=0.6\) from 15 visits; child B has \(\hat Q=0.4\) from 5 visits. With \(c_{\text{uct}}=1\), which child does UCT select? With \(c_{\text{uct}}=0.3\)?

\(\ln20=3.0\). A: \(0.6+\sqrt{3.0/15}=0.6+0.447=1.047\). B: \(0.4+\sqrt{3.0/5}=0.4+0.775=1.175\). B is selected: it is under-explored. With \(c_{\text{uct}}=0.3\): A \(0.6+0.134=0.734\), B \(0.4+0.232=0.632\); A is selected. The constant sets how much evidence is needed before a branch is abandoned.

Looking ahead

Every method in this chapter depends on an evaluator, and the best results came where the evaluator was external: tests, checkers, solvers, the environment itself. Often, though, the only evaluator available is the model. Can a model critique its own outputs, learn from its own failures within an episode, and improve its answer by revising it? Reflexion's verbal post-mortems and LATS's reflections suggested yes; careful studies found that, without external feedback, models often cannot even recognise their own errors. When does self-correction work, what kind of feedback does it need, and how should verifiers and critics be built into an agent? Chapter 7 takes up reflection, critique and self-correction.

Chapter summary

  • Search has three ingredients: a generator, an evaluator and a strategy. Its outcome is bounded by what the evaluator can recognise.
  • Best-of-\(n\) with a perfect verifier achieves pass@\(n\); with false-negative and false-positive rates \(\epsilon_-,\epsilon_+\) it plateaus at the precision \(p(1-\epsilon_-)/(p(1-\epsilon_-)+(1-p)\epsilon_+)\). False positives matter most.
  • Selecting by a learned reward model over-optimises: gold reward rises then falls. Best-of-\(n\) moves the distribution by \(\log n-(n-1)/n\) nats.
  • Tree of Thoughts searches over partial solutions with model proposals and evaluations (Game of 24: 4% → 74%), at roughly a hundred times the calls. Reasoning models internalise much of this search.
  • MCTS selects by UCT, \(\hat Q+c\sqrt{\ln N(s)/N(s,a)}\); LATS applies it to agents with model evaluations, environment feedback and reflections. Searching over actions requires reversibility.
  • Planning: plan-and-execute with replanning trades flexibility for cost and commitment. LLM plans lack validity guarantees (PlanBench); LLM+P and LLM-Modulo let the model formalise and a solver or verifier guarantee.
  • Choose search effort by minimising \(nc+(1-A(n))F\). Search helps when the generator–verifier gap is large, the environment is reversible or modelled, stakes are high and the problem is within reach.

Interview questions

Warm-up What is best-of-n sampling and what limits it?

Sample \(n\) independent candidate solutions and return the one an evaluator scores highest. With a perfect verifier it achieves pass@\(n\), approaching 1 quickly when per-sample success is non-trivial. In practice it is limited by verifier quality: with false-positive rate \(\epsilon_+\) it plateaus at the verifier's precision \(p(1-\epsilon_-)/(p(1-\epsilon_-)+(1-p)\epsilon_+)\). With a learned reward model it can over-optimise, selecting candidates that exploit the reward model. It also costs \(n\) times as much and needs either a verifier or a ranking model.

Warm-up Explain Tree of Thoughts.

Problem solving as search over partial solutions. Each node is a sequence of thoughts; the model proposes several next thoughts; the model (or another evaluator) scores each resulting state for promise; a breadth-first search with a beam, or depth-first with back-tracking, expands the best. It prunes bad starts early, which helps on problems with crisp intermediate evaluations (Game of 24: 4% → 74% for GPT-4) at the cost of tens to hundreds of calls per problem. Reasoning models now perform similar search internally.

Warm-up What is plan-and-execute and when would you use it?

A planner call writes an explicit sequence of steps; an executor (cheaper model or workflow) carries them out; surprises trigger replanning. Use it when tasks have predictable structure, when a plan can be shown to a user or checked before acting, when cost matters (one strong planning call, cheap execution), and when commitment avoids dithering. Avoid it when the environment is highly uncertain and each observation changes what to do next; there a ReAct-style loop is more robust.

Core Derive the accuracy ceiling of best-of-n with an imperfect verifier and discuss its implications.

Candidates are correct with probability \(p\); the verifier accepts correct ones with probability \(1-\epsilon_-\) and incorrect ones with \(\epsilon_+\). For large \(n\), the numbers of accepted correct and incorrect candidates are about \(np(1-\epsilon_-)\) and \(n(1-p)\epsilon_+\); choosing uniformly among accepted gives correctness probability equal to their ratio, the precision. Implications: false positives dominate; accuracy plateaus after a few samples; improving the verifier beats sampling more; and when \(p\) is small even a modest \(\epsilon_+\) gives a low ceiling (e.g. \(p=0.1,\epsilon_+=0.1\): about 0.5). If the verifier outputs scores and we take the arg-max, the analysis depends on score distributions, but the same qualitative conclusion holds.

Core What is reward-model over-optimisation, and how does it affect search?

A learned reward model is accurate near the training distribution but has errors elsewhere. Optimising against it, by RL or by strong selection, moves outputs towards regions where it overestimates quality, so true quality first improves and then degrades while proxy reward keeps rising (Gao et al., 2022). For search, it means more candidates or deeper search against a learned evaluator can make results worse. Mitigations: limit optimisation pressure (moderate \(n\), KL penalties), use ensembles or uncertainty-aware reward models, prefer verifiable checks where available, and validate on held-out gold evaluations.

Core State the UCT rule and explain how MCTS balances exploration and exploitation.

At each node choose \(\arg\max_a\hat Q(s,a)+c\sqrt{\ln N(s)/N(s,a)}\). The first term exploits children with high observed value; the second is an optimism bonus that is large for rarely visited children and decays as they are visited, while growing slowly with the parent's visits so that every child is eventually re-examined. This is UCB1 applied recursively, inheriting logarithmic regret in the bandit setting. The constant \(c\) sets how much evidence is required to abandon a branch; it should scale with the range of values.

Core Can LLMs plan? Summarise the debate.

Critics (Valmeekam, Kambhampati and colleagues) showed with PlanBench that 2023 models produced valid plans for a small fraction of classical problems, about 12% on average for GPT-4 autonomously, collapsing under obfuscation, suggesting pattern retrieval rather than planning; LLM plans carry no validity guarantee. Reasoning models improved results considerably, and LLMs are clearly useful for commonsense, approximate planning in open domains. The productive synthesis is LLM-Modulo: models propose plans or formalise problems (LLM+P translating to PDDL), and verifiers or classical planners check or solve them with guarantees. For agents: use the model for open-ended planning with replanning on feedback, and solvers wherever structure allows.

Core How many attempts should a coding agent make per issue? Set up the decision.

Minimise \(nc+(1-A(n))F\), where \(c\) is the cost per attempt, \(F\) the cost of leaving the issue unresolved, and \(A(n)\) best-of-\(n\) accuracy given the selector. Estimate \(p\) and the selector's error rates from logged runs. With \(c=\$1.15\), \(F=\$50\), \(p=0.3\) and a good selector, the optimum is around eight attempts; with a poor selector, about four. Also consider latency (attempts can run in parallel), and the alternative of spending budget on a better selector, such as writing a reproduction test first.

Deep-dive Derive the KL divergence of the best-of-n distribution and explain why it matters.

Let scores be continuous with CDF \(F_S\) under the base policy. The best-of-\(n\) sample has density \(n F_S(s)^{n-1}\) relative to the base in score space (the max of \(n\) i.i.d. uniforms after the probability-integral transform). KL \(=\mathbb E[\log(nU^{n-1})]\) with \(U\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)\): \(\log n+(n-1)\mathbb E[\log U]=\log n+(n-1)(-1/n)=\log n-(n-1)/n\). It matters because it measures optimisation pressure: best-of-\(n\) moves only logarithmically in \(n\), so it is a weak but robust optimiser compared with RL, and over-optimisation curves can be plotted against KL to compare methods on a common axis.

Deep-dive Why does search over actions require reversibility, and how can agents search in irreversible environments?

Tree search revisits states to try alternative actions; that requires restoring an earlier state. In games and simulators states are copyable; in code sandboxes, file-system snapshots or containers allow it; on live systems (sent messages, payments, production databases) it is impossible. Options: search over plans in the model's reasoning; use read-only tools as a model of the environment and commit writes after verification; dry-run or staging modes for tools; simulate users or environments with models (with the risk of simulator bias); and confirmation from humans before irreversible actions. The design principle is to separate exploration (cheap, reversible) from commitment (once, checked).

Deep-dive What is the generator–verifier gap and how should it guide test-time compute allocation?

It is the difference between how reliably something can check a solution and how reliably the model can produce one. Large gaps (code with tests, maths with answers, formal constraints) make sampling plus verification efficient: accuracy approaches pass@\(n\). Small gaps (open-ended writing, judgement) make verification nearly as hard as generation; model judges have high false-positive rates, so best-of-\(n\) plateaus low, and compute is better spent on deeper reasoning or a stronger generator. Allocation: estimate verifier precision on your task, compute the ceiling, and choose between more samples, better verification and stronger generation accordingly.

Deep-dive Compare explicit tree search (ToT/LATS) with a reasoning model thinking longer.

Explicit search: parallelisable, can use external evaluators and environment feedback at every node, can exceed one context's size, and its structure is inspectable; but it costs many calls, needs a good state evaluator, and the model's evaluations of its own partial states can be poor. Reasoning model: one call, search internalised and trained end-to-end on reward, efficient at using its own judgement; but sequential, limited by context, uses no external feedback mid-chain unless interleaved with tools, and is opaque if reasoning is hidden. Practice: reasoning models for internal deliberation; explicit search when an external verifier is strong and the environment is reversible (code with tests), or for very large spaces.

Design Design a system that uses search to fix bugs reliably in a large codebase.

Sandbox with snapshotting. Phase 1: localisation, generating several hypotheses with a reasoning model and checking each cheaply (search, read, targeted test runs). Phase 2: write a reproduction test that fails before any fix (this is the verifier). Phase 3: generate \(n\) candidate patches in parallel from the snapshot (vary prompts and hypotheses for diversity), run the reproduction test and the existing suite on each, discard failures. Phase 4: if several pass, rank by a judge or by diff size and touched files, or cluster by behaviour on additional generated tests. Set \(n\) by the cost model and stop early once a candidate passes everything. Log all candidates for review. Measure resolved rate, cost per resolved issue, and false-positive rate of the selection (patches that pass but are wrong) via hidden tests or human review.

Design How would you add planning to a support agent that handles multi-leg itinerary changes?

Separate read and write tools. Planning phase: gather information with read tools (booking, availability, fares, hotel terms), then have the model enumerate candidate itineraries as structured plans; compute costs and policy compliance with deterministic tools (a fare engine, a policy checker) rather than the model; optionally hand the combinatorial part to a solver if constraints are complex. Presentation: show the customer the top options with costs. Execution: after confirmation, call write tools in a defined order with verification (read back each change), and a compensation path if a later step fails (cancel earlier changes). Log the plan for audit. Evaluate with simulated customers over many itinerary scenarios.

Design A team wants to use a model judge to pick the best of 16 research reports. What do you tell them?

Measure the judge first: on reports with known quality, estimate how often it prefers a worse report (its false-positive behaviour). Best-of-16 accuracy plateaus at the judge's precision, and with learned judges more candidates can increase the chance of selecting a report that games the judge (length, confident tone, many citations). Improve the judge with rubrics, reference checks (do citations support claims?), and pairwise comparisons; consider fewer candidates; diversify generation; and use checkable sub-criteria (facts verified against sources) as filters before the judge. Report the cost: 16 reports cost 16× and may buy little if the judge's gap is small.

Core Why is pass@k with hidden tests not a fair comparison with a deployed agent's success rate?

pass@k with a perfect selector assumes we can identify a correct attempt among \(k\), which uses information (the hidden tests) unavailable at deployment; it is an upper bound on best-of-\(k\). A deployed agent must choose with its own imperfect verifier, achieving at most the verifier's precision ceiling, and pays \(k\) times the cost. Fair comparisons fix the number of attempts, the selection method and the cost.
