Part III · Acting: Tools and Environments — Chapter 9

Retrieval and Knowledge

An agent knows what its model learned before its training cut-off and what is in its context now. Everything else, this morning's news, the airline's policy manual, the repository it was asked to fix, must be found. Retrieval is the craft of finding, in a large collection, the few pieces that matter for the decision at hand, and it is one of the most heavily used capabilities of every practical agent.

Why this chapter

Chapter 3 listed the model's knowledge cut-off and its lack of private knowledge among the limitations an agent inherits (§3.8). Chapter 4 showed that stuffing the context with everything that might be relevant is expensive and degrades the model's use of it (§4.7). Chapter 8 ended with retrieval as the way to cope with too many tools. All three point to the same need: a mechanism that, given the current question, returns a small set of relevant items from a collection too large to read.

Retrieval is older than language models by decades. Search engines solved it for the web with inverted indexes and ranking functions; recommender systems solved it for products with embeddings; question-answering systems combined the two. What changed with language agents is who issues the queries and who reads the results. The reader is now a model that can synthesise, cite and reason over what it retrieves, and the searcher can be the same model, issuing queries iteratively as its understanding develops. This chapter builds retrieval from the ground up: lexical scoring, dense embeddings, approximate nearest-neighbour search, chunking, hybrid ranking and reranking, then retrieval-augmented generation and the agentic forms in which the model drives the search, and finally the choice between retrieving and simply reading a long context.

Learning objectives
  1. Compute a BM25 score and explain inverse document frequency and length normalisation.
  2. Explain dense retrieval with dual encoders, the contrastive objective that trains them, and how it compares with lexical retrieval.
  3. Describe approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (graph-based, inverted-file, quantised) and their memory and recall trade-offs.
  4. Design a retrieval pipeline with chunking, hybrid search, rank fusion and cross-encoder reranking, and reason about recall at \(K\).
  5. Describe retrieval-augmented generation, its failure modes and how to evaluate it.
  6. Explain agentic and iterative retrieval, and decide between retrieval, long context and graph-based summaries for a task.

We use the context budget of Chapter 4, the cost model of §3.7 and the tool design principles of Chapter 8.

9.1 What retrieval is for

An agent needs external knowledge for three reasons, and each shapes what retrieval must do. Recency: the model's knowledge stops at its training cut-off, so anything newer (a library's latest API, this week's events, today's fares) must be fetched. Privacy: the model never saw the organisation's internal documents, code or customer records, so they must be supplied. Grounding: even for things the model "knows", its recall is unreliable (§3.8), and a claim supported by a retrieved source can be checked and cited, while a recalled one cannot. The research agent needs all three; the support agent needs mostly privacy (the policy manual and booking data) and grounding; the coding agent needs privacy (the repository) and recency (current documentation).

Formally, retrieval is a ranking problem. We have a collection \(\mathcal D\) of documents or chunks \(d\), a query \(q\), and a relevance relation we cannot observe directly. A retriever computes a score \(\operatorname{score}(q,d)\) for each item and returns the top \(K\). Its quality is measured by recall@\(K\), the fraction of relevant items among those returned out of all relevant items, and by precision@\(K\), the fraction of returned items that are relevant. For an agent the two matter in different ways. Recall is critical, because an item that is not retrieved is invisible to the model and no amount of reasoning recovers it. Precision matters because every irrelevant item costs tokens and attention in the context (§4.7). A good pipeline therefore retrieves broadly and then filters, a structure that runs through this chapter. Figure 9.1 shows its standard shape.

Figure 9.1's bottom row names the quantity each stage should optimise, and it is the key to debugging retrieval systems: when an answer is wrong, the first question is which stage failed. Was the relevant chunk never retrieved (a recall failure), retrieved but ranked out by the reranker (a precision failure), or present in the context but misused by the model (a faithfulness failure)? The rest of the chapter goes stage by stage, starting with the oldest and still indispensable form of scoring.

9.2 Lexical retrieval and BM25

The simplest idea of relevance is shared words: a document is relevant to "refund for schedule change" if it contains those words. Two refinements turn the idea into a good ranking function. First, not all words are equally informative. "Change" appears in a large fraction of an airline's documents and says little; "refund" appears in fewer and says more. Weighting each term by its inverse document frequency (IDF), a decreasing function of the fraction of documents containing it, rewards matches on rare, discriminative terms. Second, repeated occurrences should help with diminishing returns, and long documents should not win merely by containing more words. BM25, developed by Robertson and colleagues in the 1990s from a probabilistic model of relevance, combines these in a formula that remains the standard lexical baseline thirty years later[1].

Definition — BM25

For a query with terms \(u_1,\dots,u_n\), a document \(d\) of length \(\lvert d\rvert\) in a collection of \(N_{\mathcal D}\) documents with average length \(\overline{\lvert d\rvert}\), let \(\mathrm{tf}(u,d)\) be the count of term \(u\) in \(d\) and \(\mathrm{df}(u)\) the number of documents containing \(u\). Then \[ \operatorname{BM25}(q,d)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IDF}(u_i)\,\frac{\mathrm{tf}(u_i,d)\,(k_1+1)}{\mathrm{tf}(u_i,d)+k_1\big(1-b_{\text{BM}}+b_{\text{BM}}\,\lvert d\rvert/\overline{\lvert d\rvert}\big)},\qquad \mathrm{IDF}(u)=\ln\!\Big(\frac{N_{\mathcal D}-\mathrm{df}(u)+0.5}{\mathrm{df}(u)+0.5}+1\Big), \] with typical parameters \(k_1\in[1.2,2]\) (saturation of term frequency) and \(b_{\text{BM}}=0.75\) (strength of length normalisation).

Worked example — scoring two policy pages

The airline's help centre has \(N_{\mathcal D}=10{,}000\) pages of average length 400 words. The query is "refund schedule change". Document frequencies: "refund" 500, "schedule" 2,000, "change" 4,000. IDFs: \(\ln(9500.5/500.5+1)=\ln19.98=2.995\); \(\ln(8000.5/2000.5+1)=\ln5.0=1.609\); \(\ln(6000.5/4000.5+1)=\ln2.5=0.916\). Use \(k_1=1.2\), \(b_{\text{BM}}=0.75\).

Page A (200 words; "refund" ×2, "schedule" ×1, "change" ×1). Length factor \(1-0.75+0.75\times0.5=0.625\), so the denominator constant is \(1.2\times0.625=0.75\). Term weights: \(2\times2.2/(2+0.75)=1.600\) and \(2.2/1.75=1.257\). Score: \(2.995\times1.600+1.609\times1.257+0.916\times1.257=4.79+2.02+1.15=7.97\).

Page B (800 words; "schedule" ×3, "change" ×4, no "refund"). Length factor \(0.25+0.75\times2=1.75\), constant 2.1. Weights \(6.6/5.1=1.294\), \(8.8/6.1=1.443\). Score: \(1.609\times1.294+0.916\times1.443=2.08+1.32=3.40\).

Interpretation. A short page mentioning the rare term "refund" beats a long page full of the common terms. That is usually right, and it fails precisely when the relevant page uses a different word ("reimbursement") for the same idea.

Lexical retrieval has two lasting virtues for agents. It is exact on identifiers: error codes, function names, booking classes, product numbers and rare technical terms are matched precisely, which embeddings often blur. And it needs no training and little memory: an inverted index (for each term, the list of documents containing it) makes BM25 fast over billions of documents. Its weakness is the vocabulary mismatch of the worked example: it cannot match "reimbursement" to "refund", "my flight moved" to "schedule change", or a question to an answer phrased differently. Solving that required representing meaning rather than words.

9.3 Dense retrieval

Dense retrieval represents each query and each document as a vector, an embedding, such that relevant pairs have high similarity. An embedding model \(e:\Sigma^*\to\mathbb R^{d_e}\), typically a Transformer encoder with \(d_e\) between 384 and 4,096, maps text to a vector, usually normalised to unit length, and relevance is scored by the cosine similarity \(\operatorname{sim}(q,d)=e(q)^\top e(d)\). Because queries and documents are encoded independently, by two copies of the same network or by two related networks, this architecture is called a dual encoder or bi-encoder, and all document embeddings can be computed once, offline, and indexed.

The embeddings are trained so that geometry matches relevance. Given a query \(q\), a relevant document \(d^+\) and a set of irrelevant documents \(d^-_1,\dots,d^-_m\), the standard contrastive (InfoNCE) loss is

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{con}}=-\log\frac{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(q,d^+)/T_e\big)}{\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(q,d^+)/T_e\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\exp\!\big(\operatorname{sim}(q,d^-_j)/T_e\big)}, \]

a softmax classification of the relevant document among the candidates, with a temperature \(T_e\). In practice the negatives are the other documents in the same training batch ("in-batch negatives"), plus hard negatives: documents that look relevant (high BM25 score, same topic) but are not, which teach the finest distinctions. Karpukhin and colleagues' Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR, 2020), trained this way on question–passage pairs, retrieved a passage containing the answer in its top 20 for 78.4% of Natural Questions queries against 59.1% for BM25[2], and dense retrieval became a standard component of question-answering systems. Modern general-purpose embedding models are trained on hundreds of millions of pairs mined from the web and fine-tuned on curated data, and are compared on benchmarks such as MTEB.

For the mathematician — an embedding is a learned metric

The embedding model maps text to the unit sphere \(S^{d_e-1}\), and the contrastive loss trains it so that the angular distance between a query and its relevant documents is smaller than that to irrelevant ones. Retrieval then becomes a nearest-neighbour problem on the sphere: find the \(K\) document vectors with the largest inner product with \(e(q)\), the maximum inner-product search problem. The geometry is learned, not given; two texts are close if they were close in the training signal, which is why embeddings trained on question–answer pairs place a question near its answer rather than near its paraphrase, and why domain-specific vocabulary unseen in training may embed poorly.

Dual encoders trade accuracy for speed in a precise way. Because the query and document never "see" each other, all the information about their relationship must pass through a single inner product of two fixed vectors. A cross-encoder instead feeds the query and document together into one Transformer and outputs a relevance score, letting every query token attend to every document token. Cross-encoders are markedly more accurate, but they must run once per query–document pair, so they cannot be used to search a large collection, only to rerank a short list. Figure 9.2 contrasts the two. The standard pipeline of Figure 9.1 uses each where it fits: the dual encoder (with BM25) for broad, cheap recall over the whole collection, the cross-encoder for accurate ranking of the hundred or so candidates.

Choosing and adapting an embedding model

For an agent builder, the embedding model is usually chosen, not trained, and the choice has three dimensions. Domain fit: general-purpose models are trained on web text and question–answer pairs; on specialised vocabularies (airline fare codes, internal product names, legal or medical terms) they may place unrelated items close together and related ones far apart. Dimension and cost: larger embeddings are often slightly more accurate but cost proportionally more memory (§9.4) and search time; many modern models support truncating embeddings to a prefix of their dimensions (Matryoshka-style training), trading a little accuracy for a large saving. Asymmetry: queries are short and documents long, and many models expect different prefixes or encoders for each; using a document encoding for queries, or vice versa, quietly degrades recall. Public leaderboards rank models on averages over many tasks, which may not reflect a particular collection, so the decisive test is recall@K on a few hundred labelled queries from the agent's own domain.

When no off-the-shelf model is good enough, a modest amount of fine-tuning usually is. The training pairs can be mined from the agent's own logs (queries and the documents that answered them), generated synthetically by having a language model write questions that each chunk answers, and paired with hard negatives drawn from the current retriever's near misses. A few thousand such pairs, trained with the contrastive loss above, often give large gains in domain recall. The same pairs train the reranker, which is where domain adaptation usually pays most, since the reranker makes the final selection.

Dense and lexical retrieval fail in complementary ways, as Figure 9.2's architecture suggests. Embeddings capture paraphrase and meaning but blur exact identifiers and rare terms (two different error codes may embed almost identically); BM25 is exact on identifiers but blind to paraphrase. This complementarity is the reason almost every production system uses both, a combination we return to in §9.5. First, though, we must make dense retrieval fast.

9.4 Searching vectors fast

Finding the \(K\) nearest of \(N_{\mathcal D}\) vectors exactly means computing all \(N_{\mathcal D}\) inner products: \(2N_{\mathcal D}d_e\) floating-point operations and, more limiting, reading all \(N_{\mathcal D}d_e\) numbers from memory for every query. For a million chunks with 1,024-dimensional float32 embeddings that is 4 GB per query scan, roughly a tenth of a second at typical memory bandwidth, acceptable for one agent but not for many, and impossible at a billion chunks. Production systems therefore use approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes, which return most of the true nearest neighbours while examining a small fraction of the collection. Three families dominate, and an engineer should know what each trades.

Graph-based indexes, above all HNSW (hierarchical navigable small-world graphs; Malkov and Yashunin, 2016)[3], connect each vector to a few dozen near neighbours, in several layers of decreasing density. A search starts at an entry point in the sparsest top layer, moves greedily to whichever neighbour is closest to the query, descends a layer when no neighbour is closer, and repeats down to the dense bottom layer, keeping a small candidate list. Each query visits a number of nodes that grows roughly logarithmically with \(N_{\mathcal D}\). HNSW gives excellent recall at low latency, at the price of memory for the graph and slow insertion. Figure 9.3 sketches it. Inverted-file indexes (IVF) cluster the vectors with \(k\)-means and, at query time, search only the few clusters whose centroids are nearest the query; they use less memory and suit very large collections, with recall controlled by how many clusters are probed. Quantisation compresses the vectors themselves: product quantisation splits each vector into sub-vectors and replaces each by the index of its nearest centroid in a small codebook, shrinking a 4 KB float32 vector to a few dozen bytes, at some cost in accuracy that is usually recovered by re-scoring the top candidates with full vectors. Libraries such as FAISS combine all three[4].

Figure 9.3's greedy descent explains the main practical property of graph indexes: they are fast and accurate when the embedding space is well behaved, and they degrade gracefully, by missing some true neighbours, rather than failing outright. For an agent designer, two numbers matter. The first is index recall, the fraction of the exact top \(K\) that the ANN index returns, typically 0.95–0.99 when tuned; it multiplies with the embedding model's own recall, so a mediocre index can waste a good embedding model. The second is memory, which decides cost. Figure 9.4 computes the raw vector storage for 1,024-dimensional embeddings at three precisions.

Figure 9.4 makes a point that is often missed in discussions of retrieval for agents: most agent collections are small. A repository of a few thousand files, a help centre of ten thousand pages or a company's policy library fits in memory on one machine at full precision, and exact search over it is fast. ANN indexes and quantisation matter for web-scale search, for large enterprise corpora and for many tenants sharing one service. For the coding agent, the more important retrieval question is usually not how to search vectors fast but whether to use vectors at all, since lexical search over code (exact identifiers) and structural tools (go to definition) often beat embeddings there.

9.5 Chunking, hybrid search and reranking

Documents are usually too long to retrieve whole: a 40-page policy contains the answer in one paragraph, and returning all 40 pages wastes the context. Collections are therefore split into chunks, each indexed and retrieved separately. Chunk size is a genuine trade-off. Small chunks (100–300 tokens) are precise, so retrieved text is mostly relevant, but they lose context: a chunk that says "this does not apply to Basic fares" is useless if it does not say what "this" is. Large chunks (1,000+ tokens) keep context but dilute relevance, both for the retriever (the embedding averages over more content) and for the reader (more irrelevant tokens). Common remedies include splitting at natural boundaries (sections, paragraphs, functions in code) rather than at fixed lengths, overlapping adjacent chunks, prefixing each chunk with its document title and section heading, and contextual retrieval, in which a model writes a one-sentence description of where each chunk sits in its document, prepended before embedding and indexing, which Anthropic reported reduced retrieval failures substantially in its experiments[5].

Because lexical and dense retrieval fail differently (§9.3), combining them is one of the most reliable improvements available. Hybrid search runs both and merges their ranked lists. Scores from different retrievers are not comparable, so the usual merge uses only ranks. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF; Cormack et al., 2009) gives each item the score

\[ \operatorname{RRF}(d)=\sum_{\text{retrievers }j}\frac{1}{k_{\text{rrf}}+\operatorname{rank}_j(d)}, \]

with a constant \(k_{\text{rrf}}\) around 60, and sorts by it[6]. An item ranked highly by either retriever scores well; one ranked moderately by both scores better than one ranked moderately by one. RRF is simple, needs no tuning of score scales, and is hard to beat.

How many candidates should the first stage return? Recall at \(K\) rises with \(K\), and it falls with the size of the collection, because more documents means more distractors that happen to score highly. A simple model shows both effects. Suppose the relevant chunk's score is normally distributed with mean \(\zeta\) and unit variance, and each of \(N_{\mathcal D}\) irrelevant chunks has a standard normal score. The relevant chunk is in the top \(K\) if fewer than \(K\) distractors outscore it. Figure 9.5 plots recall@\(K\) computed from this model.

Figure 9.5 explains the shape of the standard pipeline. Top-1 or top-5 retrieval directly from a first-stage retriever is unreliable, even with good embeddings, so the first stage should return many candidates (50–200), where recall is high, and a reranker should choose the few that enter the context. The reranker, usually a cross-encoder (§9.3), has a much better separation on the candidates because it reads query and chunk together. It also shows why growing a collection without improving the retriever quietly hurts agents: the same query against a hundred times more documents finds the right one far less often.

Worked example — a two-stage pipeline for the support agent

The help centre has 30,000 chunks. Hybrid retrieval of the top 100 has recall 0.95 for the chunk that answers a typical question. A cross-encoder reranker, given those 100, puts the answering chunk in its top 5 with probability 0.92 when it is present.

End-to-end recall@5. \(0.95\times0.92=0.874\), against perhaps 0.6 for taking the first stage's top 5 directly.

Cost. The reranker reads 100 pairs of about 600 tokens: 60,000 tokens through a small model, a fraction of a cent and roughly 100–200 ms when batched. The agent's context receives 5 chunks of 500 tokens, 2,500 tokens, instead of 50,000 for twenty unreranked chunks.

Where the remaining 12.6% goes. 5 points are first-stage misses (fix: better chunking, query rewriting, synonyms such as "reimbursement" for "refund"); 7.6 points are reranker misses (fix: fine-tune the reranker on the airline's data, or pass the top 10).

Drill 9.1 — reciprocal rank fusion

BM25 ranks chunks A, B, C at 1, 2, 30; the dense retriever ranks them at 40, 3, 2. With \(k_{\text{rrf}}=60\), what is the fused order?

A: \(1/61+1/100=0.01639+0.01000=0.02639\). B: \(1/62+1/63=0.01613+0.01587=0.03200\). C: \(1/90+1/62=0.01111+0.01613=0.02724\). Order: B, C, A. B, ranked well by both, wins; A, first for BM25 but poor for dense, comes last of the three.

9.6 Retrieval-augmented generation

Retrieval becomes useful to an agent when its results are placed in the model's context and the model generates from them. Lewis and colleagues named this retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) in 2020, training a generator and a dense retriever end to end so that the model conditioned on retrieved Wikipedia passages; it set new results on open-domain question answering and produced more specific, factual text than a model alone[7]. With today's instruction-tuned models, RAG is usually done without joint training: retrieve, put the chunks in the context with instructions to answer from them and cite them, and generate. In the probabilistic language of §3.3, retrieved passages are strong evidence that shifts the model's posterior towards the facts they contain.

RAG fails in four characteristic ways, each corresponding to a stage of Figure 9.1. Retrieval misses: the answer is in the collection but not retrieved, because of vocabulary mismatch, poor chunking or a query that does not express the need. Context noise: relevant chunks are retrieved along with many irrelevant ones, and the model is distracted or confused (§4.7). Ignored or overridden context: the model answers from its parametric knowledge despite the retrieved evidence, especially when the evidence contradicts what it learned in training, or conversely follows a wrong retrieved passage too faithfully. Synthesis errors: the answer requires combining several chunks and the model combines them wrongly. Evaluating a RAG system means measuring each separately: context recall (is the needed information retrieved?), context precision (how much of what is retrieved is relevant?), faithfulness (is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved context?) and answer correctness. Frameworks such as RAGAS automate these with model judges, which must themselves be validated (§7.7).

Several context-engineering practices (Chapter 4) specifically improve how models use retrieved material. Put retrieved chunks in a clearly delimited block, each with an identifier, a title and a date, so that the model can cite them and judge their recency. Order matters because of the position effects of §4.7: placing the most relevant chunks first or last, rather than in the middle of a long list, measurably helps. Instruct the model explicitly about the role of the material ("answer only from the documents below; if they do not contain the answer, say so"), which reduces both ignored context and invented answers. And keep the number of chunks matched to the question: retrieving thirty chunks for a single-fact question adds noise without adding recall once the reranker is good, while a synthesis question may need them all. An answer of "the documents do not say" should be treated as a success when it is true; an agent that always finds something to say is more dangerous than one that admits a retrieval miss and searches again or escalates.

For agents the most important of these properties is faithfulness, because it is what makes answers checkable. An agent that answers "Basic fares are non-refundable, except for schedule changes of more than three hours [policy §4.2]" can be audited: the citation either supports the claim or it does not, and the check can be automated (§7.5). This is the reason grounding with citations, not merely retrieval, is the standard for research and support agents.

9.7 Agentic retrieval

Classic RAG retrieves once, with the user's question as the query. That works when the question names what is needed. It fails for questions whose answer requires information the question does not mention: "Is the airline that operates the flight from my booking a member of the alliance whose lounge I'm allowed to use?" needs the booking (to find the operating airline), then the airline's alliance, then the lounge policy, and no single query retrieves all three. Such multi-hop questions are common for agents, and the remedy is to let the model drive retrieval: decide what to search for, read the results, and decide what to search for next.

A sequence of methods made this explicit. Self-Ask (Press et al., 2022) prompted models to pose and answer follow-up questions before the final answer, with a search engine answering the follow-ups[8]. IRCoT (Trivedi et al., 2022) interleaved retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning: each new sentence of reasoning became the query for the next retrieval, so that what was retrieved followed what the model was thinking about, and multi-hop question answering improved substantially over one-shot retrieval[9]. FLARE (Jiang et al., 2023) retrieved only when the model was about to generate low-confidence tokens, using the tentative sentence as the query[10]. In agents, the same idea is simply ReAct with a search tool: retrieval is an action, the query is an argument the model chooses, and the results are an observation. Figure 9.6 shows the loop.

Figure 9.6 is the core loop of a research agent, and it inherits all the properties of agent loops from Part I. Each hop is a step with its own error rate, so a four-hop question succeeds with probability roughly \(\rho^4\) unless errors are detected; a wrong first hop (the wrong operating airline) poisons every later one. Queries written by the model can be much better than the user's original question, because the model can add synonyms, expand abbreviations and target the specific fact it lacks; rewriting the query before the first retrieval is a cheap and effective step even in non-agentic pipelines. And the loop needs a stopping rule (§2.7): the agent must decide when it has enough evidence to answer, which in practice means asking whether every claim in the planned answer has a supporting source.

The fullest form of agentic retrieval is the deep research agent, introduced in products from late 2024: given an open question, it plans sub-questions, runs dozens to hundreds of searches, reads and extracts from many pages, tracks what it has found and what remains unknown, and writes a cited report. Its engineering problems are exactly those of the previous chapters: the context fills with page contents (§4.7), so pages must be summarised or extracted before they enter it; searches for different sub-questions are independent and can run in parallel (§8.6) or in sub-agents (Chapter 15); and its claims must be checked against its sources (§7.5).

Drill 9.2 — multi-hop reliability

A three-hop question requires three retrievals, each finding the needed fact with probability 0.9 and, when it fails, returning a plausible wrong fact 60% of the time (the rest of the time nothing useful, which the agent notices). If the agent re-queries once after noticing a failure, with the same success probability, what is the probability the final answer is right?

Per hop: succeed first time 0.9; fail noticeably 0.04 then succeed 0.9 → 0.036; fail with a wrong fact 0.06 (undetected). Per hop success \(0.936\); three hops \(0.936^3=0.820\). Undetected wrong facts are the dominant loss; asking the agent to confirm each fact with a second source would target them.

9.8 Retrieval, long context, or both?

As context windows grew to hundreds of thousands of tokens, a natural question arose: why retrieve at all, when the whole document collection, or a large part of it, could be placed in the context? For small, stable corpora the answer is sometimes "don't": a 50-page policy (about 32,000 tokens) can simply be included, cached in the prefix, and the model will find what it needs without a retrieval pipeline that might miss it. For anything larger, three of the book's recurring considerations favour retrieval.

Cost and latency: every context token is paid at every call (§4.1). Quality: long contexts degrade the model's use of their contents (§4.7), so a focused context of the five relevant chunks often beats a full context containing them among thousands of others. Scale and change: most collections do not fit in any window, and they change; an index can be updated incrementally, while a cached long prefix is invalidated by any edit. In practice the two combine: retrieve generously (tens of chunks rather than three) into a large context, so that recall is high and the model, not the reranker, makes the final judgement of relevance.

Worked example — the policy manual in context or retrieved?

The airline's full policy library is 200 pages, about 130,000 tokens. A support conversation has 12 turns.

In context, cached across conversations. Each call reads 130,000 cached tokens: \(0.13\times\$0.30=\$0.039\) per turn, $0.47 per conversation, plus the occasional cache write (\(0.13\times\$3\times1.25\approx\$0.49\)) whenever the cache expires. Latency per turn is fine once cached, but a cold start prefills 130,000 tokens, about 26 s at our reference speed.

Retrieved. Each turn retrieves 5 chunks of 500 tokens (2,500 fresh tokens): \(0.0025\times\$3=\$0.0075\) per turn, $0.09 per conversation, plus negligible search and reranking cost.

Quality. Long context: no retrieval misses, but the relevant paragraph is buried among 200 pages of text in the context. Retrieval: about 12% of turns miss the needed chunk (the 0.874 recall of §9.5), but the model's context is focused.

Verdict. Retrieval is five times cheaper and avoids cold-start latency; long context avoids misses. A hybrid, keeping a 10-page summary of the most-used policies cached in the prefix and retrieving the rest, gets most of both. Measure end-to-end accuracy on real conversations to decide.

One class of question defeats chunk retrieval however well it is tuned: global questions about a whole collection, such as "What are the main themes of complaints this quarter?" or "Which policies changed most since last year?" No chunk contains the answer; it emerges from all of them. GraphRAG (Edge et al., Microsoft, 2024) addresses this by having a model extract entities and relations from every chunk into a knowledge graph, cluster the graph into communities, and write a summary of each community in advance; global questions are then answered by combining the community summaries[11]. The idea generalises: for questions about the whole, precompute summaries at several levels (documents, topics, the collection) and retrieve over those. The cost is moved from query time to indexing time, where a model must read the entire collection, which is worthwhile for collections that are queried often and change slowly.

9.9 Freshness, trust and conflict

Retrieval brings the outside world into the agent's context, and the outside world is neither consistent nor uniformly reliable. Three problems recur. Staleness: an index built last month returns last month's fares; documents must carry dates, indexes must be refreshed on a schedule that matches how fast the content changes, and time-sensitive facts are better fetched live from an API than retrieved from an index. Source quality: the web contains errors, outdated pages and content optimised to be found rather than to be true; a research agent should weigh sources by authority and corroboration, prefer primary sources, and say when evidence is thin. Conflict: retrieved passages may contradict each other or the model's own knowledge. Models handle conflict inconsistently, sometimes siding with the retrieved text, sometimes with their training, and sometimes with whichever passage appears last. Good practice is to surface conflicts explicitly in the context ("these two sources disagree on the change fee"), instruct the agent to prefer the authoritative or most recent source by a stated rule, and report the disagreement rather than silently choosing.

Retrieved content is also untrusted input in the security sense of §8.9. A page, a document or a support article can contain instructions aimed at the agent, and retrieval is the channel through which they arrive. Retrieval systems for agents should therefore index only trusted sources where possible, mark provenance in what they return, and never let retrieved text expand the agent's permissions. Chapter 20 returns to this.

9.10 Evaluating retrieval

A retrieval-augmented agent can fail because the retriever did not find the right passage or because the model did not use it, and the two failures need different fixes. Evaluating the retriever on its own, with metrics computed from ranked lists against relevance judgements, separates them. The data needed is a set of queries, each with the documents (or chunks) judged relevant, which can come from people, from logged user behaviour, or from a strong model judging candidates and checked on a sample.

Three metrics cover most needs. Recall@\(K\) is the fraction of the relevant documents that appear in the top \(K\); for retrieval-augmented generation it is the most important, because a passage outside the context cannot be used however good the model is. Mean reciprocal rank (MRR) averages \(1/\text{rank}\) of the first relevant result over queries; it suits tasks where one good passage suffices. Normalised discounted cumulative gain (nDCG; Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002) rewards placing relevant results near the top, with graded relevance \(g_j\) at rank \(j\)[12]:

\[ \mathrm{DCG}@K=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{g_j}{\log_2(j+1)},\qquad \mathrm{nDCG}@K=\frac{\mathrm{DCG}@K}{\mathrm{IDCG}@K}, \]

where IDCG is the DCG of the ideal ordering, so nDCG lies in \([0,1]\). It matters when rank position matters, as it does for agents that read only the first few results, or when context budgets force a small \(K\).

Worked example — one query, three metrics

A query has three relevant chunks. The retriever's top 5 contains two of them, at ranks 2 and 5 (binary relevance).

Recall@5. \(2/3=0.667\). Reciprocal rank. \(1/2=0.5\).

nDCG@5. DCG \(=1/\log_23+1/\log_26=0.631+0.387=1.018\). The ideal list places the three relevant chunks at ranks 1–3: IDCG \(=1+0.631+0.5=2.131\). nDCG \(=0.478\).

Reading them together. The context would contain two-thirds of the evidence (recall), but an agent that reads only the top three results sees just one relevant chunk; nDCG's low value flags that a reranker (§9.5) would help.

Retrieval metrics are necessary but not sufficient. The end-to-end question is whether answers are correct and supported, which needs the evaluation methods of Chapter 19: answer accuracy, citation faithfulness and judge-based grading. A useful diagnostic is to evaluate the generator with the gold passages supplied directly (an "oracle retriever"): the gap between oracle and real retrieval is the retriever's share of the errors, and the gap between oracle and perfect is the generator's. For agentic retrieval (§9.7), where the agent issues many queries, the unit of evaluation becomes the episode: did the agent eventually retrieve what it needed, and at what cost in queries and tokens?

Looking ahead

Retrieval gives an agent knowledge; computation tools give it exact arithmetic. One kind of tool subsumes both and much more: a code interpreter. An agent that can write and run code can query databases, transform data, call any library, test hypotheses about a program's behaviour and build new tools for itself. Code is also how the coding agent, our first running example, does its whole job. What does it mean for code to be the action space, how are code-executing agents kept safe in sandboxes, how do coding agents navigate and change large repositories, and how are they evaluated? Chapter 10 takes up code as action.

Chapter summary

  • Agents retrieve for recency, privacy and grounding. Retrieval is ranking, measured by recall@\(K\) (critical: unretrieved items are invisible) and precision@\(K\) (irrelevant items cost context).
  • BM25 scores term overlap with IDF weighting, saturating term frequency and length normalisation; exact on identifiers, blind to paraphrase.
  • Dense retrieval: dual encoders map text to the unit sphere, trained with a contrastive loss with in-batch and hard negatives (DPR: top-20 78.4% vs BM25 59.1% on NQ). Cross-encoders score pairs jointly, more accurately, for reranking.
  • ANN indexes (HNSW graphs, IVF clusters, product quantisation) trade recall for speed and memory; most agent collections are small enough for exact search.
  • Chunk at natural boundaries with context; combine lexical and dense results by reciprocal rank fusion; retrieve broadly (recall falls sharply at small \(K\) and large collections) and rerank to a few.
  • RAG fails by retrieval misses, context noise, ignored context and synthesis errors; evaluate context recall, context precision, faithfulness and correctness; cite to make answers checkable.
  • Agentic retrieval (Self-Ask, IRCoT, FLARE, ReAct with search) lets the model write queries and hop; deep research agents scale it with parallel searches, extraction and verification.
  • Long context suits small, stable corpora; retrieval suits large or changing ones and is cheaper; hybrids are common. GraphRAG precomputes summaries for global questions.
  • Manage staleness, source quality and conflicts explicitly; retrieved text is untrusted input.
  • Evaluate retrieval separately with recall@K, MRR and nDCG, and use an oracle-retriever comparison to split errors between retriever and generator.

Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then reveal the model answer and rate yourself.

Warm-up Explain RAG and why it is used.

Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves documents relevant to a query from an external collection and places them in the model's context so the model answers from them, ideally with citations. It supplies knowledge the model lacks (recent or private), reduces hallucination by grounding claims in sources, and makes answers checkable. It adds a retrieval pipeline whose recall and precision limit answer quality, and it introduces new failure modes (misses, noise, ignored context).

Warm-up Compare BM25 and dense retrieval.

BM25 scores term overlap with IDF weighting, term-frequency saturation and length normalisation; it needs no training, is exact on identifiers and rare terms, is cheap with inverted indexes, but misses paraphrases and synonyms. Dense retrieval embeds queries and documents with a trained dual encoder and ranks by cosine similarity; it captures meaning and paraphrase, but can blur exact identifiers, depends on training data and domain, and needs vector indexes. They fail differently, so hybrid search with rank fusion usually beats either.

Warm-up What is a reranker and why use one?

A model, usually a cross-encoder, that reads the query and each candidate together and outputs a relevance score, used to reorder the top 50–200 candidates from a fast first-stage retriever and keep the best few. It is far more accurate than dual-encoder similarity because tokens of query and document attend to each other, but too slow to run over a whole collection. The two-stage design gives high recall cheaply in stage one and high precision in stage two.

Core Derive and explain the contrastive loss used to train embedding models.

For a query \(q\) with positive \(d^+\) and negatives \(d^-_j\), \(\mathcal L=-\log\frac{e^{s^+/T}}{e^{s^+/T}+\sum_j e^{s^-_j/T}}\) with \(s=\operatorname{sim}(q,d)\): a softmax cross-entropy treating retrieval as classification of the positive among candidates. Minimising it raises \(s^+\) relative to negatives. In-batch negatives make it efficient (every other example's positive is a negative); hard negatives (lexically or topically similar but irrelevant) sharpen fine distinctions; the temperature controls how much hard examples dominate. It approximates a lower bound on mutual information between queries and relevant documents. Failure modes: false negatives in batches, and domain shift when deployed on unseen vocabulary.

Core How would you choose chunk size for a RAG system?

Balance precision (small chunks are mostly relevant) against context (large chunks keep the information needed to interpret them) and the reader's budget. Start by splitting at natural boundaries (sections, paragraphs, functions), add document titles and headings to each chunk, overlap neighbours, and consider contextual descriptions. Then measure: build a labelled set of questions with known answer locations, and compare recall@K and end-to-end answer accuracy across chunking schemes. Often 300–800 tokens with structure-aware splitting works well, but the data decide.

Core Your RAG system gives wrong answers. How do you debug it?

Locate the failing stage per example. Is the needed chunk in the index at all (ingestion, parsing, staleness)? In the first-stage top 100 (recall: query rewriting, hybrid search, synonyms, chunking)? In the reranked top K (reranker quality, K too small)? In the context but misused (faithfulness: instructions to answer from context, conflicting sources, too much noise, position)? Build a labelled set, log retrieved chunks and ranks for every query, and measure context recall, context precision and faithfulness separately before changing anything.

Core Explain HNSW and its trade-offs.

A multi-layer proximity graph: each vector links to a few nearest neighbours; upper layers are sparse samples with long-range links. Search starts at the top, greedily moves to neighbours closer to the query, descends when stuck, and keeps a candidate list at the bottom layer. Query cost grows roughly logarithmically with collection size and recall is high when tuned (efSearch, M). Trade-offs: substantial memory for the graph, slower inserts and difficult deletes, and recall that degrades with poorly distributed embeddings; IVF and quantisation trade differently (less memory, lower recall without re-scoring).

Core Why does recall at small K fall as the collection grows, and what do you do about it?

More documents means more distractors, and the chance that some distractor scores above the relevant document grows with their number; in a Gaussian score model with separation ζ, recall@K at 100,000 chunks can be a fraction of recall at 1,000. Remedies: retrieve more candidates and rerank; improve separation (better or fine-tuned embeddings, hybrid search, query rewriting); filter by metadata (date, product, language) to shrink the effective collection; and partition indexes by domain.

Core When would you put documents in a long context instead of retrieving?

When the corpus is small enough to fit comfortably (tens of thousands of tokens), stable (so the cached prefix stays valid) and queried often (so caching amortises), and when retrieval misses would be costly. Retrieval wins for large or changing corpora, when per-call cost matters, and when focused contexts give better answers than long ones. Hybrids are common: a cached summary of core material plus retrieval for the rest, or generous retrieval (dozens of chunks) into a large context.

Deep-dive Model recall@K for a retriever with Gaussian score separation and discuss what it implies.

Let the relevant document's score be \(S\sim\mathcal N(\zeta,1)\) and each of \(N\) distractors \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), independent. Given \(S=s\), the number of distractors above \(s\) is Binomial\((N,1-\Phi(s))\), approximately Poisson with mean \(N(1-\Phi(s))\). Recall@K \(=\mathbb E_S[\Pr(\text{Bin}\le K-1)]\). Since \(1-\Phi(s)\approx\phi(s)/s\) decays like \(e^{-s^2/2}\), keeping the expected number of better distractors fixed as \(N\) grows by a factor \(m\) requires \(\zeta\) to grow by about \(\sqrt{2\ln m}\) in the tail; this is why recall degrades with scale and why rerankers (which increase separation on a small candidate set) and metadata filters (which reduce \(N\)) are effective. Real score distributions are not Gaussian and relevance is not binary, but the qualitative dependence holds.

Deep-dive How do models handle conflicts between retrieved context and their parametric knowledge, and how should agents be designed around this?

Inconsistently: models sometimes follow the retrieved text (even when wrong), sometimes revert to training knowledge (even when the context is right and newer), with behaviour depending on how plausible the context is, how strongly the model "knows" the fact, phrasing and position. Design: instruct the agent to prefer retrieved authoritative sources for facts about the organisation and recent events; mark provenance and dates; surface conflicts between sources explicitly; for critical facts, use deterministic APIs rather than retrieval; evaluate with tests where the context contradicts common knowledge (counterfactual evaluation) to measure adherence.

Deep-dive Compare one-shot RAG with agentic, iterative retrieval in reliability and cost.

One-shot RAG: one retrieval, low latency and cost, but fails on multi-hop questions and when the user's wording does not match the documents. Agentic retrieval: the model writes queries, reads, and re-queries, handling multi-hop and vocabulary mismatch, but each hop is a step with error rate (success roughly \(\rho^{\text{hops}}\) without detection), adds latency and tokens, and needs a stopping rule. Mitigations: parallel searches for independent sub-questions, extraction of relevant passages to keep the context small, verification of each critical fact against a second source, and budget caps. Choose by the distribution of questions: route simple look-ups to one-shot RAG and complex ones to the agent.

Design Design retrieval for the airline support agent.

Sources: policy manual, help centre, fare rules (structured), notices of disruptions (time-sensitive). Structured, time-sensitive data via APIs, not retrieval. Unstructured documents chunked at section boundaries with titles and effective dates, indexed with BM25 and a domain-tuned embedding model; hybrid retrieval with RRF of top 100; cross-encoder reranking to top 5–8; metadata filters by fare type and region; a cached summary of the most-used policies in the system prompt. The agent calls a search_policy tool (can re-query) and must cite sections. Evaluation: labelled questions with gold sections (recall@K, faithfulness), conversation-level accuracy, and freshness checks when policies change (re-index on publish).

Design Design the retrieval components of a deep research agent.

Planner decomposes the question into sub-questions; for each, the agent issues several web searches in parallel (query rewriting, varied phrasings), fetches top results, and a cheap extraction step returns relevant passages with URLs and quotes, keeping raw pages out of the main context. Findings go into a structured notes store (claim, evidence, source, date, confidence). The agent identifies gaps and contradictions and searches again; stops when each planned claim has adequate support or the budget is reached. Before writing, a verification pass checks quotes against pages and seeks a second source for key claims. Source quality heuristics prefer primary sources. Evaluation: citation accuracy, coverage against expert reference answers, cost and time per report.

Design How should a coding agent find relevant code in a large repository?

Mostly not with embeddings alone. Use exact search (grep/ripgrep) for identifiers and error messages, structural tools (go-to-definition, find-references via a language server), file and symbol maps (a repository map listing files and top-level symbols), and the test failure trace as a starting point. Embedding search over code chunks helps for natural-language queries ("where do we validate email addresses?"). Combine: a repository map in context, tools for exact and structural search, and optional semantic search; let the agent explore just in time rather than preloading files.

Core What is reciprocal rank fusion and why is it popular?

It merges ranked lists from several retrievers by scoring each item \(\sum_j1/(k+\text{rank}_j)\) with \(k\approx60\). It uses only ranks, so it needs no calibration of incomparable scores, is robust and parameter-light, rewards items ranked well by any retriever and especially by several, and in practice is hard to beat with learned fusion unless there is ample labelled data.
