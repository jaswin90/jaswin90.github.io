Part VI · Learning to Act — Chapter 17

Learning from Demonstrations and Preferences

Everything so far has improved agents without changing the model: better contexts, tools, memory and architectures. The most powerful lever is to change the model itself, so that good agent behaviour becomes its default. This chapter covers the first two ways of doing so: imitating demonstrations of good trajectories, and learning from preferences between better and worse ones, with the mathematics of behaviour cloning, reward models and direct preference optimisation.

Why this chapter

Prompting has limits that the earlier chapters ran into repeatedly. A behaviour that must be described anew in every context costs tokens on every call (Chapter 4). A behaviour far from the model's prior is hard to elicit by examples (§3.3). A frontier model that does a routine task well may cost ten times what a small model would, if only the small model could be taught (§3.7). And some behaviours, such as reliably checking one's work before declaring success, recovering gracefully from a failed tool call, or calling tools in the right format for a new harness, are learned far more robustly from training data than from instructions. Training turns what an agent can be told into what it does without being told.

Part VI covers the two families of training. This chapter treats learning from supervision: demonstrations of good behaviour (behaviour cloning) and comparisons between alternatives (preference learning). Chapter 18 treats learning from outcomes: reinforcement learning in environments that reward success. The two chapters share a theme. The shift from single responses to multi-step agents changes what can go wrong in training as much as in deployment: imitation of trajectories suffers from compounding error in a precise, quantifiable way; preference learning must decide whether to compare whole trajectories or individual steps; and both depend on the quality of data that is expensive to produce. We derive the key results, including the quadratic error growth of behaviour cloning and the direct preference optimisation loss, and finish with the practical question of when training is worth it.

17.1 What training can change

A model is trained in stages (Figure 3.1): pre-training gives knowledge and in-context learning, post-training gives instruction following and preferences. An agent developer who fine-tunes a model is adding a further post-training stage aimed at a particular kind of behaviour. Four goals recur. Format and protocol: making a model reliably emit tool calls in a harness's format, use a particular set of tools well, or follow a house procedure, without long instructions. Competence on a domain: making a model better at the tasks of one environment (one company's support policies, one code base's conventions) than prompting can. Robust behaviours: instilling habits that prompts elicit unreliably, such as verifying before submitting, asking for clarification when a request is ambiguous, or recovering from errors. Cost: teaching a small model to do what a large one does, so that the same behaviour is served at a fraction of the price, which is distillation.

Figure 17.1 shows the training pipeline that the rest of the chapter dissects. Demonstrations, from people or from a stronger model, train the policy by supervised fine-tuning. Comparisons between outputs train a reward model, which reinforcement learning then optimises against, or are used directly by preference optimisation without an explicit reward model. Rejection sampling closes a loop: the policy generates attempts, a verifier keeps the good ones, and they become new demonstrations.

Figure 17.1's left column is the expensive part. Demonstrations and comparisons must be produced, and for agents each data point is a whole trajectory, often thousands of tokens of reasoning, actions and observations in a particular environment. Much of the craft of agent training is in producing such data cheaply and well, by distilling from stronger models, by generating and filtering, and by deciding what to compare. We begin with the simplest method, imitation.

17.2 Behaviour cloning on trajectories

Behaviour cloning treats agent training as supervised learning: collect trajectories of an expert (a person or a strong model) performing tasks, and train the model to predict the expert's actions given the history. In the token-level view of §2.4, a trajectory is one long sequence in which some spans were produced by the agent (reasoning and tool calls) and others by the environment (observations, user messages). The supervised fine-tuning loss is the cross-entropy of the agent's tokens only:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)=-\,\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\mathcal D_{\text{demo}}}\Big[\sum_{i}m_i\log p_\theta(x_i\mid x_{<i})\Big],\qquad m_i=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x_i\text{ was written by the agent}\\0&\text{if }x_i\text{ came from the environment.}\end{cases} \]

The mask matters. The model should learn to produce good actions given observations, not to predict observations, which would teach it to hallucinate tool outputs, exactly the failure §3.8 warned about. With the mask, behaviour cloning on agent trajectories is effective and data-efficient. FireAct (Chen et al., 2023) fine-tuned a 7-billion-parameter Llama 2 model on 500 question-answering trajectories in the ReAct format generated by GPT-4, and raised its accuracy on HotpotQA by 77% relative to the prompted base model[1]. AgentTuning (Zeng et al., 2023) assembled verified trajectories across six agent environments and found that a fine-tuned 70-billion-parameter open model approached GPT-3.5 on held-out agent tasks while keeping its general abilities, provided agent data was mixed with general instruction data[2].

Behaviour cloning has a fundamental weakness for multi-step tasks, identified in robotics long before language agents. The model is trained on states the expert visits, but at deployment it visits states produced by its own actions. After one mistake it is in a state the expert never reached, where its training data says nothing about how to recover, so it is more likely to err again. Errors compound, and the expected number of mistakes grows faster than the horizon. Ross and Bagnell made this precise[3].

Theorem — compounding error in behaviour cloning (Ross and Bagnell, 2010)

Let a learned policy \(\hat\pi\) disagree with the expert \(\pi^*\) with probability at most \(\epsilon\) per step on the expert's state distribution, and let each disagreement cost at most 1. Over a horizon of \(H\) steps, the expected cost of \(\hat\pi\) exceeds that of \(\pi^*\) by at most \(\epsilon H^2\), and this bound is tight for some environments. A policy trained with DAgger, which queries the expert on states visited by the learner, has excess cost \(O(\epsilon H)\).

Intuition for the \(H^2\). At step \(t\), the learner has made no mistake so far with probability at least \(1-\epsilon t\); once it has made one, it may be in unfamiliar states and err at every remaining step. The expected cost is therefore up to \(\sum_{t=1}^{H}\epsilon t\cdot\) (remaining steps), of order \(\epsilon H^2\). Training on the learner's own states removes the distribution mismatch, so mistakes stay at rate \(\epsilon\) per step.

Figure 17.2 shows what goes wrong. The expert's trajectories pass through a narrow band of states, and that band is all the training data covers. The learner follows the band until its first deviation, and from then on it is in states, such as a half-applied edit, a misread error message, or a booking changed to the wrong date, that no demonstration contains. The statistician will recognise this as covariate shift: the conditional distribution we learned, actions given states, is evaluated under a distribution of states different from the one it was fitted on, and the shift is caused by the learner itself. The difference from ordinary covariate shift is that the shift grows with the length of the episode, because every deviation moves the learner further from the band and makes the next deviation more likely.

The intuition in the theorem can be made exact. Suppose, pessimistically, that a learner which has left the expert's band fails at every remaining step. The probability that the first mistake happens at step \(t\) is \(\epsilon(1-\epsilon)^{t-1}\), and it then costs \(H-t+1\) steps, so the expected excess cost is \(\sum_{t=1}^{H}\epsilon(1-\epsilon)^{t-1}(H-t+1)\). For small \(\epsilon H\) this is approximately \(\epsilon\sum_t (H-t+1)=\epsilon H(H+1)/2\), quadratic in the horizon; as \(\epsilon H\) grows it approaches \(H\), the trivial ceiling. The constant \(1/2\) is lost in the theorem's statement, but the order is the point: halving the per-step error rate halves the excess cost, while doubling the horizon quadruples it. Figure 17.3 plots the two bounds.

Figure 17.3 is plotted on a logarithmic axis because the gap is a factor of \(H\), which reaches 100 at a hundred steps. It explains a pattern seen repeatedly in agent fine-tuning: a model cloned from demonstrations does well on short tasks, nearly matching its teacher, but its advantage fades on long ones, where a teacher that would have recovered from a mistake is being imitated by a student that never saw a mistake to recover from.

Worked example — how often does a cloned coding agent leave the band?

A small model is cloned from a frontier model's trajectories on coding tasks of \(H=30\) steps. On held-out states from the teacher's trajectories, the student picks a different action from the teacher's in \(\epsilon=2\%\) of steps.

Probability of a clean run. \((1-\epsilon)^H=0.98^{30}=0.546\). In 45% of episodes the student leaves the teacher's distribution at least once.

Pessimistic excess cost. If every step after the first deviation is wasted, the expected number of wasted steps is \(\sum_{t=1}^{30}0.02\cdot0.98^{t-1}(31-t)=7.7\) steps per episode, a quarter of the horizon. The cruder bound \(\epsilon H^2=18\) overstates it, as the exact sum shows, but both are an order of magnitude above DAgger's \(\epsilon H=0.6\).

With \(\epsilon=1\%\). A clean run has probability \(0.99^{30}=0.740\) and the expected excess falls to 4.2 steps: halving the error rate roughly halves the damage, but it does not change the quadratic shape.

Consequence. Not every deviation is a mistake (two good solutions often differ), so 7.7 wasted steps is an upper bound on the harm. The useful conclusion is where to invest: in the states the student reaches after deviating, not in more demonstrations of clean runs.

DAgger (dataset aggregation; Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011) is the remedy that the theorem points to[4]. Train a first policy by behaviour cloning. Then repeat: run the current policy (in early rounds mixed with the expert, so that runs do not wander too far), record the states it visits, ask the expert what it would do in each of those states, add the labelled states to the dataset, and retrain on everything collected so far. Because the training distribution now includes the learner's own states, including the unfamiliar ones after mistakes, the learner is taught to recover, and Ross and colleagues proved the excess cost becomes linear in \(H\). DAgger is an instance of a general principle that will recur in Chapter 18: on-policy data, collected by the policy being trained, fixes the mismatch that off-policy data creates.

For language agents the expert in DAgger is usually a stronger model, which makes the scheme cheap to run. The student's trajectory prefix, up to some step, is given to the teacher, which writes the action it would take next; that action becomes a training label for that prefix. Two practical cautions apply. A teacher continuing someone else's trajectory may behave differently from a teacher running its own (it may, for instance, start over rather than repair the student's half-finished edit), so its labels should be checked by running them, not trusted blindly. And the labels must be produced in the student's context format, including the student's tools and harness, or the student learns to imitate actions it cannot take. A cheaper relative of DAgger, sometimes enough in practice, is to inject deliberate errors into teacher runs (a failed tool call, a misleading search result) and keep the teacher's recovery as demonstration data, which buys some coverage of off-band states without querying the teacher on the student's runs.

Drill 17.1 — the horizon budget

A team wants the pessimistic excess cost \(\epsilon H(H+1)/2\) of a cloned agent to stay below one step. What per-step disagreement rate does this require at \(H=10\), \(H=30\) and \(H=100\)?

\(\epsilon\le 2/(H(H+1))\): \(2/110=1.8\%\) at \(H=10\); \(2/930=0.22\%\) at \(H=30\); \(2/10{,}100=0.02\%\) at \(H=100\). Each tripling of the horizon demands roughly a ninefold lower error rate. With DAgger's linear bound the requirement is \(\epsilon\le1/H\): 10%, 3.3% and 1%, which are achievable. This is the quantitative reason long-horizon agents are rarely trained by pure imitation.

DAgger needs an expert to query. When the expert is a frontier model, every label costs a frontier call, and for some tasks no model is a reliable expert at all. A different source of demonstrations needs no expert: the policy's own attempts, filtered by whether they succeeded. That is the subject of the next section.

17.3 Rejection sampling and self-improvement

Many agent tasks come with a verifier (Chapter 6): the tests pass or fail, the booking matches the request, the answer equals the reference. When a verifier exists, demonstrations can be manufactured. Sample \(k\) attempts at each task from the current policy, keep the ones the verifier accepts, and fine-tune on them. This is rejection-sampling fine-tuning, and when it is repeated, so that the improved policy produces the next round's data, it is self-improvement: the model is taught by its own best work. Figure 17.4 shows the loop.

The idea appeared in several forms between 2022 and 2024. STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner; Zelikman, Wu, Mu and Goodman, 2022) applied it to reasoning: the model writes a rationale and an answer for each question, rationales that reach the correct answer are kept for fine-tuning, and the loop is iterated[5]. STaR added a trick for hard questions, rationalisation: for a question the model got wrong, it is shown the correct answer as a hint and asked to produce a rationale for it, which is then used as if it had been generated unaided. ReST (Reinforced Self-Training; Gulcehre and colleagues at DeepMind, 2023) framed the same loop as offline reinforcement learning, alternating a Grow step that samples a dataset from the policy and an Improve step that filters it by a reward threshold and fine-tunes, with the threshold raised across iterations[6]. For agents, the same recipe was used to train software-engineering models on trajectories that pass a repository's tests, and to build tool-use data by keeping the attempts whose final answers check out.

Why does filtering one's own samples help? The answer connects rejection sampling to the reinforcement learning of Chapter 18. Let \(R(\tau)\in\{0,1\}\) be the verifier's verdict. The filtered SFT objective, on data sampled from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_0}\), is

\[ J_{\text{RFT}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{x}\,\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta_0}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[R(\tau)\log\pi_\theta(\tau\mid x)\big],\qquad \nabla_\theta J_{\text{RFT}}\big|_{\theta=\theta_0}=\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]\big|_{\theta=\theta_0}. \]

The second identity is the score-function (log-derivative) trick, \(\nabla\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}[R]=\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}[R\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta]\), which Chapter 18 develops as the policy gradient. At the current parameters, the gradient of the filtered likelihood is exactly the gradient of the expected success rate. Rejection-sampling fine-tuning is therefore an approximate policy-improvement step: it moves the policy in the direction that increases reward, then re-samples. It differs from on-line reinforcement learning in two ways: the data is sampled once per round rather than continually, and failed attempts are simply discarded rather than pushed down. Both make it more stable and simpler to run, at the cost of using less of the information in each sample.

The same view explains the method's biases. The amount of data a task contributes is proportional to how often the policy already solves it. With success probability \(p\) and \(k\) attempts, a task contributes \(kp\) successes on average and contributes nothing with probability \((1-p)^k\). Easy tasks dominate the dataset, hard tasks are under-represented, and tasks the policy cannot yet solve contribute nothing at all, so the loop sharpens what the model already does rather than teaching it new skills. There is also a subtler bias. The verifier accepts some trajectories for the wrong reasons: a test suite that misses a bug, a reference answer reached by a lucky guess, a booking that matches by coincidence. Those trajectories are trained on as if they were exemplary, and if a policy can exploit a gap in the verifier, self-improvement will find and amplify it, which is the first appearance of the reward hacking that Chapter 18 treats in detail.

Worked example — what rejection sampling actually trains on

The support agent has 1,000 training tasks: 400 easy (current success probability \(p=0.8\)), 400 medium (\(p=0.3\)) and 200 hard (\(p=0.02\)). We draw \(k=8\) attempts per task.

All successes kept. Expected successes: \(400\cdot8\cdot0.8=2{,}560\) easy, \(400\cdot8\cdot0.3=960\) medium, \(200\cdot8\cdot0.02=32\) hard, so the dataset is 72% easy, 27% medium and 0.9% hard, although hard tasks are 20% of the task set.

Coverage. The probability that a task yields at least one success is \(1-(1-p)^8\): essentially 1 for easy tasks, \(1-0.7^8=0.942\) for medium tasks and \(1-0.98^8=0.149\) for hard ones. About 170 of the 200 hard tasks contribute nothing.

One success per task. Keeping at most one trajectory per task gives \(400+377+30=807\) examples: 50% easy, 47% medium, 3.7% hard. Capping rebalances the mix but cannot create data for unsolved tasks.

Raising \(k\) for hard tasks. With \(k=64\) on hard tasks only, coverage rises to \(1-0.98^{64}=0.726\), about 145 tasks, for an extra \(200\cdot56=11{,}200\) attempts. Spending the sampling budget where the success rate is low is the cheapest way to extend the frontier, and it is what adaptive self-improvement schemes do.

The remedies follow from the arithmetic. Cap the number of trajectories kept per task, so that easy tasks do not crowd out the rest. Allocate more attempts to tasks with low success rates. Use hints or partial solutions, in the spirit of STaR's rationalisation, to obtain successes on tasks the policy cannot yet solve, while checking that the resulting trajectories do not refer to the hint. Strengthen the verifier, or add a second check (a judge model, a human spot-check) on accepted trajectories, so that lucky successes are filtered out. And mix in data from outside the policy, a teacher's demonstrations or human ones, for the hard tail, which brings us back to distillation (§17.8). Rejection sampling remains the workhorse of agent data generation because it is cheap, stable and needs nothing but a verifier.

Drill 17.2 — the cost of a round

A coding-agent round samples \(k=8\) attempts on 2,000 tasks. An attempt uses 150,000 input tokens (mostly cached at \(\kappa_{\text{cache}}=\$0.30\)/M, say 80% cached) and 6,000 output tokens on the small model (\(\$0.25\)/M input, \(\$1.25\)/M output, assume cache reads at a tenth of input price). What does the round cost, and what if the frontier model generated the attempts?

Small model, per attempt: uncached input \(30{,}000\times0.25/10^6=\$0.0075\); cached \(120{,}000\times0.025/10^6=\$0.003\); output \(6{,}000\times1.25/10^6=\$0.0075\); total $0.018. For 16,000 attempts, $288, plus sandbox time. Frontier model: \(30{,}000\times3/10^6=\$0.09\), \(120{,}000\times0.30/10^6=\$0.036\), \(6{,}000\times15/10^6=\$0.09\): $0.216 per attempt, $3,456 per round. Generating with the small policy is twelve times cheaper, but it yields fewer successes on the hard tasks, so many pipelines mix the two.

Rejection sampling needs a verifier that says whether an attempt succeeded. Many of the behaviours we most want, such as a clear explanation to an anxious customer, a well-organised research report, or an appropriate amount of clarification, have no such verifier. People can, however, say which of two attempts is better far more reliably than they can score one in isolation or write a perfect one themselves. Learning from such comparisons is the next idea.

17.4 Preferences and reward models

A preference is a judgement that one output \(y_w\) (the "winner") is better than another \(y_l\) (the "loser") for the same input \(x\), written \(y_w\succ y_l\). Preferences have three attractions as training data. They are easier to give than demonstrations: a labeller who could not write an excellent research report can still tell which of two reports is better. They are more consistent than absolute scores: people disagree about whether a reply deserves 6 or 7 out of 10 far more than about which of two replies is better. And they capture qualities that no verifier encodes, such as tone, clarity and judgement. The price is that a comparison carries at most one bit of information, and that the bit is noisy, so a model is needed that turns many noisy comparisons into a usable training signal.

The standard model is due to Bradley and Terry (1952), who used it to rank sports teams from the outcomes of games[7]. It posits a latent score \(r(x,y)\) for each output and models the probability of a preference as a logistic function of the score difference:

\[ P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\big),\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{RM}}(\phi)=-\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D_{\text{pref}}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big]. \]

A reward model \(r_\phi\) is a neural network, usually the language model itself with its output layer replaced by a scalar head, trained by minimising \(\mathcal L_{\text{RM}}\) on a dataset \(\mathcal D_{\text{pref}}\) of comparisons. The loss is the logistic-regression loss on score differences, and it has two properties worth noticing. First, only differences matter: adding any function of \(x\) alone to \(r\) changes nothing, so rewards are identified only up to a per-input constant, a fact that DPO will exploit in §17.6. Second, the gradient with respect to the margin \(\Delta=r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\) is \(-\sigma(-\Delta)\): pairs the model already orders correctly by a wide margin contribute almost nothing, and the training signal concentrates on pairs it gets wrong or is unsure about.

Preference learning for language models came from reinforcement learning. Christiano and colleagues (2017) trained simulated robots and Atari agents from a few hundred to a few thousand human comparisons of short video clips, by fitting a Bradley–Terry reward model and optimising a policy against it[8]. Stiennon and colleagues (2020) applied the method to summarisation, and found that summaries from a policy optimised against a reward model trained on human comparisons were preferred to the human-written reference summaries; and Ouyang and colleagues (2022) used it in InstructGPT to make GPT-3 follow instructions, the pipeline behind the first ChatGPT[9]. This is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF, §3.4), and the reward model is its centre.

Worked example — what a reward model's numbers mean

A reward model scores two replies of the support agent to an angry customer: \(r_\phi(x,y_1)=1.5\) (apologises, explains the rebooking options, offers the voucher the policy allows) and \(r_\phi(x,y_2)=0.5\) (correct but curt).

Predicted preference. \(P(y_1\succ y_2)=\sigma(1.0)=0.731\). The model predicts that about 73% of labellers would prefer \(y_1\).

Loss on this pair. If the label is \(y_1\succ y_2\), the loss is \(-\log 0.731=0.313\); if the label is reversed, \(-\log0.269=1.313\). A reversed label costs four times as much and produces a gradient of magnitude \(\sigma(1)=0.73\) rather than 0.27.

The ceiling. If labellers themselves agree on only about 73% of comparisons (InstructGPT reported inter-labeller agreement of roughly this level), no reward model can reach much above 73% accuracy on held-out labels, and a reward model reporting 90% is probably fitting artefacts. Reward-model accuracy is meaningful only relative to human agreement.

Reward models have weaknesses that matter more as the policy optimised against them gets stronger. They are trained on outputs of an earlier policy, so they are least reliable precisely on the novel outputs that optimisation produces. They learn shortcuts present in the labels: longer answers, confident tone, and lists were each preferred often enough in early datasets that reward models rewarded them for their own sake. And a single scalar must summarise many qualities that labellers weigh differently. The consequence is reward over-optimisation: as a policy is pushed to higher reward-model scores, its true quality first rises, then falls, as it finds outputs that the reward model scores highly for the wrong reasons (Gao, Schulman and Hilton, 2022, measured the shape of this curve)[10]. The defence that RLHF builds in is to keep the policy close to the model it started from, which is the KL-regularised objective of the next section.

17.5 The RLHF pipeline and its objective

A reward model scores outputs; it does not by itself produce better ones. The RLHF pipeline of InstructGPT, which became the template for the post-training of most chat models between 2022 and 2024, has three stages. First, supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations gives a policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) that already follows instructions reasonably. Second, the SFT policy generates pairs of outputs for many prompts, people (or, increasingly, models) compare them, and a reward model is fitted to the comparisons. Third, the policy is optimised by reinforcement learning to maximise the reward model's score, subject to a penalty for moving away from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\):

\[ \max_{\pi_\theta}\;\mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb E_{x}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big]. \]

The KL term does two jobs. It limits reward over-optimisation, because outputs the reward model overrates are usually far from what \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) would write, and moving there costs KL. And it preserves the abilities of the starting model, its fluency and general knowledge, which the reward model knows nothing about. The coefficient \(\beta\) sets the trade-off: a small \(\beta\) chases reward and risks hacking it; a large \(\beta\) barely moves the policy.

We met this objective in §3.4, where we derived its optimum in closed form. For each input \(x\), maximising \(\sum_y\pi(y)r(x,y)-\beta\sum_y\pi(y)\log(\pi(y)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y))\) over distributions \(\pi\) gives, by a Lagrange multiplier for normalisation,

\[ \pi^\star(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big),\qquad Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big). \]

The optimal policy reweights the reference policy by the exponentiated reward. It never assigns probability to outputs \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) considers impossible, and it moves probability towards high-reward outputs by a factor \(e^{\Delta r/\beta}\) for a reward difference \(\Delta r\). The optimum cannot be computed directly, because \(Z(x)\) sums over all possible outputs, so RLHF approximates it by reinforcement learning, classically with proximal policy optimisation (PPO, Chapter 18). PPO for language models is expensive and delicate: it keeps four models in memory (the policy, the reference policy, the reward model and a learned value function), generates fresh samples throughout training, and is sensitive to many hyperparameters. By 2023 the cost and fragility of this third stage had become the bottleneck of preference learning, and the question was whether it could be avoided.

17.6 Direct preference optimisation

Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning and Finn (2023) observed that the closed-form optimum can be inverted[11]. Their paper's title states the conclusion: your language model is secretly a reward model. Taking logarithms of the optimum and solving for the reward gives

\[ r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x). \]

Every reward function corresponds to an optimal policy, and every policy (with the same support as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)) corresponds to a reward, up to the term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) that depends only on \(x\). Now substitute this expression into the Bradley–Terry model of §17.4. The probability of a preference depends only on the difference of rewards for the same \(x\), so the intractable \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels:

\[ P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big). \]

This is a model of preferences parameterised by a policy. Fitting it to data by maximum likelihood, with the policy \(\pi_\theta\) in place of \(\pi^\star\), gives the DPO loss:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D_{\text{pref}}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big]. \]

The derivation is short but the consequence is large. Minimising \(\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}\) is equivalent to fitting a Bradley–Terry reward model whose rewards are the implicit rewards \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))\) and simultaneously taking the KL-regularised optimal policy for that reward, because by construction \(\pi_\theta\) is that policy. No separate reward model is trained, no samples are generated during training, and no reinforcement-learning machinery is needed: DPO is a classification loss on log-probability ratios, as easy to run as supervised fine-tuning, with two models in memory (the policy and a frozen reference). For a trajectory, \(\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) is the sum of the log-probabilities of its tokens, with the observation mask \(m_i\) of §17.2 applied, so DPO extends to agent trajectories without change.

The gradient shows what DPO does to the policy. Writing \(\hat\Delta=\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\) for the implicit margin,

\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb E\Big[\sigma(-\hat\Delta)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big]. \]

Each step raises the likelihood of the preferred output and lowers that of the dispreferred one, weighted by \(\sigma(-\hat\Delta)\), which is large when the implicit reward model currently orders the pair wrongly. Without the weight, the loss would be a simple "unlikelihood" objective that is known to degenerate; with it, pairs already learned are left alone, which is the same self-limiting behaviour as the Bradley–Terry gradient in §17.4.

Worked example — one DPO step by hand

Take \(\beta=0.1\). For a prompt \(x\), the current policy assigns the preferred trajectory \(y_w\) 1.2 times the probability the reference policy does, and the dispreferred \(y_l\) 0.8 times.

Implicit rewards. \(\hat r(y_w)=0.1\log1.2=0.1\times0.1823=0.01823\); \(\hat r(y_l)=0.1\log0.8=0.1\times(-0.2231)=-0.02231\). Margin \(\hat\Delta=0.04055\).

Loss. \(-\log\sigma(0.04055)=-\log0.5101=0.6731\). At initialisation, when \(\pi_\theta=\pi_{\text{ref}}\), the margin is 0 and the loss is \(\log2=0.6931\); the step so far has reduced it by only 0.02, because \(\beta=0.1\) makes implicit rewards small.

Gradient weight. \(\sigma(-\hat\Delta)=0.4899\), nearly the maximum 0.5: the pair is still almost unlearned. To reach a loss of 0.1 (\(\sigma(\hat\Delta)=0.905\), \(\hat\Delta=2.25\)), the log-ratio difference must reach \(2.25/\beta=22.5\) nats; for a long trajectory, spread over thousands of tokens, that is a small change per token.

Reading \(\beta\). At \(\beta=0.5\), the same ratios give \(\hat\Delta=0.203\) and a loss of 0.5969. A larger \(\beta\) means each nat of divergence buys more margin, so the loss is satisfied with a smaller departure from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): \(\beta\) plays the same role as in the RLHF objective.

DPO was quickly adopted, because it made preference learning accessible to anyone who could run supervised fine-tuning; within a year most open-weight chat models were post-trained with DPO or one of its variants. Its practical behaviour also revealed problems. DPO is offline: it learns from a fixed dataset of comparisons, usually generated by another model, so it never sees its own outputs and can only push probability around among the outputs present in the data. The loss depends only on the margin, so it can be reduced by lowering the likelihood of both outputs, the dispreferred one faster; in practice the likelihood of preferred responses often falls during training, which can move probability to outputs in neither set. Because the log-probability of a sequence sums over tokens, longer outputs produce larger log-ratios, and DPO-trained models tend to grow more verbose when longer answers were preferred in the data. And when preferences are deterministic (\(y_w\) always preferred), the Bradley–Terry likelihood is maximised only at infinite margin, which drives the policy far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) whatever \(\beta\) is.

A family of variants addresses these issues. IPO (Azar and colleagues, 2023) replaced the logistic loss with a squared loss that targets a finite margin, \((\hat\Delta-1/(2\beta))^2\) in suitable units, so that deterministic preferences no longer push the margin to infinity[12]. KTO (Ethayarajh and colleagues, 2024) learns from unpaired signals, a thumbs-up or thumbs-down on single outputs, using a loss modelled on prospect theory, which suits production feedback where pairs are rarely available[13]. Length-normalised and reference-free variants (SimPO, 2024, among others) divide log-probabilities by length or drop \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). And online or iterative DPO regenerates the comparison pairs from the current policy every round, judged by a reward model or a verifier, which recovers much of the benefit of on-policy RL while keeping DPO's simple loss. For agents, the iterative form is the useful one, for the reason §17.2 gave: data from the policy's own states is what fixes compounding error.

Drill 17.3 — when has DPO learned a pair?

With \(\beta=0.1\), what log-ratio difference \(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\) makes the implicit model assign 90% probability to the observed preference? If the change is spread evenly over the 2,000 agent-written tokens of each trajectory, how much does each token's log-probability move?

\(\sigma(\hat\Delta)=0.9\Rightarrow\hat\Delta=\log 9=2.197\), so the log-ratio difference is \(2.197/0.1=21.97\) nats. Split between the two trajectories (up about 11 nats on \(y_w\), down about 11 on \(y_l\)), that is about 0.0055 nats per token, a 0.55% change in each token's probability. Small per-token changes add up to large sequence-level effects, which is why DPO on long trajectories can shift behaviour substantially while each token looks almost unchanged, and why length matters so much to the loss.

17.7 Preferences for agents: trajectories or steps?

For a chat model, a comparison is between two responses. For an agent it can be between two whole trajectories, or between two alternative actions at the same step of one trajectory, and the choice matters for the same reason credit assignment matters in reinforcement learning. A trajectory-level preference says that one 40-step run was better than another, but not which of its 40 steps made the difference; the two runs may share 35 good steps and differ in five, of which perhaps one was decisive. The DPO gradient spreads the preference over all the agent's tokens in both runs, pushing up the shared good steps in \(y_w\) and pushing them down in \(y_l\), which mostly cancels, and leaving a weak and noisy signal on the step that mattered. With enough data the noise averages out, but each comparison is expensive.

Step-level preferences localise the signal. Take a trajectory prefix \(h_t\) (the history up to step \(t\)) and two candidate next actions \(a\) and \(a'\); continue the trajectory from each, several times, and estimate the success probability of each continuation; the action with the higher estimate is preferred. The resulting pair \((h_t,a\succ a')\) differs in exactly one action, so the DPO gradient falls where the decision was made. Figure 17.5 shows the construction. It is a Monte Carlo estimate of the action value \(Q(h_t,a)\), which Chapter 18 defines formally, used to label a comparison rather than as a reward for RL.

Figure 17.5 also shows the cost: each preference requires several rollouts per branch, so a step-level dataset is many times more expensive to collect than a trajectory-level one, and the estimates \(\hat Q\) are noisy when rollouts are few. With four rollouts per branch, as in the figure, the standard error of each estimate is up to \(\sqrt{0.25/4}=0.25\), so a difference of 0.5 between branches is about 1.4 standard errors of the difference, enough to be suggestive but not conclusive; pairs with small estimated differences should be discarded. The practical compromise is to branch only at steps where the trajectories of a task diverge, or where a verifier or judge flags a likely mistake, rather than at every step.

Agent Q (Putta and colleagues, 2024) combined these ideas for web agents[14]. A Llama-3 70B agent explored a booking website with Monte Carlo tree search, guided at each node by a model's own critique of candidate actions; the branches of the search tree supplied step-level preference pairs, scored by the eventual outcome and the critique, and the model was trained on them with DPO, iteratively. On real bookings on the OpenTable website, the zero-shot success rate of the base model was 18.6%; after training, 81.7%; and with search at inference time as well, 95.4%. Other work reached similar conclusions in other environments: process-level signals beat outcome-only signals per comparison collected, and iterating, so that pairs come from the current policy's own states, matters as much as the loss used.

Who provides the comparisons? For agents, mostly not people. Human comparisons of long trajectories are slow and expensive (a labeller must read thousands of tokens of reasoning, actions and observations), and people are poor at spotting which of two plausible tool calls was better. The common sources are a verifier (the trajectory whose tests pass is preferred), rollout statistics as in Figure 17.5, and a judge model with a rubric (Chapter 6), which is cheaper but inherits the judge's biases, including its preference for longer and more confident trajectories. RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback, and Constitutional AI (Bai and colleagues, 2022), in which a model judges outputs against a written set of principles, showed that model-generated preferences can substitute for most human ones for many qualities[15]. Human comparisons remain essential where they are irreplaceable: for qualities no model judges reliably, and for auditing the model-generated labels, a small sample of which should always be checked by people.

Drill 17.4 — budgeting step-level data

A team wants 5,000 step-level pairs for the coding agent. Each pair needs two branches with 4 rollouts each; a rollout from the branch point takes on average 12 further steps of 0.004 dollars (small model, mostly cached). Branching is done only at flagged steps, of which there are on average 2 per task. How many tasks and how many dollars are needed, and how many pairs survive if 40% are discarded for small differences?

Each pair costs \(8\times12\times\$0.004=\$0.384\) in rollouts, so 5,000 attempted pairs cost $1,920, using \(5{,}000/2=2{,}500\) task runs to supply branch points. With 40% discarded, 3,000 pairs survive, so to keep 5,000 the team must attempt about 8,333 pairs, costing $3,200 and needing about 4,170 task runs. The rollouts, not the training, dominate the cost of step-level preference learning.

Preference learning, like behaviour cloning, has so far assumed that the model being trained is the one that will be deployed and that its parameters can be changed freely. Two practical questions remain. Which model should be trained, given that the cheapest way to obtain a good small agent is often to copy a large one? And how can a model with tens of billions of parameters be fine-tuned without the resources to update them all? The next two sections answer them.

17.8 Distillation: teaching a small model

The most common reason to fine-tune an agent in practice is not to exceed what any model can do but to make a cheap model do what an expensive one already does. Distillation, named by Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015) for training a small network to match a large one's output distribution[16], has for agents become mostly sequence-level: the teacher, a frontier model in a well-engineered harness, performs the target tasks, its successful trajectories are kept (filtered by a verifier or judge, exactly as in §17.3), and the student is trained on them by the masked SFT loss of §17.2. FireAct's 500 GPT-4 trajectories were distillation in this sense. When the teacher's token probabilities are available, as they are for open-weight teachers, the student can instead match the teacher's full next-token distribution on every position, which carries more information per token than the sampled tokens alone, and can be done on the student's own trajectories (on-policy distillation), the DAgger idea of §17.2 at the token level.

Whether distillation pays is a matter of arithmetic. The training run, the data generation and the evaluation are fixed costs; the saving is per conversation. Take the support agent: a conversation uses about 20,000 input tokens and 1,700 output tokens, which on the reference frontier model costs \(20{,}000\times\$3/10^6+1{,}700\times\$15/10^6\approx\$0.085\). A fine-tuned small model on the same conversation costs about \(\$0.007\) at the small model's list prices; serving a custom fine-tune usually carries a premium, so we take \(\$0.008\). Suppose generating 20,000 teacher trajectories, training and evaluating cost $5,000 in total, amortised over a year, $417 a month. At \(V\) conversations a month the frontier model costs \(0.085V\) and the distilled model \(0.008V+417\), so distillation breaks even at \(V=417/0.077\approx5{,}400\) conversations a month. Figure 17.6 plots the two cost lines.

Figure 17.6 omits two costs that often decide the question. The first is quality: the distilled model must actually match the teacher on the deployment distribution, and §17.2 told us where it will not, on long tasks and on the rare, off-distribution conversations where the teacher's ability to recover matters. A distilled model that resolves 2 percentage points fewer conversations, each escalation to a person costing $4, loses \(0.02\times\$4=\$0.08\) per conversation, nearly the whole saving. The second is maintenance: the deployment distribution drifts, policies change, and the teacher improves, so a distilled model must be re-trained and re-evaluated periodically, each time at part of the fixed cost. The usual answer is a cascade (§3.7): the distilled model handles the conversations it is confident about and routes the rest to the frontier model, which captures most of the saving while protecting quality on the hard tail.

Worked example — distillation with escalation

The distilled support model of Figure 17.6 resolves 91% of conversations, against 94% for the frontier model; an unresolved conversation is escalated to a person at $4. Volume is 50,000 conversations a month.

Frontier only. Model \(50{,}000\times\$0.085=\$4{,}250\); escalations \(0.06\times50{,}000\times\$4=\$12{,}000\); total $16,250.

Distilled only. Model \(\$400+\$417=\$817\); escalations \(0.09\times50{,}000\times\$4=\$18{,}000\); total $18,817. Cheaper on tokens, more expensive overall.

Cascade. The distilled model handles every conversation first; on the 15% it flags as uncertain, the frontier model takes over (at full frontier cost). Suppose the flagged set contains most of its failures, so that the cascade resolves 93.5%. Model cost \(\$817+0.15\times50{,}000\times\$0.085=\$817+\$638=\$1{,}455\); escalations \(0.065\times50{,}000\times\$4=\$13{,}000\); total $14,455, the cheapest of the three. The cascade wins only if the flag is good: the arithmetic depends on the distilled model knowing when it does not know, which must be measured.

17.9 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Fine-tuning every parameter of a large model is expensive in memory as much as in computation. Training with the Adam optimiser stores, for each parameter, the weight, its gradient and two moment estimates, about 16 bytes per parameter in mixed precision, so full fine-tuning of a 70-billion-parameter model needs over a terabyte of accelerator memory, and each fine-tuned copy is a full-size checkpoint to store and serve. For an organisation that wants a dozen fine-tunes of one base model, one per product or customer, this is impractical. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning trains a small number of new parameters while keeping the base model frozen, and its dominant form is LoRA (low-rank adaptation; Hu and colleagues at Microsoft, 2021)[17].

LoRA rests on an empirical observation: the change in a weight matrix during fine-tuning has low effective rank, because adapting a capable model to a narrower task needs only a few new directions. LoRA therefore parameterises the update as a product of two thin matrices, \(W=W_0+\frac{\alpha_{\text{lo}}}{d_r}BA\), where \(W_0\in\mathbb R^{d_1\times d_2}\) is frozen, \(B\in\mathbb R^{d_1\times d_r}\) and \(A\in\mathbb R^{d_r\times d_2}\) are trained, the rank \(d_r\) is small (4 to 64 is typical), and \(\alpha_{\text{lo}}\) is a scaling constant. \(B\) is initialised to zero, so training starts exactly at the base model. Figure 17.7 shows the structure.

For the mathematician — what a rank constraint buys

The full update \(\Delta W\in\mathbb R^{d_1\times d_2}\) has \(d_1d_2\) free entries; the LoRA update \(BA\) lies on the set of matrices of rank at most \(d_r\), a manifold of dimension \(d_r(d_1+d_2-d_r)\), parameterised (redundantly, since \(BA=(BG)(G^{-1}A)\) for any invertible \(G\)) by \(d_r(d_1+d_2)\) numbers. By the Eckart–Young theorem, the best rank-\(d_r\) approximation of any matrix in Frobenius norm is its truncated singular-value decomposition, so LoRA can represent a full fine-tuning update well exactly when that update's singular values decay quickly, which is the empirical finding that motivated it. The adapter can be merged after training, \(W=W_0+\frac{\alpha_{\text{lo}}}{d_r}BA\), so it adds no inference latency; or kept separate, so that one server holds one base model and many adapters, applying the right one to each request.

Figure 17.7 makes the saving concrete for Llama 3 8B, whose attention projections are \(4{,}096\times4{,}096\) matrices of 16.8 million parameters each. A rank-16 adapter on one such matrix trains \(16\times(4{,}096+4{,}096)=131{,}072\) parameters, 0.78% of the matrix; adapters on the query, key, value and output projections and the feed-forward matrices of all 32 layers come to a few tens of millions of trained parameters, well under 1% of the model, and a checkpoint of tens of megabytes. Optimiser memory falls in proportion, so a fine-tune that would need several accelerators fits on one. QLoRA (Dettmers and colleagues, 2023) went further by storing the frozen base model in 4-bit precision while training a LoRA adapter in higher precision, which made fine-tuning a 65-billion-parameter model possible on a single 48 GB accelerator[18]. The cost of these savings is capacity: a low-rank update cannot express every change, and for large shifts in behaviour (a new language, a large new body of knowledge) full fine-tuning remains better. For the typical agent goals of §17.1, format, protocol, and a domain's conventions, low-rank adapters usually come close to full fine-tuning.

17.10 When is training worth it?

Fine-tuning an agent is a commitment, and the chapter's methods should be weighed against the alternative the rest of the book has developed: better prompting, context engineering, tools and architecture, applied to the strongest available model. Four considerations decide. Volume: training has a large fixed cost and a per-use saving, so it pays only at scale, as Figure 17.6 showed. Stability: a fine-tuned model freezes a behaviour; if the policy, the tools or the base model change monthly, the model must be re-trained just as often, while a prompt can be edited in minutes. Data: training needs thousands of good trajectories and a reliable way to tell good from bad, which is to say a verifier or a trustworthy judge; if these do not exist, they are the first project, and building them also improves evaluation (Chapter 19) and prompting. Ceiling: if the best prompted frontier model already meets the quality target, training can only reduce cost; if no prompted model meets it, training may be the only route, but then the data must come from somewhere better than the models available, usually from people or from search with a verifier.

The decision usually comes out in a characteristic order. Start with the strongest model and the best harness, and measure. If the system works and costs too much, distil it into a smaller model, with a cascade for the hard tail. If a specific behaviour is unreliable despite good prompting (a tool format, a recovery habit, a policy rule), fine-tune for that behaviour with a small amount of targeted data, often with LoRA. If the system's quality is limited by multi-step competence that prompting cannot reach, and a verifier exists, train from outcomes, with rejection sampling first and reinforcement learning when that plateaus (Chapter 18). At every stage, keep the evaluation set fixed and held out, because each training method finds ways to improve the metric that do not improve the system, and the evaluation is the only defence.

Looking ahead

Every method in this chapter learns from data that someone, a person, a stronger model or a verifier applied to a fixed set of samples, has already labelled as good. That limits the student to the quality of its supervision: behaviour cloning cannot exceed its expert; DPO on a fixed dataset only redistributes probability among outputs it has seen; rejection sampling sharpens what the model can already do. The exceptions were telling: DAgger, iterative DPO and search-based step preferences all improved by collecting data from the policy's own behaviour. Taken to its conclusion, that idea is reinforcement learning: let the agent act in an environment, observe the outcomes, and adjust the policy to make good outcomes more likely, with no demonstrations at all. Chapter 18 develops it: the policy gradient we glimpsed in §17.3, PPO and the group-relative methods that made reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards practical for reasoning models, multi-turn credit assignment for agents, and the reward hacking that appears whenever a strong optimiser meets an imperfect reward.

Summary

  • Training turns behaviours an agent must be told into defaults. The recurring goals are format and protocol, domain competence, robust habits and cost (distillation).
  • Behaviour cloning fine-tunes on demonstration trajectories with a loss masked to the agent's own tokens; masking prevents the model learning to invent observations.
  • Cloning suffers covariate shift: excess cost grows as \(\epsilon H^2\) because mistakes lead to unfamiliar states. DAgger labels the learner's own states with the expert's actions and makes the growth linear, \(O(\epsilon H)\).
  • Rejection-sampling fine-tuning keeps the verified successes of the policy's own attempts; at the current parameters its gradient equals the gradient of expected success. Its data is biased towards easy tasks and can include lucky successes.
  • Preferences \(y_w\succ y_l\) are easier and more consistent to give than demonstrations or scores. The Bradley–Terry model fits a reward model by logistic loss on reward differences; accuracy is bounded by labeller agreement.
  • RLHF maximises reward with a KL penalty to the reference policy; its optimum is \(\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}/Z\). DPO inverts this to express the reward through the policy, cancels \(Z\) in the Bradley–Terry model, and gives a simple classification loss on log-probability ratios.
  • DPO is offline, can lower the likelihood of both responses, and is sensitive to length; IPO, KTO, length-normalised and iterative variants address these.
  • For agents, step-level preferences from branching rollouts localise credit far better than trajectory-level ones, at higher collection cost; Agent Q combined tree search and step-level DPO.
  • Distillation trains a small model on a teacher's verified trajectories; it pays above a break-even volume, best combined with a cascade. LoRA trains a low-rank update \(BA\), under 1% of parameters, and QLoRA adds a 4-bit frozen base.
  • Train when volume is high, behaviour is stable, data and verifiers exist, and prompting has hit a ceiling; always keep a held-out evaluation.

Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then reveal the model answer and rate yourself.

Warm-up When would you fine-tune an agent rather than improve its prompt?

When volume is high enough to amortise the fixed cost; the behaviour and environment are stable; good trajectories and a verifier or judge exist; and either prompting has hit a quality ceiling or a prompted frontier model works but costs too much (distillation). Typical targets: a harness's tool format, a recovery or verification habit, a domain's conventions, and cost. Keep a held-out evaluation throughout.

Warm-up What is behaviour cloning, and how do you set up the loss for agent trajectories?

Supervised learning on expert trajectories: predict the expert's actions given the history. Serialise each trajectory as tokens and take the cross-entropy over agent-written tokens only (reasoning and tool calls), masking observations and user messages, so the model learns to act given observations and not to hallucinate tool outputs. Mix in general instruction data to preserve general abilities.

Warm-up What is DPO in one paragraph?

Direct preference optimisation trains a policy directly on preference pairs without a reward model or RL. It uses the closed-form optimum of the KL-regularised objective to write the reward as \(\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) plus a term in \(x\) that cancels in the Bradley–Terry model, giving the loss \(-\log\sigma(\beta[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}])\). It is as simple as SFT, with two models in memory.

Core Derive the quadratic compounding-error bound for behaviour cloning.

Let the learner disagree with the expert with probability \(\epsilon\) per step on expert states, and pessimistically assume that after the first disagreement it fails every remaining step. The first mistake occurs at step \(t\) with probability \(\epsilon(1-\epsilon)^{t-1}\) and costs \(H-t+1\), so the excess is \(\sum_t\epsilon(1-\epsilon)^{t-1}(H-t+1)\approx\epsilon H(H+1)/2=O(\epsilon H^2)\) for small \(\epsilon H\). Ross and Bagnell showed \(\epsilon H^2\) is an upper bound and tight. DAgger trains on the learner's states, keeping errors at rate \(\epsilon\) per step: \(O(\epsilon H)\).

Core How would you apply DAgger to distil a frontier coding agent into a small one?

Clone from teacher trajectories first. Then iterate: run the student on training tasks in its own harness; at sampled steps (or flagged ones), give the teacher the student's trajectory prefix in the student's format and tools and ask for the next action; validate the teacher's action by executing it where possible; add the labelled states to the dataset and retrain. Mix teacher control early. Cheaper complement: inject errors into teacher runs and keep its recoveries.

Core Why does rejection-sampling fine-tuning improve a model, and what are its biases?

Its objective \(\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta_0}}[R\log\pi_\theta]\) has, at \(\theta_0\), the same gradient as expected reward, so it is an approximate policy-improvement step. Biases: data proportional to current success (easy tasks dominate; unsolved tasks contribute nothing), lucky or verifier-gaming successes are trained on, and diversity can collapse. Mitigate with per-task caps, more samples on hard tasks, hints, stronger verifiers and spot checks, and teacher data for the tail.

Core How is a reward model trained, and how do you know if it is good?

Collect comparisons \(y_w\succ y_l\) for prompts; fit a scalar-head model by the Bradley–Terry logistic loss \(-\log\sigma(r_\phi(y_w)-r_\phi(y_l))\). Evaluate held-out pairwise accuracy relative to inter-labeller agreement (the ceiling), calibration of predicted probabilities, accuracy on outputs of the current (optimised) policy rather than the old one, and sensitivity to known shortcuts (length, confidence, formatting). Watch for over-optimisation by tracking true quality with human or verifier checks as reward rises.

Core Derive the DPO loss from the RLHF objective.

The KL-regularised objective \(\max\mathbb E[r]-\beta\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) has optimum \(\pi^\star=\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}/Z(x)\). Invert: \(r=\beta\log(\pi^\star/\pi_{\text{ref}})+\beta\log Z(x)\). Substitute into Bradley–Terry; \(Z\) cancels in the difference. Replace \(\pi^\star\) by \(\pi_\theta\) and maximise the likelihood of the observed preferences: \(\mathcal L=-\mathbb E\log\sigma(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)})\).

Core What does the DPO gradient do, and why is the weight important?

\(\nabla\mathcal L=-\beta\mathbb E[\sigma(-\hat\Delta)(\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l))]\): it raises the preferred and lowers the dispreferred likelihood, weighted by how wrongly the implicit reward model currently orders the pair. Without the weight it would be an unlikelihood objective that keeps pushing learned pairs and degenerates; with it, learned pairs stop contributing.

Core Compare RLHF with PPO and DPO.

PPO-RLHF: trains a reward model, then on-policy RL with KL penalty; four models (policy, reference, reward, value), fresh samples, many hyperparameters; can explore and improve beyond the data; reward model can be hacked. DPO: no reward model or sampling; two models; stable and cheap; offline (only redistributes among dataset outputs), length bias, both-likelihoods-fall, overfits deterministic preferences. Iterative/online DPO with a judge or verifier recovers much of the on-policy benefit.

Deep Should agent preferences be collected at the trajectory or step level?

Trajectory-level is cheap (one comparison per pair of runs) but spreads credit over all steps, most of which are shared, giving a weak noisy signal on the decisive step. Step-level branches from a shared prefix and compares actions by rollout success estimates (Monte Carlo \(Q\)), so the signal falls on one decision, but each pair costs many rollouts and estimates are noisy (discard small differences). Compromise: branch only at divergence points or steps flagged by a verifier or judge; iterate on the current policy (Agent Q-style).

Deep Why does DPO tend to make models verbose, and how would you counter it?

A sequence's log-probability sums over tokens, so log-ratio margins grow with length, and if longer outputs were preferred more often in the data, the cheapest way to increase the margin is to favour length. Counter by balancing length in the preference data (or controlling for it in judging), length-normalising log-probabilities (SimPO-style), adding a length penalty, and evaluating with length-controlled metrics.

Deep Explain LoRA mathematically and when it falls short.

Freeze \(W_0\) and train \(W=W_0+(\alpha/d_r)BA\) with \(B\in\mathbb R^{d_1\times d_r}\) (init 0) and \(A\in\mathbb R^{d_r\times d_2}\): \(d_r(d_1+d_2)\) parameters instead of \(d_1d_2\), e.g. 131k vs 16.8M for rank 16 on 4096×4096. Works when fine-tuning updates have rapidly decaying singular values (Eckart–Young). Mergeable, so no inference overhead; many adapters per base model. Falls short for large shifts (new languages, much new knowledge), where full fine-tuning or higher rank is needed. QLoRA keeps the base in 4-bit.

Design Design a pipeline to distil a frontier support agent into a small model.

Define the task distribution from logs; run the frontier agent in the production harness on thousands of tasks; filter trajectories by policy verifiers, outcome checks and a judge, with human spot checks; balance by task type and difficulty; mask observations; mix general data; LoRA or full SFT on the small model; DAgger rounds on the student's own states. Evaluate on a held-out, production-like set: resolution, policy violations, escalation, cost. Deploy as a cascade with a calibrated uncertainty flag routing to the frontier model; monitor drift; schedule retraining. Check the economics: break-even volume including escalations.

Design You have a coding agent and a test-based verifier. How would you improve it with training, step by step?

Build a large task set with reproducible environments and tests (and held-out evaluation). Round 1: distil teacher trajectories that pass tests (masked SFT). Round 2+: rejection sampling on the student's own attempts, capped per task, more samples on hard tasks, checks for test-gaming (hidden tests, judge review of diffs). Add step-level preferences at divergence points and train with iterative DPO. When that plateaus, move to on-policy RL with the verifier as reward (Chapter 18). Track held-out pass rate, pass^k reliability, cost per task and reward-hacking indicators every round.

Design How would you collect preference data for a research agent's reports?

No verifier for overall quality, so use comparisons. Generate pairs from the current policy for the same question (varied sampling or prompts), and decompose judging: automatic checks for citation faithfulness and coverage, a judge model with a rubric for organisation and accuracy (randomised order, length-controlled), and expert human comparisons on a sample to calibrate and audit the judge. Collect step-level pairs at key decisions (which sources to read, when to stop searching) where rollouts are affordable. Train with iterative DPO or KTO from user feedback; check for verbosity and confident-but-wrong drift.

Core What goes wrong with reward models as the policy improves?

They are trained on an older policy's outputs, so they are least reliable on the new outputs optimisation produces; they learn label shortcuts (length, confidence, lists); a scalar compresses many criteria. Pushing reward up eventually lowers true quality (over-optimisation). Defences: KL penalty, retraining the reward model on current-policy outputs, ensembles, held-out true-quality checks, and early stopping on true quality.
