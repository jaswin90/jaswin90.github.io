Part VII · Reliability, Safety and Production — Chapter 19

Evaluating Agents

Every claim in this book about what works has rested on a measurement, and agents are unusually hard to measure: they are stochastic, they act in environments that must be reproduced, their trajectories are long, and success often has no single definition. This chapter treats evaluation as a discipline: the benchmarks and what they measure, reliability metrics, the statistics of small task sets, threats to validity, model judges and their correction, and how to build an evaluation for one's own agent.

Why this chapter

Part VII turns from building and training agents to making them dependable, and dependability starts with knowing how good an agent is. The question sounds simple and is not. Suppose a new version of the coding agent solves 52% of a benchmark's tasks and the old one solved 49%. Is the new one better, or is the difference noise? Would it be better on our repositories? Does it solve the same tasks every time, or a different half on each run? Did it solve them, or did it find a way to make the checks pass (§18.7)? And at what cost per task? Each of these questions has a quantitative answer, and getting the answers wrong is the most common way agent projects fool themselves, sometimes for months.

Chapter 1 introduced the two reliability metrics, pass@\(k\) and pass\(^k\) (§1.10), and Chapter 6 introduced verifiers and judges. This chapter builds on both. It is the most statistical chapter of the book, and the mathematics is elementary (binomial proportions, paired tests, a little measurement-error correction), but its consequences are not: most published agent comparisons rest on a few hundred tasks and a single run, which, as we will compute, often cannot distinguish the systems being compared.

Learning objectives
  1. Explain why agent evaluation is harder than model evaluation, and name the major agent benchmarks, what they measure, and when and why each appeared.
  2. Compute pass@\(k\) and pass\(^k\) with unbiased estimators, and explain why pass\(^k\) depends on how success is distributed across tasks.
  3. Put confidence intervals on success rates, decide how many tasks and runs an evaluation needs, and compare two agents with a paired test.
  4. Identify threats to validity: contamination, saturation, broken tasks, shortcut solutions and harness effects.
  5. Use model judges with rubrics, recognise their biases, and correct measured success rates for judge error.
  6. Design an evaluation for a production agent: task sources, graders, offline and online evaluation, and cost-aware reporting.

We use pass@\(k\) and pass\(^k\) (§1.10), the compounding of errors (§2.6), verifiers and their error rates (§6.2), the coding, computer-use and web agents of Chapters 10–11, and reward hacking (§18.7).

19.1 Why evaluating agents is hard

Evaluating a language model on a question-answering benchmark is, mechanically, easy: feed each question, compare the answer with a reference, count. Evaluating an agent breaks every step of this. The input is not a question but a task together with an environment: a repository at a given commit with its dependencies, a website in a given state, a simulated customer with a goal and a temperament. The environment must be reproduced exactly for every run, because a website that has changed, or a package that has a new version, changes the task. The output is not an answer but a trajectory, tens or hundreds of steps long, and what we want to score is usually its effect on the environment (did the tests pass, is the booking right) together with properties of the path (did it violate a policy on the way, how much did it cost). Success may have no single definition: a research report can be better or worse along many dimensions. And the agent is stochastic: the same task, run twice, can succeed once and fail once, so a single run is a sample, not a measurement.

Each difficulty has a consequence for how evaluations are built. Reproducible environments mean containers, snapshots and simulators, which are expensive to build and maintain, and which is why public agent benchmarks are few and much reused. Outcome-based scoring means writing checkers for end states, which can be wrong in both directions: accepting failures and rejecting valid solutions (§6.2's false-positive and false-negative rates, now applied to the evaluation itself). Stochasticity means repeated runs and statistics. And the cost of a run, which for a long coding task can be dollars and tens of minutes, means that evaluation sets are small, a few hundred tasks, which makes the statistics matter. The rest of the chapter takes these in turn, beginning with the public benchmarks, because they define what the field measures and shape what it builds.

19.2 A map of agent benchmarks

Agent benchmarks evolved with agents, and each generation appeared because the previous one had stopped discriminating. Before 2023, the environments used for language agents came from other fields: ALFWorld (2020), a text version of household tasks; WebShop (2022), a simulated shopping site; HotpotQA (2018), multi-hop questions answered with a search tool. They were small, synthetic and quickly saturated by ReAct-style agents (Chapter 1). In 2023 a new generation tried to capture real work. AgentBench (Liu and colleagues) collected eight environments, from operating-system shells and databases to games, and found a large gap between proprietary and open models[1]. WebArena (Zhou and colleagues) built self-hosted copies of real kinds of websites (shopping, forums, code hosting, maps) and 812 tasks on them; the best GPT-4 agent succeeded on 14.4%, humans on 78%[2]. GAIA (Mialon and colleagues) posed 466 questions that are easy for people with a browser and tools but require many steps; humans answered 92% and GPT-4 with plugins 15%[3].

The benchmark that most shaped agent development was SWE-bench (Jimenez and colleagues, 2023): 2,294 real GitHub issues from 12 Python repositories, each paired with the tests that the actual fix made pass[4]. An agent is given the repository and the issue and must produce a patch; the patch succeeds if the "fail-to-pass" tests now pass and the existing tests still do. At release, the best system (Claude 2 with retrieval) solved 1.96%. Because many original tasks turned out to be underspecified or to have tests that rejected valid fixes, OpenAI released SWE-bench Verified in August 2024, 500 tasks screened by professional developers, on which GPT-4o then scored 33.2%. Scores on Verified rose above 70% during 2025, and the benchmark became the standard yardstick for coding agents, with all the risks of a yardstick that everyone optimises for (§19.5). In 2024 two further benchmarks targeted dimensions the others missed. τ-bench (Yao and colleagues) simulated customer-service conversations in retail and airline domains, with a model playing the user, a database, and a policy document, and scored the final database state; it introduced pass\(^k\) to measure consistency, and found even the best function-calling agents solved under half the retail tasks once, and under a quarter on all eight of eight tries[5]. OSWorld (Xie and colleagues) put agents in real desktop operating systems with 369 tasks across applications; humans succeeded on 72%, the best model on 12%[6].

The table shows a pattern worth noticing: every benchmark launched with a large gap between models and people, and every gap narrowed fast. A benchmark is useful while it discriminates between the systems being compared, and the useful life of agent benchmarks has been one to two years. This drove two responses. One is continual renewal: benchmarks with fresh tasks collected after models' training cut-offs, and harder successors. The other is a different kind of measure. METR's time-horizon metric (Kwa and colleagues, 2025) measures the length of tasks, in the time a skilled human professional needs for them, that an agent completes with 50% probability; across models from 2019 to 2025 this horizon doubled roughly every seven months, reaching about an hour for the best models of early 2025[7]. A horizon does not saturate in the way a percentage does, and it connects directly to the compounding arithmetic of Chapter 1: an agent's per-step reliability determines how long a task it can finish.

Figure 19.1 organises the benchmarks along the two axes that determine how an evaluation is built: what kind of environment the agent acts in, and how success is decided. The cheapest and most trustworthy evaluations sit at the top left (a deterministic environment, a programmatic check of the end state); the most realistic sit at the bottom right (an open environment, judgement of quality), and they are the hardest to trust.

Figure 19.1 also explains why the public benchmarks are concentrated in the top row: a programmatic check is the only way to grade thousands of runs cheaply and without dispute. That concentration has a cost, since it rewards behaviours that programs can check and is silent about the rest, such as whether the patch is maintainable, whether the customer was treated well, or whether the report's reasoning is sound. An organisation evaluating its own agent will usually need all three rows, and the lower rows need the tools of §19.6. Before that, we need the metrics that turn runs into numbers, and the statistics that say how far to trust them.

19.3 Success, capability and reliability

Let task \(i\) of an evaluation set of \(m\) tasks have success probability \(p_i\) for the agent being measured: the probability that one run succeeds. Everything we report is a functional of the distribution of the \(p_i\). The success rate is their mean, \(\bar p=\frac1m\sum_ip_i\). pass@\(k\), the probability that at least one of \(k\) independent runs succeeds, averaged over tasks, is \(\frac1m\sum_i\big(1-(1-p_i)^k\big)\); it measures capability, what the agent can do given retries and a way to recognise success, and it is the right metric when a verifier can pick the successful run (§6.2). pass\(^k\), the probability that all \(k\) runs succeed, is \(\frac1m\sum_ip_i^k\); it measures reliability, what a user who runs the agent repeatedly experiences, and it is the right metric when there is no verifier and every run is used, as for the support agent answering customers.

The two metrics diverge, and the way they diverge depends on how success is spread across tasks, not just on its mean. Figure 19.2 shows three agents with the same success rate of 60%. In the first, every task has \(p_i=0.6\): the agent is a coin with bias 0.6 on everything. In the second, 60% of tasks are always solved and 40% never: the agent is deterministic. In the third, a realistic mixture, 30% of tasks are nearly always solved (\(p=0.95\)), 40% are solved 60% of the time, and 30% are solved a quarter of the time. Their pass@1 is identical; their pass\(^8\) ranges from 1.7% to 60%.

Figure 19.2 has a practical moral: a single success rate does not tell a product owner what users will experience. Two agents with the same pass@1 can differ by an order of magnitude in how often a user who asks three times gets three right answers, and the difference comes from the per-task distribution, which only repeated runs reveal. By Jensen's inequality \(\frac1m\sum p_i^k\ge\bar p^{\,k}\), so the uniform agent is the worst case for reliability at a given mean; the gap above it measures how concentrated, and therefore how predictable, the agent's successes are. τ-bench's finding that pass\(^8\) fell below a quarter of pass\(^1\) for strong models of 2024 was the observation that their successes were closer to the uniform curve than to the deterministic one.

Estimating these quantities needs care. Given \(n\) runs on a task with \(c\) successes, the obvious estimates \(1-(1-c/n)^k\) and \((c/n)^k\) are biased, because they are non-linear functions of an estimate. Chen and colleagues (2021), introducing the Codex model, gave the unbiased estimator of pass@\(k\) from \(n\ge k\) samples: the probability that a random subset of \(k\) of the \(n\) runs contains at least one success[8]. The same argument gives pass\(^k\):

\[ \widehat{\text{pass@}k}_i=1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}},\qquad \widehat{\text{pass}^k}_i=\frac{\binom{c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}, \]

averaged over tasks. Each is unbiased because the expectation of a hypergeometric indicator over subsets of i.i.d. Bernoulli runs equals the probability for \(k\) fresh runs.

Worked example — estimating pass@3 and pass^3 from ten runs

The support agent is run \(n=10\) times on one scenario and succeeds \(c=6\) times.

pass@3. \(1-\binom{4}{3}/\binom{10}{3}=1-4/120=0.967\). The plug-in estimate \(1-0.4^3=0.936\) is lower; both say three tries almost always include a success.

pass\(^3\). \(\binom{6}{3}/\binom{10}{3}=20/120=0.167\). The plug-in \(0.6^3=0.216\) overstates reliability by 30% relative: squaring and cubing a noisy estimate inflates it, since \(\mathbb E[\hat p^3]>p^3\).

Across the set. On 200 scenarios with ten runs each, average the per-task estimates. If 30% of scenarios have \(c=10\), the task-level pass\(^3\) of those is 1, and the overall figure is dominated by how many scenarios are solved every time, which is the quantity a support manager cares about.

Cost. Ten runs of 200 scenarios is 2,000 conversations; at about $0.085 each on the frontier model (§17.8) that is $170, a small price for knowing the agent's reliability rather than guessing it from one run.

19.4 How many tasks? Statistics for small evaluation sets

An evaluation set is a sample of tasks from the population we care about, and the success rate measured on it is an estimate. With \(m\) tasks, each run once, and the true rate \(\bar p\), the number of successes is approximately binomial, so the standard error of the estimate \(\hat p\) is about \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/m}\) and a 95% confidence interval is \(\hat p\pm1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/m}\). Figure 19.3 plots the half-width of this interval. With SWE-bench Verified's 500 tasks and a success rate near 50%, it is \(\pm4.4\) percentage points; with a 100-task internal evaluation, \(\pm9.8\) points. Differences of two or three points between agents, which are often reported and celebrated, are well inside these intervals.

The binomial interval assumes one run per task. An agent is stochastic, so a second source of variation is which runs happened to succeed. Write the variance of \(p_i\) across tasks as \(\sigma^2_{\text{task}}\) and the average within-task Bernoulli variance as \(\overline{p(1-p)}\); for one run per task they add to \(\bar p(1-\bar p)\). With \(n\) runs per task and the task means averaged,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=\frac1m\Big(\sigma^2_{\text{task}}+\frac{\overline{p(1-p)}}{n}\Big). \]

Repeated runs shrink only the second term. If, at \(\bar p=0.5\), the tasks contribute \(\sigma^2_{\text{task}}=0.15\) and the runs \(0.10\), then with \(m=500\) the standard error is 2.24 points with one run per task, 1.84 with five, and never below 1.73: repeated runs are worth doing to measure reliability (§19.3) but are a poor way to narrow the interval on the success rate, for which only more tasks help. The interval is also about the tasks: it says how well the success rate on the population from which the tasks were drawn is pinned down, and nothing about other populations. An agent's rate on SWE-bench's twelve repositories is a noisy estimate of its rate on those repositories, and a biased one for ours.

Comparisons between two agents should exploit the fact that they were run on the same tasks. Tasks vary greatly in difficulty, so most of the variation in each agent's score is shared, and a paired analysis removes it. Figure 19.4 shows the structure: of \(m\) tasks, both agents solve some, both fail some, and the evidence for a difference lies entirely in the discordant tasks, those solved by one agent and not the other. McNemar's test compares the two discordant counts \(b\) (only A succeeds) and \(c\) (only B succeeds): under the null hypothesis of no difference, each discordant task is equally likely to fall either way, so \(z=(b-c)/\sqrt{b+c}\) is approximately standard normal.

Worked example — is the new coding agent better?

On SWE-bench Verified, a new version A solves 260 of 500 tasks (52%), the old version B 245 (49%). Agreements: both solve 220, neither 215; A alone solves 40, B alone 25.

Unpaired comparison. Standard error of the difference \(\sqrt{0.52\cdot0.48/500+0.49\cdot0.51/500}=0.0316\); \(z=0.03/0.0316=0.95\), far from significant.

Paired comparison. \(z=(40-25)/\sqrt{65}=1.86\), two-sided \(p\approx0.06\). The paired analysis is about twice as sensitive, but three points on 500 tasks is still only suggestive.

Discordance from noise. If the agents are stochastic, some discordant tasks are discordant only by chance: running A twice would itself produce discordance with itself. Running each agent three times and comparing per-task success fractions with a paired test on the means separates a real difference from run-to-run noise.

Decision. Ship if the change is cheap and has no regressions elsewhere; do not publish a claim of improvement. To confirm a three-point difference, see Drill 19.1.

Drill 19.1 — how many tasks to detect three points?

Two agents with success rates near 50% differ by 3 points. How many tasks are needed for 80% power at the 5% level, (a) with unpaired comparison, (b) with a paired comparison where 13% of tasks are discordant?

We need the standard error of the difference to be at most \(0.03/(1.96+0.84)=0.0107\). (a) Unpaired: \(\sqrt{2\times0.25/m}\le0.0107\Rightarrow m\ge0.5/0.0107^2\approx4{,}360\) tasks. (b) Paired: the variance of the difference is about (discordant fraction)\(/m=0.13/m\), so \(m\ge0.13/0.0107^2\approx1{,}130\) tasks. Even paired, a reliable three-point comparison needs more than twice SWE-bench Verified's size; most such claims in practice are underpowered.

Three more statistical cautions apply to agent evaluations in particular. Clustering: tasks from the same repository, website or scenario template are correlated, so the effective number of independent tasks is smaller than \(m\), and intervals should be computed by resampling clusters, not tasks. Multiple comparisons: a team that tries twenty prompt variants and reports the best one has run twenty tests, and the best of twenty noisy estimates is biased upwards; a final comparison on a held-out set, used once, removes the bias. Small proportions: for success rates near 0 or 1, or for few tasks, the normal approximation fails, and the Wilson interval (Wilson, 1927) is the standard replacement[9]. Each is routine in clinical statistics; agent evaluation is still learning them.

19.5 Threats to validity

A confidence interval quantifies sampling error; it says nothing about whether the number measures what we think it measures. Agent benchmarks are exposed to five threats to validity that sampling statistics cannot detect, and each has caused published results to mislead.

Contamination. Public benchmarks are built from public data: GitHub issues and their fixes, websites, questions posted online. A model trained on a crawl that includes a repository's history may have seen the fix for a SWE-bench issue, so solving the task measures recall, not ability. Contamination is hard to rule out for any benchmark built before a model's training cut-off. The defences are benchmarks of tasks created after the cut-off (several continually refreshed variants of SWE-bench appeared in 2025 for this reason), canary strings that let model developers filter benchmark data from training, and checks for memorisation, such as whether the agent produces the exact upstream patch, comments included, or performs suspiciously better on older tasks than newer ones of similar difficulty.

Broken tasks. Some tasks are unsolvable as specified (the issue is ambiguous, the environment is missing a dependency), and some checkers are wrong (tests that reject valid solutions, answer keys with errors). Broken tasks put a ceiling below 100% and add noise to every comparison; the screening that produced SWE-bench Verified found a large fraction of the original tasks problematic. Zhu and colleagues (2025) audited widely used agent benchmarks against a checklist of good practice and found problems in most of them, including tasks that a trivial agent could pass and checkers that accepted wrong outcomes, which inflated or deflated reported scores by large relative amounts[10]. The remedy is the evaluator's equivalent of the verifier analysis of §6.2: measure the checker's false-positive and false-negative rates on a sample of runs reviewed by people, fix or remove broken tasks, and always run a trivial baseline (an agent that does nothing, or returns a fixed answer) to see what it scores.

Shortcut solutions. An agent can pass a checker without solving the task in the intended way: special-casing tests, reading an answer from an accessible file, exploiting a website bug. This is reward hacking (§18.7) appearing in evaluation rather than training, and an agent trained by RL against similar checkers is more likely to do it. Evaluation harnesses should keep checkers and answers out of the agent's reach, and a sample of successful trajectories should be read, by a person or a monitoring model, to confirm that successes are real.

Harness effects. An agent's score depends on the scaffold as much as the model: the tools, the prompts, the context management, the number of steps allowed, whether retries or a verifier are used. Published results that compare models in different harnesses compare harnesses. A fair comparison of models holds the harness fixed; a fair comparison of agents (model plus harness) must say what each was allowed: step limits, wall-clock limits, cost, number of attempts, and whether any selection among attempts used information unavailable at deployment, such as the hidden tests.

Saturation and overfitting to the benchmark. When a benchmark is the field's target, systems are tuned to it: prompts, tools and even training data shaped around its tasks. Scores rise faster than ability on the underlying problem, a collective form of the multiple-comparisons bias of §19.4. The measure that matters for any one organisation is performance on its own tasks, and public benchmarks are best used to shortlist systems, not to choose among them. Kapoor and colleagues (2024), surveying agent benchmarks, added a related warning: many leaderboards report accuracy without cost, and simple baselines, such as retrying a strong model several times, matched elaborate agent designs at a fraction of the cost on some of them[11]. We return to cost in §19.8.

19.6 Model judges

For the bottom row of Figure 19.1, success is a matter of judgement: is the research report accurate, well organised and properly cited; did the support agent handle the angry customer well? People can judge, but slowly and expensively, and the judgements must be repeated whenever the agent changes. The alternative, a language model that grades outputs against a rubric, became standard in 2023 after Zheng and colleagues showed, with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, that GPT-4's pairwise judgements of chat responses agreed with human experts' over 80% of the time, about as often as the experts agreed with each other[12]. A model judge is cheap, fast and consistent, and it is a verifier in the sense of Chapter 6, with error rates that must be measured.

Judges have characteristic biases, all documented in the 2023–2024 literature. Position bias: in pairwise comparisons, a preference for whichever response is shown first (or second), strong enough in early judges to flip a large share of verdicts when the order was swapped (Wang and colleagues, 2023)[13]. Verbosity bias: a preference for longer responses, independent of quality. Self-preference: a tendency to rate outputs from the judge's own model family higher. And surface plausibility: a confident, well-formatted wrong answer is often rated above a hesitant right one, which matters most where the judge cannot check facts. For agents, a further problem is that the judge sees a long trajectory and must decide what happened in it; judges skim, like people, and miss the step where the agent quietly changed the wrong booking.

Figure 19.5 shows a judging pipeline built to control these biases. The rubric decomposes quality into specific, checkable criteria ("every claim has a citation", "the citation supports the claim", "the refund amount matches policy"), each judged separately, rather than asking for an overall score. Pairwise comparisons are run in both orders and counted only when consistent. The judge is given the evidence it needs, such as the source documents, the policy, or the final database state, so that it checks rather than guesses; and where a criterion can be checked by code (did the agent call the refund tool with the right amount?), code checks it. Finally, the judge is calibrated: on a few hundred outputs labelled by people, its false-positive and false-negative rates are measured per criterion.

Calibration matters because a judge's errors bias the measured success rate, and they can bias comparisons in either direction. If the judge accepts a failure with probability \(\epsilon_+\) and rejects a success with probability \(\epsilon_-\), then the rate of accepted outputs is \(q=p(1-\epsilon_-)+(1-p)\epsilon_+\), and solving for the true rate gives the correction known in epidemiology as the Rogan–Gladen estimator:

\[ \hat p=\frac{q-\epsilon_+}{1-\epsilon_--\epsilon_+}, \]

valid when \(\epsilon_-+\epsilon_+<1\), with the uncertainty of \(\epsilon_\pm\) adding to the interval. The correction is only as good as the calibration set: error rates measured on one agent's outputs may not hold for another's, and that is exactly the case where comparisons go wrong.

Worked example — a judge that favours the verbose agent

A judge grades research reports. On 300 human-labelled reports from agent A, it accepts 10% of bad reports (\(\epsilon_+=0.10\)) and rejects 5% of good ones (\(\epsilon_-=0.05\)).

Correcting A. The judge accepts \(q_A=62\%\) of A's reports, so \(\hat p_A=(0.62-0.10)/0.85=0.612\).

Agent B, naively. B, which writes longer reports, gets \(q_B=70\%\). With A's error rates, \(\hat p_B=(0.70-0.10)/0.85=0.706\): B looks ten points better.

Agent B, calibrated. Labelling 300 of B's reports shows the judge accepts 25% of B's bad reports, because their length makes them look thorough: \(\epsilon_+=0.25\), \(\epsilon_-=0.05\). Then \(\hat p_B=(0.70-0.25)/0.70=0.643\). B is still better, by three points rather than ten, and within noise for a few hundred reports.

Lesson. When the systems being compared differ in style, calibrate the judge on each, or the comparison measures the judge's taste.

For agents, a judge can itself be an agent. Agent-as-a-judge (Zhuge and colleagues, 2024) gave the judging model tools to inspect the artefacts an agent produced, reading files, running code, checking intermediate requirements, and found its verdicts on development tasks much closer to human experts' than a judge that only read the final output[14]. The principle generalises: the more of the evidence a judge can check rather than infer, the more it behaves like a verifier and the less like a reader of impressions.

Drill 19.2 — when is the correction impossible?

A judge has \(\epsilon_+=0.4\) and \(\epsilon_-=0.5\) on some criterion. It accepts 45% of an agent's outputs. What can we conclude?

\(1-\epsilon_--\epsilon_+=0.1\), so \(\hat p=(0.45-0.4)/0.1=0.5\), but the estimate's sensitivity is ten times the measured rate's: an error of one point in \(q\) moves \(\hat p\) by ten. With \(\epsilon_-+\epsilon_+=1\) the judge's verdict would be independent of the truth and nothing could be concluded. A judge this poor on a criterion should be replaced (by a stronger judge, evidence, decomposition or people), not corrected.

19.7 Building an evaluation for your own agent

Public benchmarks shortlist systems; an organisation's own evaluation decides. Building one is an engineering project with a recognisable shape, shown in Figure 19.6, and the work is front-loaded: once the task suite, environments and graders exist, every later change to the agent can be measured in hours.

Tasks. The suite should be a sample of the real distribution of work, drawn from production traces (anonymised), supplemented by tasks written by domain experts for rare but important cases, by every failure observed in production, and by adversarial tasks (Chapter 20). Each task needs a definition of success precise enough to grade; writing it is often where a team discovers that it had not agreed what success meant. A suite of a few hundred tasks catches large regressions; detecting small improvements needs more (Drill 19.1), so suites grow over time. A held-out portion, never used during development, guards against the overfitting of §19.4.

Environments. Each run must start from the same state. For coding agents this means container images pinned to a commit and dependency versions; for web agents, self-hosted snapshots or recorded sites; for support agents, a seeded database and a user simulator. When the agent depends on external services, their responses can be recorded once and replayed, which makes runs deterministic and cheap but cannot test how the agent reacts to responses that were not recorded. A user simulator is itself a model, whose behaviour must be checked against real users: an evaluation of a support agent against a too-cooperative simulated customer will overestimate it.

Graders. Prefer, in order: code checks of end states (database contents, test results, files), code checks of trajectories (policy rules: was the refund tool called only after the eligibility check?), calibrated judges for the rest, and people for calibration and for the highest-stakes criteria. Report each criterion separately as well as an aggregate, because aggregates hide trade-offs.

Offline and online. Offline evaluation, on the suite, gates releases: a change ships if it shows no significant regression on any critical criterion and the paired comparison of §19.4 supports any claimed improvement. Online evaluation measures what the suite cannot: real users, real distribution shifts, long-term effects. It uses A/B tests on live traffic for decisions that can wait for statistical power, sampled human review of live trajectories, and proxy signals (escalations, user corrections, repeat contacts). Production failures found online become new suite tasks, closing the loop of Figure 19.6.

19.8 Error analysis: reading what went wrong

A success rate says how often an agent fails; it does not say why, and the why is what tells a team what to change. The most productive evaluation activity for an agent in development is therefore not computing metrics but reading failed trajectories and classifying them. The practice is old (it is how machine-translation and speech-recognition systems were improved for decades), and it matters more for agents because their failures are so varied: the same 40% failure rate can be mostly retrieval failures, mostly tool-format errors, mostly premature stopping, or mostly tasks that were broken, and each calls for a different fix.

The procedure is simple. Sample failed runs, say fifty to a hundred, read each, and record the earliest step at which the trajectory went irrecoverably wrong and the kind of error it was. The categories emerge from the reading rather than being fixed in advance, and they are usually specific to the agent: "searched the wrong file", "misread the error message", "edited without running the tests", "declared success without verification", "policy misapplied", "task broken". Counting them gives a failure taxonomy, and multiplying each category's share by an estimate of how fixable it is, and by how much fixing it would cost, gives a priority list. A model can help, by proposing a category for each trajectory once a person has defined the categories on an initial sample, which lets the analysis scale to thousands of runs; the person's sample remains the check on the model's labelling.

The earliest-error rule matters because of compounding (Chapter 2). A trajectory that misreads the issue at step 3 then fails in many ways downstream, and recording the downstream symptom ("the tests failed") rather than the cause ("misread the issue") points at the wrong fix. The rule also connects error analysis to the step-level credit assignment of §17.7 and §18.5: the step where a trajectory went wrong is exactly where a step-level preference or a process reward would place its signal, so a good failure taxonomy is also the specification for training data.

Worked example — where to spend the next month

The coding agent fails 48% of the internal suite. A reading of 80 failures gives: 30 localisation errors (edited the wrong place), 18 premature success claims (stopped before running the tests), 14 environment problems (dependency missing, flaky test), 10 misread requirements, 8 other.

Shares of all tasks. Multiplying by the 48% failure rate: localisation 18 points, premature stopping 10.8, environment 8.4, requirements 6, other 4.8.

Fixability. Premature stopping can be largely removed by a harness rule (run the tests before submitting, a gate of §16.2): suppose 80% fixable, about 8.6 points for a week's work. Environment problems are evaluation bugs, not agent failures: fixing them raises the measured rate by up to 8.4 points without changing the agent, and must be done first so that later comparisons are honest. Localisation, the largest category, needs better retrieval or search (Chapter 9): perhaps a third fixable in a month, 6 points.

Order. Fix the environment (honest measurement), then the stopping rule (cheap and large), then localisation. Without the reading, the team might have spent the month on a new model.

19.9 Reporting cost, latency and safety alongside success

An agent that solves 2% more tasks at three times the cost is not better for most purposes, and an evaluation that reports only success invites exactly that kind of "improvement". A complete report gives, for each system and with intervals: success rate and pass\(^k\) for the relevant \(k\); cost per task, as a distribution, not just a mean, since a long tail of expensive runs matters for budgets; latency, also as a distribution; the rate of policy violations and unsafe actions, which for many deployments is a constraint rather than a quantity to trade; and the conditions (harness, step and time limits, attempts, model versions).

With several metrics, comparisons become questions about a Pareto frontier: a system is dominated if another is at least as good on every metric and better on one, and only undominated systems deserve consideration. Choosing among them needs the deployment's own exchange rates, such as the value of a resolved conversation, the cost of an escalation and the price of a minute of a user's waiting, which Chapter 21 develops. The evaluation's job is to measure each axis well enough that the choice is not decided by noise; the arithmetic of this chapter says how much measurement that takes, and it is usually more than teams expect.

Drill 19.3 — success per dollar

Agent A resolves 64% of support tasks at $0.09 per task; agent B resolves 66% at $0.30 (it uses a reasoning model at every turn). An unresolved task escalates to a person at $4. Which is cheaper per task in total, and how large would B's advantage need to be to break even?

A: \(0.09+0.36\times4=\$1.53\). B: \(0.30+0.34\times4=\$1.66\). A is cheaper despite resolving fewer. B breaks even when \(0.30+(1-p_B)\times4=1.53\), i.e. \(p_B=0.6925\): B would need about 69.3% resolution, 5.3 points above A. And the 2-point difference measured on, say, 300 tasks is far inside the noise of Figure 19.3, so the evaluation could not have established it anyway.

Looking ahead

An evaluation measures what an agent does on the tasks we thought to give it. The tasks that matter most for safety are the ones nobody thought of, or ones written by someone who wants the agent to fail: a web page that instructs the research agent to send the user's files elsewhere, a customer who talks the support agent into a refund the policy forbids, a repository whose README tells the coding agent to disable the tests. Measuring an agent under such adversarial pressure, and building agents that withstand it, is the subject of Chapter 20. It treats prompt injection as the defining security problem of agents, the sandboxing and least-privilege designs that limit the damage when defences fail, architectures such as the dual-LLM pattern and CaMeL that separate trusted instructions from untrusted data, and the evaluations that probe whether agents pursue goals their designers did not intend.

Summary

  • Agent evaluation needs reproducible environments, end-state checkers, repeated runs and statistics; runs are costly, so task sets are small and statistics matter.
  • Benchmarks evolved from synthetic environments to real work: AgentBench, WebArena (GPT-4 14% vs humans 78%), GAIA (15% vs 92%) and SWE-bench (2%) in 2023; SWE-bench Verified, τ-bench and OSWorld in 2024; METR's time horizon, doubling about every seven months. Each benchmark discriminates for one to two years.
  • pass@\(k\) (capability) and pass\(^k\) (reliability) are means of \(1-(1-p_i)^k\) and \(p_i^k\) over tasks; agents with the same success rate can differ greatly in pass\(^k\). Use the unbiased estimators \(1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom nk\) and \(\binom ck/\binom nk\).
  • The 95% half-width of a success rate is \(1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/m}\): ±4.4 points at 500 tasks and 50%. Repeated runs reduce only within-task variance; more tasks are what narrow the interval.
  • Compare agents with paired tests on the same tasks (McNemar: only discordant tasks carry evidence); a three-point difference needs over a thousand tasks even when paired. Watch clustering and multiple comparisons; use Wilson intervals for extreme rates.
  • Threats to validity: contamination, broken tasks and checkers, shortcut solutions, harness differences, and benchmark overfitting. Run trivial baselines and read successful trajectories.
  • Model judges agree with experts about as often as experts agree with each other on chat, but have position, verbosity and self-preference biases. Decompose rubrics, check by code where possible, give judges evidence, judge both orders, and calibrate: \(\hat p=(q-\epsilon_+)/(1-\epsilon_--\epsilon_+)\), with error rates measured per system.
  • Own evaluations: tasks from traces, experts and failures; reproducible environments; graders in the order code, judge, human; offline release gates and online A/B tests and review; report cost, latency and violations with success, and choose on the Pareto frontier.

Interview questions

Answer out loud first, then reveal the model answer and rate yourself.

Warm-up What is the difference between pass@k and pass^k, and when do you use each?

pass@k: probability at least one of k runs succeeds, mean of \(1-(1-p_i)^k\); capability given retries and a way to pick the success (a verifier). pass^k: probability all k runs succeed, mean of \(p_i^k\); reliability as experienced by users when every run counts. A support agent needs pass^k; a coding agent with tests can use pass@k.

Warm-up Name the main agent benchmarks and what each measures.

SWE-bench / Verified: fixing real GitHub issues, graded by tests. WebArena: tasks on self-hosted websites, end-state checks. GAIA: multi-step assistant questions with exact answers. τ-bench: tool-using customer service with simulated users, database state, pass^k. OSWorld: real desktop tasks, scripted checks. AgentBench: eight mixed environments. METR time horizon: task length (human time) at 50% success.

Warm-up Why is a single run per task insufficient?

Agents are stochastic: one run samples each task's success once. It gives an unbiased success rate but no information about consistency (pass^k), confuses run-to-run noise with differences between agents, and hides which tasks are reliably solved. Use several runs for reliability and for separating noise from real differences, and more tasks for narrower intervals.

Core Two agents score 52% and 49% on 500 tasks. Is one better?

Not established. Unpaired: SE of difference ≈ 3.2 points, z ≈ 0.95. Paired (McNemar) with, say, 40 vs 25 discordant tasks: z = 15/√65 ≈ 1.86, p ≈ 0.06. Suggestive at best. To confirm 3 points needs ~1,100 tasks paired (13% discordance) or ~4,400 unpaired; also run multiple seeds to separate noise, check clustering, and confirm on held-out tasks.

Core Give the unbiased estimators of pass@k and pass^k and explain them.

With n ≥ k runs and c successes on a task: pass@k = \(1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom nk\), the probability a random k-subset contains a success; pass^k = \(\binom ck/\binom nk\), the probability a random k-subset is all successes. Average over tasks. Unbiased because subsets of i.i.d. runs are distributed like fresh runs; plug-ins like \((c/n)^k\) are biased upward by convexity.

Core How do repeated runs and more tasks each affect the precision of a success rate?

\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=(\sigma^2_{\text{task}}+\overline{p(1-p)}/n)/m\). More runs n shrink only within-task variance; more tasks m shrink both. With between-task variance 0.15 and within 0.10 at m=500, SE goes 2.24 → 1.84 (n=5) → 1.73 (n→∞). So use runs to measure reliability, tasks to narrow intervals.

Core What threats to validity would you check before trusting a benchmark score?

Contamination (tasks or fixes in training data; check task dates, exact-patch reproduction, old-vs-new gap); broken tasks and checkers (human audit of a sample, trivial-agent baseline); shortcut solutions (read successful trajectories, protect checkers); harness differences (fixed scaffold, limits, attempts, selection using hidden information); overfitting from repeated tuning (held-out set). Then statistics.

Core How do you use an LLM judge responsibly?

Decompose into specific criteria; use code where a criterion is checkable; give the judge evidence (sources, policy, final state); for pairwise, judge both orders; control length; avoid same-family judges where self-preference matters; calibrate on human labels to get ε+ and ε− per criterion and per system; correct rates with \((q-\epsilon_+)/(1-\epsilon_--\epsilon_+)\); re-calibrate when systems or judges change; audit samples.

Core Derive the judge-error correction.

Observed acceptance \(q=p(1-\epsilon_-)+(1-p)\epsilon_+=\epsilon_++p(1-\epsilon_--\epsilon_+)\). Solve: \(p=(q-\epsilon_+)/(1-\epsilon_--\epsilon_+)\), defined for \(\epsilon_-+\epsilon_+<1\). Sensitivity \(1/(1-\epsilon_--\epsilon_+)\) amplifies noise in q and in the estimated error rates; a judge near \(\epsilon_-+\epsilon_+=1\) is uninformative and should be replaced, not corrected.

Core Why can the same success rate hide very different user experiences?

pass^k is the mean of \(p_i^k\), which depends on how success is distributed across tasks. 60% success can be 60% of tasks always solved (pass^8 = 60%) or every task at 60% (pass^8 = 1.7%). Users who retry, or who face the same task type repeatedly, experience pass^k. Report per-task distributions or pass^k for relevant k, from multiple runs.

Deep How would you detect and handle contamination in a coding benchmark?

Compare performance on tasks before vs after the model's training cut-off at matched difficulty; check whether patches reproduce upstream code verbatim (identifiers, comments); probe the model for memorised issue text or fixes; use canary strings. Handle by using continually refreshed tasks created after the cut-off, private held-out tasks, and reporting both. Treat large old-vs-new gaps as contamination until shown otherwise.

Deep How do you evaluate an agent whose environment includes external services you cannot reset?

Record and replay responses for determinism (cheap, but cannot test unrecorded behaviour); build simulators or sandboxed clones of the services (validated against real behaviour); use staging environments with seeded data; and complement with online evaluation (shadow mode, canaries, A/B tests, sampled review). Record environment versions with results, and treat drift as a source of variance between runs.

Deep What does METR's time-horizon metric capture that percentages do not?

It measures the task length (human time) at which success is 50%, fitting success against log task length across a wide range of tasks. It does not saturate like a percentage on a fixed set, it is comparable across benchmark generations, and it connects to compounding reliability: longer horizons need higher per-step reliability. Its observed doubling (~7 months, 2019–2025) is a trend in capability; caveats include task selection, human-time estimates and messiness of real tasks.

Design Design the evaluation for the airline support agent.

Tasks: scenarios sampled from anonymised conversations by intent, plus expert-written policy edge cases, past failures and adversarial customers. Environment: seeded booking database, policy, user simulator validated against real transcripts. Graders: code checks of the final database state and policy rules on the trajectory (eligibility before refund), calibrated judge for tone and explanation, human audit sample. Metrics: resolution, pass^k (k=3–8 runs per scenario), violations, escalations, cost and latency distributions, with paired comparisons and held-out set. Online: A/B with escalation and repeat-contact rates, sampled review; new failures become scenarios.

Design How would you evaluate the research agent's reports?

Questions with expert reference notes; graders: automatic citation checks (does each quoted source exist and contain the claim), coverage checks against reference key points, calibrated judges per criterion (accuracy given sources, organisation, hedging), pairwise comparisons in both orders with length control, experts on a sample to calibrate. Report per criterion with intervals, cost and time per report, and fabricated-citation rate as a hard constraint.

Design A product team wants a leaderboard to pick an agent vendor. What do you build?

A private task suite from the team's own work (not public benchmarks), fixed harness conditions per vendor stated explicitly (limits, attempts, tools), multiple runs per task, metrics: success with intervals, pass^k, cost and latency distributions, violation rates; paired comparisons; trivial baselines; human audit of successes for shortcuts; held-out tasks refreshed periodically to avoid tuning to the suite; a Pareto view with the team's exchange rates for the final choice.

Core How many tasks would you put in a regression suite, and why?

Depends on the effect sizes you need to detect: a few hundred tasks catch large regressions (±4–7 points at 95%); detecting 3-point changes needs ~1,000+ paired. Prioritise critical behaviours with dedicated subsets and hard constraints (zero violations), include past failures, hold out a portion, and grow the suite as failures appear. Balance cost: each run of the suite costs tokens and sandbox time.
