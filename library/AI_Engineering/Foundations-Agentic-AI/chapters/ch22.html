Part VII · Reliability, Safety and Production — Chapter 22

The Agent System Design Interview

The system-design round asks a candidate to design an agent for an open-ended problem in forty-five minutes, out loud, under questioning. It tests exactly what this book has tried to teach: turning a vague goal into a quantified design, choosing the least autonomy that works, anticipating failure, and knowing how to measure and secure the result. This chapter gives a method for the round, five worked case studies built from the book's running examples and others, and the rubric by which answers are judged.

Why this chapter

Twenty-one chapters have built up a body of technique: the reliability arithmetic of Chapters 1–2, reasoning and search (Part II), tools, retrieval, code and protocols (Part III), memory and long horizons (Part IV), multi-agent systems and architectures (Part V), training (Part VI), and evaluation, security and production (Part VII). An interview does not test whether a candidate can recite any of it. It tests whether they can use it, quickly and in the right order, on a problem they have not seen, and whether their reasoning is quantitative where it should be and humble where it must be. That is a skill of its own, and like any skill it improves with a method and with practice on worked examples.

The chapter is organised for practice. §22.1 describes what interviewers look for. §22.2 gives a method for the forty-five minutes and the back-of-envelope numbers to carry into the room. §22.3–§22.7 work five case studies in the form an interview answer would take, each emphasising a different part of the book: a coding agent for a large repository, the airline support agent, the research agent, a personal email-and-calendar assistant, and a data-analysis agent over a company warehouse. §22.8 collects the common mistakes and how they are scored. The book's final "Looking ahead" then names the open problems that the field, and the reader, will work on next.

Learning objectives
  1. Describe what an agent system-design interview assesses and how answers are scored.
  2. Apply a structured method: clarify, estimate, baseline, deepen, evaluate, secure, operate, evolve.
  3. Carry and use a set of reference numbers for cost, latency, reliability and capacity.
  4. Design, quantify and defend agents for coding, customer support, research, personal assistance and data analysis.
  5. Recognise and avoid the common failure patterns of design answers.

This chapter uses the whole book; the most frequently cited results are the reliability law \(\rho^H\) (§1.8, §2.6), the episode cost model (§2.8, §21.1), autonomy choice (§16.2), verifiers (Chapter 6), retrieval (Chapter 9), sandboxes (§10.2), long-horizon techniques (Chapter 14), evaluation statistics (§19.4), the trifecta and architectural defences (§12.5, §20.4), and production economics (Chapter 21).

22.1 What the round assesses

A system-design round for an agent role typically opens with a one-sentence prompt, "design an agent that resolves customer refund requests", "design a coding agent that fixes failing tests in our monorepo", and leaves the rest to the candidate. The interviewer plays the role of a product owner and a sceptical senior engineer at once: they will answer questions about requirements, and they will probe any claim that sounds unexamined. Interviewers at AI labs and large technology companies differ in emphasis, but their rubrics converge on six dimensions, and a strong answer touches all of them in proportion to the problem.

Problem framing: does the candidate establish who the users are, what a task is, what success means and how it is measured, and what constraints (latency, cost, risk, data access) apply, before designing? Quantitative reasoning: do they estimate volume, tokens, cost, latency, concurrency and reliability, and let the numbers drive choices? Architecture and autonomy: do they choose the least autonomy that meets the need (§16.2), decompose the system into components with clear responsibilities, and justify the model, tools, context and memory choices? Reliability and failure modes: do they anticipate how the agent fails (compounding errors, tool failures, premature stopping, hallucinated success) and build verification and recovery into the design? Evaluation and iteration: do they say how they would know it works, with what data, metrics and statistics, and how they would improve it? Safety, security and operations: do they identify the threat model, bound the blast radius, and plan for observability, rollout and human oversight?

Two things distinguish a strong answer from an adequate one across all six. The first is prioritisation: a strong candidate spends time where the problem's difficulty lies (security for the email assistant, long-horizon reliability for the coding agent, citation faithfulness for the research agent) and says explicitly what they are setting aside. The second is calibrated confidence: they distinguish what they know (the arithmetic), what they would measure (resolution rates, error profiles), and what they are guessing (a model's behaviour on this domain), and they design so that the guesses are tested early. Interviewers are wary of answers that name many techniques without numbers, and of answers that commit to an elaborate multi-agent design before establishing that a simpler one fails.

22.2 A method for forty-five minutes

A method keeps an answer from drifting into whichever component the candidate finds most interesting. The eight steps of Figure 22.1 take roughly the time shown; the order matters more than the timings, and an interviewer's question at any point is a signal to go deeper there.

1. Clarify. Ask who uses the agent and for what; what one task is, with an example; what success means and who decides; what the agent may do (read, write, send, spend); and the constraints (latency tolerance, budget, regulatory limits, data residency). Then restate the problem in one or two sentences, including a success metric, and confirm it with the interviewer. Five minutes here saves twenty of designing the wrong thing.

2. Estimate. Volume (tasks per day and at peak), task length (turns, tokens per turn), cost per task with caching, latency per task, concurrency by Little's law, and the reliability required. The last is often the most revealing number: if a task needs \(H\) steps and a target success rate \(T\), the per-step reliability must be \(\rho=T^{1/H}\) without recovery (Figure 22.2), which immediately shows whether the design needs verification and recovery (§2.6) or a shorter horizon. The habit of estimating before designing is borrowed from conventional system design, where back-of-envelope numbers for throughput and storage drive the architecture[1].

3. Baseline. Propose the simplest system that could meet the requirement: often a workflow with one or two model calls, or a single agent loop with a handful of tools. Say why simpler options fail if they do; practitioner guides from model developers give the same advice[2]. Draw it: boxes for components, arrows for data, a marker on every tool that writes or sends.

4. Deepen. Take the components where the difficulty lies and design them properly: the tool interfaces (§8.5), the context and its budget (Chapter 4), retrieval (Chapter 9), memory (Chapter 13), verification (Chapter 6: tests, checkers, judges), recovery and checkpoints (Chapter 14), and, only if justified, multiple agents (Chapter 15). Use the numbers from step 2: if cost per task is too high, where does it go? If latency is too high, what is on the critical path?

5. Evaluate. What is the evaluation set, where does it come from, how is success graded, how many tasks are needed for the comparisons that matter (§19.4), and how is reliability (pass\(^k\)) measured? What is the offline gate and the online signal?

6. Secure. Write the threat model in two sentences (§20.1): what untrusted input reaches the agent, and which capabilities could it trigger? Then bound the blast radius with least privilege and architecture (§20.4–§20.5).

7. Operate. Observability (traces), guardrails, human-in-the-loop placement and sizing, rollout and rollback, budgets and rate limits (Chapter 21).

8. Evolve. How does the system improve? Error analysis feeding the suite (§19.8), distillation to a cheaper model when volume justifies it (§17.8), fine-tuning or RL when a verifier exists and prompting has plateaued (Chapters 17–18), and how the design changes at ten times the scale.

22.3 Case study: a coding agent for a large repository

Prompt: "Our 2,000 engineers work in one large monorepo. Design an agent they can hand tasks to, such as fixing a failing test, making a small feature change, or doing a migration, and that comes back with a pull request."

Clarify. Tasks are well-scoped tickets of an hour or two of human work; the output is a pull request that a person reviews and merges, so the agent never merges or deploys. Success is "merged without substantial rework"; a secondary metric is reviewer time. The repository has a CI system with tests, but a full test run takes 40 minutes, while targeted tests take one to three. Engineers will delegate in the background, so a task may take tens of minutes.

Estimate. If each engineer delegates two tasks a working day, that is 4,000 tasks a day, about 400 an hour over a ten-hour day. A task is about 50 turns: reading files, searching, editing, running targeted tests. With a 15,000-token prefix (instructions, tool definitions, a repository map), 2,500 tokens added per turn and 400 output tokens per turn, the episode reads \(50\times15{,}000+2{,}500\times50\times51/2\approx3.9\) million input tokens, of which 140,000 are new. On the frontier model with caching: \(0.14\text{M}\times\$3+3.8\text{M}\times\$0.30+0.02\text{M}\times\$15\approx\$0.42+\$1.14+\$0.30=\$1.86\) per task, about $7,400 a day. The latency per turn is about \(0.5+6.7+3=10\) seconds including a typical tool, so a task takes about 8.5 minutes of model and tool time plus test runs, say 20 minutes. By Little's law, \(400/60\times20\approx133\) tasks in flight on average, so a few hundred sandboxes at peak. Reliability: at 50 steps, a 50% success rate without recovery needs \(\rho=0.986\) per step (Figure 22.2), which is plausible only with tests as a verifier inside the loop.

Value check. If half of tasks are merged and each saves an hour of engineer time worth $100, the agent produces about $200,000 of value a day for $7,400 of tokens and perhaps a similar amount of compute for sandboxes and CI. The economics are dominated by the success rate and the reviewer time, not by token prices, which tells us where to spend design effort.

Baseline and deepening. A single agent loop with read, search, edit and test tools in a sandbox is the right baseline: the steps vary by task and the environment gives rich feedback, the conditions for autonomy (§16.2). Figure 22.3 shows the design. The difficulties are localisation in a huge repository, verification, and the long horizon, and each gets a mechanism.

Localisation in a repository of millions of lines needs code search and structural navigation as tools (symbol definitions, references, ownership files), not embedding retrieval alone (§9.7); a pre-computed repository map in the prefix orients the agent, and its cost is amortised by caching. Verification uses targeted tests in the loop: the agent finds and runs the tests that cover the files it touched, plus any test named in the ticket, and a self-review gate requires them to pass and the diff to stay within the ticket's scope before a pull request is opened (§10.5). Full CI runs on the pull request; if it fails, the agent gets one retry with the log. Long horizons are handled with a notes file holding the plan and progress (external state, §14.4), compaction when the context passes a threshold, and a budget of turns and dollars per task with a summary of progress on exhaustion.

Evaluate. A suite of a few hundred historical tickets with their merged fixes and tests, replayed at the original commit; graded by the hidden tests of the real fix plus a judge comparing the diff with the real one for scope; pass\(^3\) to measure consistency; error analysis on failures (§19.8). Online: merge rate, reviewer time per pull request, and the fraction of pull requests abandoned.

Secure. Untrusted input includes tickets, code comments, dependency source and documentation in the repository. The capabilities are writing code and, potentially, network access. The blast radius is bounded by a token that can only open pull requests, a sandbox whose egress reaches only the package registry, no secrets mounted, and branch protection requiring human review. The residual risk, a subtly malicious change, is the reviewer's to catch, so pull requests carry a summary of what changed and why, and changes to sensitive paths (authentication, build configuration) require an owner's review.

Operate and evolve. Traces per task feed dashboards of merge rate, cost and turns; cache hit rate is alerted on. Routine sub-tasks (summarising a test log, writing a commit message) go to the small model. Once a few thousand merged trajectories exist, the team can distil the agent to a cheaper model for the most common ticket types (§17.8), with the frontier agent as fallback, and use the test verifier for rejection-sampling fine-tuning (§17.3).

Worked example — the interviewer pushes on cost

Interviewer: "$7,400 a day is $1.6 million a year. Halve it."

Where the money goes. Of $1.86 per task, $1.14 is cached re-reads of the growing history, $0.42 new input, $0.30 output.

Lever 1: shorter contexts. Compacting the history every 15 turns, keeping the notes file and the current diff, caps the context near 50,000 tokens instead of 140,000 at the end; cached reads fall roughly by half, to about $0.55.

Lever 2: trim observations. Truncating test output to failures and file reads to relevant ranges cuts \(\Delta n\) from 2,500 to 1,500, reducing both new input and the cached quadratic term.

Lever 3: route sub-tasks. Moving log summarisation and search-result triage to the small model removes perhaps 15% of frontier turns.

Result. Roughly $0.90 per task, without touching the success rate, which is worth checking on the suite before claiming. The interviewer is looking for the decomposition first and the levers second, and for the warning that compaction can lose information and must be evaluated.

22.4 Case study: the airline support agent

Prompt: "Design an agent that handles customer requests for an airline: changes, cancellations, refunds, baggage questions."

Clarify. Customers chat on the website and app; the agent can look up bookings, quote changes, rebook, cancel, and issue refunds or vouchers within policy; escalation goes to human agents who cost about $4 per conversation; regulatory rules on refunds apply. Success is resolution without policy violation; violations (an ineligible refund, a missed entitlement) are the costliest failure. Latency should feel conversational: a few seconds per reply.

Estimate. From Chapter 21: 50,000 conversations a month, 20 turns, about $0.31 in tokens with caching on the frontier model ($0.33 with tools), 6.3 seconds per turn of which 5 is decoding, 33 conversations in flight at the peak hour. At 70% resolution, the expected cost is $1.53 per conversation against $5 for a fully human service.

Design. This is a system, not one agent (§16.2). Authentication and identity verification are a fixed workflow. A router classifies intent; informational questions go to a retrieval-backed chain with a cached answer for the most common ones (§21.4); booking changes go to a constrained agent with read tools, a deterministic quote tool and guarded write tools; unusual complaints go to a more autonomous agent with a low threshold for escalation. Policy is enforced in code at the action guardrail: eligibility checks before any refund, caps without approval, refunds only to the original payment method (§20.5, §21.6). The user-facing reply streams; the visible text is short.

Model choice. Should the agent use the small model to save money? Figure 22.4 answers in the interview's terms. With the small model at about $0.05 per conversation including tools and the frontier at $0.35, the token saving is $0.30, which equals the escalation cost of 7.5 points of resolution at $4 each. The small model is worth it only if it resolves within 7.5 points of the frontier, and if its violation rate is no worse, which must be measured; a cascade (§21.3) is the usual compromise.

Evaluate. A simulator in the style of τ-bench (§19.2): scenarios drawn from real conversations by intent, a user-simulator model validated against transcripts (including uncooperative customers), a seeded booking database and the policy; grading by final database state and code checks of policy on the trajectory; pass\(^k\) with \(k=4\) or more per scenario because customers experience consistency; adversarial customers who try to talk the agent into ineligible refunds (§20.1). Online: resolution, escalation, repeat contacts within seven days, policy audits on a sample, and customer satisfaction.

Operate. Selective automation at the cost-minimising coverage (§21.7), with human agents sized by the workload and Little's law; approvals above refund caps with the key facts highlighted to avoid rubber-stamping; traces with redaction of payment data; releases through shadow and canary with escalation and violation rates as the gating metrics.

Drill 22.1 — the policy-violation constraint

The frontier design resolves 70% with a violation rate of 0.3% of conversations; a cheaper design resolves 69% with 0.8% violations. A violation costs on average $60 (refund leakage, regulatory risk, remediation). Per conversation, which is cheaper, given tokens of $0.35 and $0.12 respectively?

Frontier: \(0.35+0.30\times4+0.003\times60=0.35+1.20+0.18=\$1.73\). Cheaper: \(0.12+0.31\times4+0.008\times60=0.12+1.24+0.48=\$1.84\). The cheaper design loses on violations alone. In an interview, naming violations as a separate cost, measured separately, is what distinguishes a business-aware answer.

22.5 Case study: the research agent

Prompt: "Design an agent that answers open research questions, such as 'what is known about the effect of remote work on productivity?', with a cited report in a few minutes."

Clarify. Users are analysts; a report is two to five pages with citations to sources the user can check; success is a report an expert judges accurate, well-supported and reasonably complete; the cardinal failure is a citation that does not support its claim, or a fabricated one. Time budget: under five minutes. The agent reads the open web and possibly licensed databases; it does not act on the world, except by writing the report.

Estimate and design. A single agent reading pages sequentially would need perhaps 100 pages and 100 turns, \(100\times7\) seconds, about 12 minutes: too slow, and its context would overflow. The work is broad and parallelisable, the case for an orchestrator with workers (§15.4). Figure 22.5 shows the design. An orchestrator plans five to eight sub-questions; workers search and read in parallel, each in its own context (§14.5), and return structured notes: claims with verbatim supporting quotes and source URLs. A synthesis step writes the report from the notes, and a citation checker verifies every cited quote against the stored source text before the report is returned.

The numbers support the design. A worker of 25 turns reading a 3,000-token page per turn, with a 4,000-token prefix and 300 output tokens per turn, costs about $0.65 on the frontier model with caching and $0.05 on the small model; its latency is about \(25\times(0.6+5+2)=190\) seconds on the frontier and 115 on the small model. Six workers in parallel plus the orchestrator and synthesis (about $0.20) cost $4.10 with frontier workers or $0.50 with small ones, in three to four minutes. The design question is where quality comes from: extraction of claims with quotes is a task small models do well, while planning the sub-questions and synthesising a balanced report benefit from the frontier model. Small workers and a frontier orchestrator are the natural split, to be confirmed on the evaluation set.

Evaluate, secure, operate. Evaluation uses questions with expert reference notes; graders check citation faithfulness automatically (is the quote in the source, and does it support the claim, by a calibrated judge), coverage against the reference key points, and organisation by a judge with length control (§19.6). Injection exposure is real, about 100 pages per report, so \(N_up_{\text{inj}}\approx1\%\) at \(p_{\text{inj}}=10^{-4}\) (§20.2), but the agent has no tools that write or send, so the blast radius is a biased report; source-quality filters, quoting rather than paraphrasing, and showing sources to the user limit even that. In production, the fabricated-citation rate is a hard constraint monitored on every report, since the checker makes it measurable.

22.6 Case study: a personal email and calendar assistant

Prompt: "Design an assistant that manages a busy executive's email and calendar: triage, drafting replies, scheduling meetings."

This prompt is a security question in disguise, and the strongest answers recognise it within the first few minutes. The assistant holds private data (the inbox and calendar), reads untrusted content (every incoming email is written by someone else), and can communicate outward (send email, accept invitations): the full lethal trifecta (§12.5). Any design in which one model context reads incoming mail and can send mail is exposed to an injected email that instructs it to forward the inbox; at thousands of emails a month and any non-zero \(p_{\text{inj}}\), that will eventually happen.

Design. Separate reading from acting (§20.4). A quarantined reader processes each incoming email into structured fields: sender, category, urgency, requested action, proposed meeting times, a summary; it has no tools. A privileged planner sees the user's instructions and those structured fields, never raw email text, and proposes actions. Values derived from emails carry provenance capabilities, in the style of CaMeL: a recipient address extracted from an email is untrusted, so a policy permits sending only to addresses from the user's contacts or the original sender of the thread being replied to, and anything else requires confirmation. Replies are created as drafts by default; sending requires the user's tap, except for a whitelist of low-risk actions the user opts into (accepting internal meetings in free slots). Calendar writes are reversible and are allowed within rules (no double booking, working hours).

Quantities. At 150 incoming emails a day, the reader costs little on the small model (a few thousand tokens each, under $0.01 per email in total). Drafts by the frontier planner, perhaps 30 a day at $0.02 each, are cheap. The real cost is the user's attention: each confirmation costs seconds and erodes trust in the system if it is too frequent, so the measure of the design is how many confirmations a week the policy requires for how much protection. The answer should name that trade and propose measuring it.

Worked example — the confirmation budget and the residual risk

The executive sends about 30 replies a day: 25 to the original sender of the thread, 3 to other people in their contacts, 2 to addresses that appear only inside emails (a new colleague's address given in a signature, say).

Confirmations. Under the policy, only the last kind needs confirmation: about 2 a day, 10 a week. That is a tolerable burden, and each confirmation can show where the address came from ("found in an email from X, not in your contacts").

Single-agent design. Suppose about one email a week is crafted to manipulate the assistant, and a single agent that reads it follows it 2% of the time, with auto-send enabled: about one leak a year, and a leak can be the whole inbox, since the agent can read everything.

Split design. The injection reaches only the reader's structured fields. To cause a send, it must make the planner propose one to an untrusted address (say 10% of attempts, by distorting the summary), and the user must approve the confirmation (say 5% of suspicious ones): \(52\times0.1\times0.05\approx0.26\) leaks a year, each limited to the content of one reply. The count falls by about four and the severity by far more.

What to say in the room. The numbers are guesses, but the structure is not: the design converts an unbounded, unattended risk into a bounded one that needs a person's mistake, at a cost of ten confirmations a week. Then propose measuring both rates with an injection suite.

Evaluate. Utility on a set of realistic inbox scenarios, and security with an injection suite: emails carrying instructions to forward, delete, schedule or reply, in many framings, with the attack success rate and the false-confirmation rate as metrics, plus adaptive red teaming (§20.8). A design that cannot state its blast radius in one sentence ("a compromised reader can at worst mis-summarise an email") is not finished.

22.7 Case study: a data-analysis agent over a warehouse

Prompt: "Design an agent that lets business users ask questions of our data warehouse in plain English and get answers with charts."

Clarify. Users are non-technical; the warehouse has thousands of tables of varying quality; answers must be correct, and a confidently wrong number is worse than "I don't know"; some data is restricted by role (salaries, customer personal data); queries cost money (the warehouse bills by data scanned). Success: answers that an analyst would sign off, with the query shown.

Design. Figure 22.6 shows it. The core difficulty is not writing SQL but choosing the right tables and definitions: "revenue" may mean three different things in three tables. A semantic layer, curated definitions of metrics and dimensions with the tables that implement them, is retrieved into the context (§9.6), and the agent prefers defined metrics over raw tables. The agent writes a query, runs it with a dry-run first to estimate the bytes scanned and reject expensive queries, inspects the result (row counts, nulls, ranges) for plausibility, and iterates; this is code as action (Chapter 10) with the warehouse as the environment. The agent runs as the asking user, with the user's role and row-level security, on a read-only connection, so it cannot see or change what the user could not (§20.5). Answers include the query, the definitions used and any assumptions; questions that match no definition prompt clarification rather than a guess.

Quantities and evaluation. A question takes five to ten turns; with a large schema and definitions in the prefix (say 20,000 tokens, cached), cost is a few cents per question on the frontier model, while a careless query scanning a 10 TB table could cost tens of dollars at warehouse prices, so the dry-run cap matters more than the token bill. Evaluation is unusually clean: a set of questions with analyst-written reference queries, graded by executing both and comparing results (execution accuracy), plus questions that should be refused or clarified, scored for appropriate abstention. The metric that predicts user trust is the rate of confidently wrong answers, which the plausibility checks, the visible query and the abstention behaviour are all designed to drive down.

Drill 22.2 — the value of abstention

The data agent answers 85% of questions correctly, answers 10% wrongly and abstains on 5%. A wrong answer acted on costs on average $200 in bad decisions; an abstention costs $15 of analyst time to answer manually; a correct answer saves $15. A stricter plausibility check would convert half the wrong answers into abstentions, but also 3 points of correct answers into abstentions. Is the check worth it, per question?

Current: \(0.85\times15-0.10\times200-0.05\times15=12.75-20-0.75=-\$8.00\) per question: the agent destroys value. With the check: correct 82%, wrong 5%, abstain 13%: \(12.30-10-1.95=\$0.35\). The check turns a harmful system into a useful one. In interviews, reasoning about the asymmetry between wrong answers and abstentions is a strong signal of judgement.

22.8 Common mistakes and how answers are scored

Interviewers see the same failure patterns repeatedly, and most of them are failures of order or of proportion rather than of knowledge. The table summarises how the six dimensions of §22.1 are typically scored, with the behaviour that marks a weak, an adequate and a strong answer.

Six mistakes deserve naming. Designing before clarifying: an elegant design for a problem the interviewer did not pose scores below a plain design for the right one. Technique lists: naming RAG, reflection, a planner, a critic and three sub-agents without saying what each fixes, or what it costs, signals familiarity without judgement. Missing the dominant cost: optimising token prices when escalations, reviewer time or violations dominate the economics (§21.1, Drill 22.1). Trusting the model with the guarantee: putting a policy that must always hold ("never refund more than the fare") in the prompt rather than in code. Ignoring the untrusted input: designing an agent that reads email, web pages or tickets without a sentence about injection. Unmeasurable claims: "this will improve accuracy" without saying how much, measured how, on what, with what uncertainty. Each is cheap to avoid with the method of §22.2, and each, avoided, is visible to the interviewer as a strength.

Finally, the conversation itself is assessed. Thinking aloud, drawing as one goes, stating assumptions, inviting the interviewer to redirect, and changing course gracefully when given new information are all part of the performance. A candidate who says "I'd normally measure this; my guess is X, and if it turned out to be Y I would change the design in this way" demonstrates the calibrated reasoning that agent engineering needs, because so much of the field is still empirical.

22.9 How to practise

Design interviews reward practice more than almost any other kind of interview, because the method of §22.2 only becomes fluent with repetition. A practical routine has four parts. Work prompts aloud, against a clock. Take a prompt from the interview questions below or invent one from a product you use, set forty-five minutes, and talk through the eight steps, drawing as you go. Recording the session and listening to it is uncomfortable and effective: most candidates discover that they skipped clarification, or never stated a number, or spent twenty minutes on the component they found most interesting.

Rebuild the estimates from memory. The reference table of §22.2 should be derivable, not just remembered: cost per task from tokens and caching, turn latency from speeds, concurrency from Little's law, required reliability from the horizon. Practise deriving each for a new prompt in under two minutes. Interviewers notice whether a number was computed or recalled, and computed numbers can be defended when challenged.

Study real systems. Read the engineering write-ups that agent builders publish about their systems, their benchmarks' papers and their post-mortems, and for each ask the questions of the rubric: what was the task, what did they measure, what is the autonomy level of each component, where are the guarantees, what failed. The case studies in this chapter were built that way, and the same exercise on a new system takes an hour and produces an answer to a likely interview prompt.

Practise the follow-ups. Interviewers push on whichever dimension the answer neglected: "halve the cost", "it must answer in two seconds", "a customer has found a way to get refunds they are not owed", "the model provider deprecates your model next month", "traffic grows ten times". For each case study, prepare an answer to each of these pushes; they are where the quantitative models of the book do their work, and a candidate who can say which number changes and what follows from it is demonstrating exactly the skill the role requires.

Looking ahead

This is the last chapter, so "looking ahead" here means the field rather than the book. The arithmetic of Chapter 1 still frames its central problem: the length of task an agent can complete is set by its per-step reliability and its ability to detect and recover from errors, and the measured time horizon of frontier agents has grown steadily without that constraint disappearing. Several open problems follow from the chapters. Long-horizon reliability: agents that work for days on open-ended goals need better verification of intermediate progress, better memory, and recovery that does not depend on a person noticing (Chapter 14). Learning from deployment: agents that improve from their own experience safely, turning production traces into training signal without learning to game their checks (Chapters 17–18). Evaluation that keeps pace: benchmarks that resist contamination and saturation, and measures of reliability and safety that are as cheap to compute as success rates (Chapter 19). Security by design: architectures that make injection irrelevant to severe consequences while keeping agents useful, and verified policies at the boundaries (Chapter 20). Oversight that scales: monitoring and control protocols that remain effective as agents become more capable than their monitors (§20.7). And economics: as prices fall and capabilities rise, which work is better done by agents, which by people, and which by the two together (Chapter 21).

None of these problems will be solved by a single technique, and the history in Chapter 1 suggests that each solution will reveal the next bottleneck. The reader who has worked through the book has the tools to follow, and to contribute to, what comes next: a precise model of what an agent is, the mathematics of its reliability and cost, a working knowledge of each component and why it appeared, and the habits of measurement and of designing for failure that the field rewards.

Summary

  • The agent design round assesses framing, quantitative reasoning, architecture and autonomy, reliability, evaluation, and safety and operations, with prioritisation and calibrated confidence distinguishing strong answers.
  • Method: clarify (task, success, constraints); estimate (volume, tokens, cost, latency, concurrency, required \(\rho=T^{1/H}\)); baseline (least autonomy); deepen where hard; evaluate; secure; operate; evolve. Carry the reference numbers of §22.2.
  • Coding agent: a single sandboxed loop with code search, targeted tests and a self-review gate, PR-only permissions, notes and budgets; about $1.86 per task, dominated by cached history; value set by merge rate and reviewer time.
  • Support agent: a system of workflows and constrained agents with policy in code; model choice decided by resolution and violations, not token price (7.5 points of resolution equal the frontier's extra cost).
  • Research agent: orchestrator with parallel small-model workers producing quoted claims, frontier synthesis, deterministic citation checking; no send tools bounds injection to a biased report.
  • Email assistant: a trifecta problem; quarantined reader, privileged planner, provenance-based send policy, drafts by default; measure confirmations against protection.
  • Data agent: semantic layer, dry-run cost caps, run as the user read-only, plausibility checks and abstention; confidently wrong answers are the metric to drive down.
  • Practise aloud against a clock, derive the reference numbers rather than recall them, study real systems through the rubric, and prepare answers to the standard pushes on cost, latency, abuse, deprecation and scale.
  • Avoid: designing before clarifying, technique lists, missing the dominant cost, policies in prompts, ignoring untrusted input, unmeasurable claims.

Interview questions

These are full design prompts. Work each for 20–45 minutes with the method of §22.2 before revealing the outline answer.

Warm-up What do you ask in the first five minutes of an agent design round?

Who the users are and what one task looks like (with an example); what success means and who judges it; what the agent may do (read, write, send, spend) and what is irreversible; volume and latency expectations; data access and sensitivity; regulatory constraints; existing systems and tools. Then restate the problem with a success metric and confirm it.

Warm-up Which estimates do you make before designing?

Tasks per day and at peak; turns and tokens per task; cost per task with caching and per successful outcome including failure costs; latency per turn and per task; concurrency by Little's law; required per-step reliability \(T^{1/H}\); injection exposure if untrusted input is read. Keep them on the whiteboard and reuse them.

Core Design a coding agent that fixes failing CI tests automatically.

Trigger on CI failure (deduplicate by commit and test); sandbox at the failing commit with cached dependencies; agent loop with log reading, code search, edit, targeted test run; reproduce the failure first; distinguish flaky tests (rerun) from real failures; self-review gate (test passes, diff minimal, no test edits); PR-only token, egress limited; budget per task; evaluation on historical failures with known fixes; metrics: fix-merge rate, false fixes, reviewer time; guard against reward hacking (test modifications fail automatically).

Core Design an agent that processes refund requests for an e-commerce company.

Workflow skeleton: identify order (fixed), classify request, check eligibility in code, compute amount deterministically; agent for conversation and edge cases; refund tool behind policy guardrail with caps and approval above them; fraud signals (repeat refunds) route to people. Estimate cost per conversation and escalation economics; selective automation at the cost optimum; evaluation with a simulator including adversarial customers; metrics: resolution, violations, refund leakage, pass^k; traces and canaries.

Core Design a deep-research agent and estimate its cost and latency.

Orchestrator plans sub-questions; 5–8 parallel workers (small model) search and read with isolated contexts, returning claims with verbatim quotes and source snapshots; frontier synthesis writes from notes only; deterministic citation checker. Cost: ~$0.05 per small worker, ~$0.65 per frontier worker, ~$0.20 orchestration: $0.50–$4 per report; latency ~2–4 minutes with parallelism. No write/send tools. Evaluate faithfulness, coverage, organisation with calibrated judges.

Core Design an email assistant that can send on the user's behalf. How do you keep it safe?

Recognise the trifecta. Quarantined reader with no tools converts emails to structured fields; privileged planner never sees raw text; provenance on values; policy: recipients from contacts or the thread's original sender, else confirm; drafts by default, opt-in auto-send for low-risk actions; rate limits; logging; injection test suite and red teaming; measure confirmation burden against attack success. Blast radius of a compromised reader: a wrong summary.

Core Design a natural-language analytics agent over a data warehouse.

Semantic layer retrieval for metric definitions; agent writes SQL, dry-runs to cap cost, executes as the user on read-only with row-level security, checks plausibility (counts, nulls, ranges, comparison with known totals), shows query, definitions and assumptions, and abstains or clarifies when no definition fits. Evaluation by execution accuracy against analyst queries plus abstention scoring; key metric: confidently wrong answers.

Core Your design costs too much. How do you cut cost without hurting quality?

Decompose cost per task from traces: cached history, new input, output, tools, failures. Levers: caching discipline; compaction and sub-agent isolation; trimming tool outputs; shorter reasoning budgets on easy steps; routing routine nodes to small models; cascades with precise checks; semantic caching for stateless FAQs; distillation at volume. Re-measure quality on the suite with paired comparisons before claiming no loss; remember failure costs often dominate.

Core When would you choose a multi-agent design in an interview, and how would you justify it?

When the work is parallelisable (breadth of search), contexts would otherwise overflow (isolation), or components need different permissions (security separation). Justify with numbers: latency from parallelism (Amdahl), context budget per worker, blast-radius reduction. Acknowledge costs: more tokens, coordination failures, harder evaluation. Otherwise default to one agent with good tools.

Deep Design an agent that works autonomously for a week on a software migration across hundreds of services.

Decompose into per-service milestones with verifiable completion (builds, tests, canary health); orchestrator holds the plan in external state; per-service workers in sandboxes with PR-only permissions; checkpoints and durable execution; budgets per service; human review batched; monitoring for drift and loops; failure taxonomy updated daily; rollbacks per service. Reliability: milestones bound error propagation (Chapter 14); estimate cost by services × per-service task cost; evaluation on a pilot subset first.

Deep Design an agent that operates a legacy desktop application with no API.

Computer-use agent in a dedicated VM with the application; grounding via accessibility tree where available, screenshots otherwise; scripted, verified workflows for frequent tasks with the model handling exceptions; checks of end state by reading the screen or downstream records; slow (seconds per action) so batch and run asynchronously; minimal credentials; confirmation for irreversible actions; injection risk from displayed content; evaluation on recorded tasks; plan to replace with an API integration where volume justifies.

Deep How would you improve the support agent over its first year?

Weekly error analysis from traces feeding the suite; fix evaluation bugs first; harness rules for common failures; per-node routing to small models where measured safe; distillation once thousands of verified conversations exist; preference data from escalation outcomes and human edits; RL in the simulator with protected policy checks; continuous monitoring of resolution, violations, cost; release ladder for every change; track pass^k and customer outcomes, not just resolution.

Design Design an AI scheduling agent that negotiates meeting times with external people by email.

Trifecta again (calendar private, external emails untrusted, sends outward). Quarantined reader extracts proposed times; planner proposes slots from the user's calendar using rules (working hours, buffers, priorities); outbound emails reveal only proposed slots, never calendar details; recipients restricted to the thread's participants; confirmations for unusual requests (weekend, long meetings, new attendees); rate limits; evaluation with simulated counterparties including adversarial ones; metrics: time to scheduled meeting, user overrides, leaks.

Design Design a platform that lets teams across a company build their own agents safely.

Shared harness with tracing, budgets, caching and guardrails built in; a tool registry with reviewed definitions, scoped credentials per agent and per user, and provenance tracking; policy engine for consequential actions; sandboxes for code; evaluation service with suites per agent and release gates; red-team suite for injection; cost dashboards and quotas per team; model routing and pinned versions; templates for common architectures; incident response and kill switches centrally.

Design Design a voice phone agent for a restaurant chain's reservations.

Latency is the binding constraint: sub-second turn-taking needs streaming speech recognition and synthesis, a small fast model for dialogue, and tool calls (availability, booking) under ~300 ms or covered by filler speech. Fixed workflow for the booking slot-filling, model for language understanding and exceptions; confirmation read-back before booking; transfer to staff on difficulty; caller authentication for changes; evaluation with recorded and simulated calls; metrics: completion, transfer rate, latency percentiles, booking errors.

Design Design an agent that triages security alerts for a security operations team.

High volume, high cost of misses. Agent enriches each alert with read-only queries (logs, asset inventory, threat intelligence), proposes a verdict with evidence and confidence; selective automation: auto-close only low-risk categories with high measured precision; everything else ranked for analysts; no containment actions without approval initially; injection risk from attacker-controlled log content (treat as untrusted, no outward tools); evaluation on labelled historical alerts with recall on true incidents as the hard constraint; monitoring for drift as attacks change.

Core How do you present uncertainty about model behaviour in a design interview?

Separate what is derived (arithmetic: cost, latency, required reliability), what is measurable (resolution, error profiles, judge error rates), and what is assumed (a model's competence on the domain). State assumptions with a number, say how you would measure them early (pilot suite, shadow traffic), and how the design changes if they are wrong (cascade instead of small model, more verification, narrower autonomy).

Core The interviewer says traffic will grow ten times next year. What changes in your design?

Recompute: tokens per minute against rate limits (and whether cached tokens count), concurrency by Little's law for sandboxes and tool back-ends, human-review staffing at the chosen coverage, and monthly cost. At ten times the volume, fixed costs amortise better, so distillation and fine-tuning may cross their break-even (§17.8); routing and caching pay more; multi-provider fallback becomes necessary for availability; evaluation suites can grow with production traces; and rare failures become daily events, so per-class monitoring and budgets matter more.
