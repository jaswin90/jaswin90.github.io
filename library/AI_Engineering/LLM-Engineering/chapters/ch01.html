Part I · Foundations — Chapter 1

From Shannon to Serving

How predicting the next word went from a parlour game for information theorists to the most expensive computation on Earth—and why running a language model became a discipline of its own.

Why this chapter

Type a question into a chat assistant and press Enter. Within a few hundred milliseconds the first words appear, and the rest of the answer streams in at roughly the speed you can read. Behind that experience, a model with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters is evaluated once for every word-fragment it writes. Each evaluation reads gigabytes of numbers from memory. Thousands of other people are being served by the same chips at the same moment, each with their own conversation history held in that memory. Every second of this costs real money, and across the industry the bill for serving models has grown to rival or exceed the bill for training them.

This book is about the engineering that makes that experience possible: LLM inference engineering. It sits at the meeting point of numerical linear algebra, computer architecture, distributed systems and queueing theory. Before we can study any of its techniques, we need to understand how we got here. Why are today's models shaped the way they are? Why does producing text one token at a time stress hardware in such a particular way? Why did the industry's cost centre move from training to serving, and when?

This chapter answers those questions by telling the history of language modelling as a sequence of bottlenecks. Each generation of models solved the problem that limited its predecessor, and in doing so created a new problem. You will see the central tension of the whole book emerge in §1.6, when the Transformer trades a model with a small, fixed memory for one whose memory grows with every token it reads. By the end of the chapter you will be able to estimate, with nothing more than a model size and a hardware datasheet, how fast a model can possibly write. You will also have the vocabulary that the rest of the book, and every inference interview, uses.

1.1 Language as a probability distribution

In 1948 Claude Shannon wanted to understand how much information English text carries, and therefore how efficiently it could be transmitted. His insight was to treat text as the output of a random process. Each letter is drawn from a distribution that depends on the letters before it. A source with predictable output carries little information per symbol; a source whose next symbol is hard to guess carries a lot[1]. Three years later he measured the predictability of English directly. Human subjects guessed the next letter of a passage one character at a time, and from the number of guesses they needed he estimated that printed English carries roughly 0.6 to 1.3 bits per character[2]. That number is, in a precise sense, the target every language model since has been chasing.

The modern formulation is Shannon's, made explicit. Fix a finite vocabulary \(\mathcal V\) of tokens. Tokens can be characters, words or the sub-word pieces that Chapter 2 describes. A language model is a probability distribution over finite token sequences \(x_{1:T}=(x_1,\dots,x_T)\). Any such distribution can be written, with no assumptions at all, by the chain rule of probability:

\[ p(x_{1:T}) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_{<t}), \qquad x_{<t} := (x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}). \]

An autoregressive language model parameterises the conditionals \(p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})\) directly, with parameters \(\theta\). Everything in this book follows from two consequences of this choice. First, training can be parallel. Given a complete document, all \(T\) conditional probabilities can in principle be evaluated at the same time, because every prefix is already known. Whether that parallelism is actually available depends on the architecture, and this turns out to decide which architecture wins (§1.6). Second, generation is sequential. To sample a new text we must draw \(x_1\), feed it back, draw \(x_2\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_1)\), and so on. Each token needs a fresh evaluation of the model, and the \(t\)-th evaluation cannot begin until the \((t-1)\)-th has finished. This sequential dependence is the root of almost every difficulty in LLM inference.

Measuring a language model

How do we know whether one model is better than another? Let \(p^{*}\) be the true, unknown distribution of text. We would like \(p_\theta\) to be close to \(p^{*}\), and the natural measure is the per-token cross-entropy, estimated on held-out text:

\[ H(p^{*}, p_\theta) \;=\; -\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p^{*}}\Big[\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})\Big]. \]

This is exactly the training loss of every modern LLM. It decomposes as \(H(p^{*},p_\theta) = H(p^{*}) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}\,\|\,p_\theta)\). Because the Kullback–Leibler divergence is non-negative (Gibbs' inequality), the loss can never fall below \(H(p^{*})\), the entropy rate of language itself. Some uncertainty in text is irreducible: no model can know which of several equally sensible continuations an author chose. We will meet this floor again as the constant \(E\) in the scaling laws of §1.8.

Cross-entropy is often reported as perplexity, \(\mathrm{PP}=e^{H}\) when \(H\) is in nats (or \(2^{H}\) in bits). Perplexity has a pleasant interpretation as an effective branching factor. A perplexity of 8 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 8 tokens. Perplexity has one trap: it depends on what a token is. A model with a larger vocabulary predicts fewer but more informative tokens per document, so its per-token perplexity can be higher even when it assigns higher probability to the text as a whole. To compare models with different tokenisers, divide the total negative log-likelihood by the number of bytes (or characters). The result, bits per byte, is in Shannon's own units.

Worked example — converting units

A model reports a validation loss of 1.95 nats per token on English web text, using a tokeniser that averages 4.2 bytes per token. Its perplexity is \(e^{1.95}\approx 7.0\). In bits per token the loss is \(1.95/\ln 2 = 2.81\); dividing by 4.2 bytes per token gives \(0.67\) bits per byte. For English text, where a byte is essentially a character, this lies inside Shannon's estimate of human predictive ability.

We now have a target, the entropy of language, and a yardstick, cross-entropy. The history that follows is the story of how the gap between them was closed, and of what each step cost when the model is run.

1.2 Counting: the n-gram era (1948–2003)

The conditionals \(p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\) depend on an unboundedly long history. The first practical idea was to throw most of that history away. An \(n\)-gram model makes a Markov assumption of order \(n-1\): the next token depends only on the previous \(n-1\) tokens. The model then reduces to a table of counts. If \(c(\cdot)\) counts occurrences in a training corpus, the maximum-likelihood estimate is

\[ p(x_t\mid x_{<t}) \approx p(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1:t-1}), \qquad \hat p_{\text{MLE}}(w\mid h) = \frac{c(h,w)}{c(h)} . \]

For three decades this was the state of the art. Shannon himself used \(n\)-gram approximations to generate increasingly English-like gibberish in his 1948 paper. From the 1970s, groups at IBM led by Fred Jelinek built speech recognisers on trigram models. That work established the statistical, data-driven approach to language that eventually displaced hand-written grammars; Jelinek is often credited with the quip that his system's accuracy improved every time a linguist left the team.

The \(n\)-gram's weakness is sparsity, and it is exponential. There are \(|\mathcal V|^{n-1}\) possible contexts. With a modest vocabulary of 50,000 words a trigram model has \(2.5\times10^{9}\) contexts, each with 50,000 possible continuations: \(1.25\times10^{14}\) table cells, almost none of which appear in any corpus. The maximum-likelihood estimate assigns probability zero to every unseen combination. One unseen trigram in a test sentence makes its probability zero and its perplexity infinite. A great deal of ingenuity went into smoothing, which moves probability mass from seen to unseen events. Methods included add-one (Laplace), Good–Turing, Katz back-off, linear interpolation, and finally Kneser–Ney smoothing[3]. Kneser–Ney's insight is subtle and worth remembering. When backing off to a shorter context, a word's probability should reflect how many different contexts it follows, not how often it occurs. "Francisco" is frequent, but almost always after "San", so it deserves little probability after an unfamiliar word. Chen and Goodman's careful comparison established modified Kneser–Ney as the strongest count-based method[4].

Smoothing reduced the symptoms but could not cure the disease. A count table cannot generalise: evidence about "the cat sat on the mat" says nothing about "the dog sat on the rug", because the model has no notion that cats and dogs are similar. Increasing \(n\) to capture longer context only makes the sparsity worse.

Why an inference engineer should still care about n-grams

Two ideas from this era return later in the book. First, an \(n\)-gram's state is the last \(n-1\) tokens: its memory is bounded, an idea that reappears as sliding-window attention (Chapter 8). Second, matching recent tokens against earlier text is a free and surprisingly good predictor. Modern servers use it as a "draft model" in prompt-lookup and n-gram speculative decoding (Chapter 20), for example when a model is copying code or quoting a document.

1.3 Learning representations: the neural turn (2003–2013)

The cure for sparsity was to stop treating words as atomic symbols. Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent and Jauvin proposed the neural probabilistic language model in 2003[5]. Each word \(w\) is assigned a learned vector \(e_w\in\mathbb R^{d}\), its embedding. The previous \(n-1\) embeddings are concatenated, passed through a hidden layer, and mapped by a softmax to a distribution over the whole vocabulary:

\[ p(x_t = w \mid x_{t-n+1:t-1}) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\big(b + U\tanh(c + H[e_{x_{t-1}};\dots;e_{x_{t-n+1}}])\big)_w . \]

Because similar words are trained to have similar vectors, what the model learns about one context transfers smoothly to similar contexts. This is generalisation by continuity in embedding space rather than by counting heuristics. The model still used a fixed window of \(n-1\) words, but it beat smoothed \(n\)-grams on perplexity.

Two details of Bengio's model are worth noticing, because they are still true of the largest models today. The embedding is a lookup, not a computation. Fetching row \(x_t\) of a table costs no arithmetic, only a memory read. And the softmax over the vocabulary was the computational bottleneck: the output layer has one row per vocabulary word, so it was by far the largest matrix, and Bengio et al. had to distribute it across machines. Twenty years later the output projection of a model with a 128,000-token vocabulary is still one of its largest matrices (Chapter 2).

Embeddings became ubiquitous with Mikolov and colleagues' word2vec (2013)[7]. It trained embeddings cheaply on billions of words and revealed striking linear structure: \(e_{\text{king}}-e_{\text{man}}+e_{\text{woman}}\approx e_{\text{queen}}\). The lesson the field drew was that geometry in a learned vector space can carry meaning. But the fixed context window remained. To condition on everything that came before, the model needed some form of memory.

1.4 Remembering: the recurrent era (2010–2017)

A recurrent neural network (RNN) abandons the fixed window. It carries a hidden state \(h_t\in\mathbb R^{d}\) forward through the sequence, updating it with each new token:

\[ h_t = \phi(W h_{t-1} + U e_{x_t} + b), \qquad p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{\le t}) = \operatorname{softmax}(W_{\text{out}} h_t). \]

Mikolov's recurrent language model (2010)[6] showed large perplexity gains over \(n\)-grams. In principle \(h_t\) can summarise the entire prefix. In practice, training it to do so is hard. Back-propagation through time multiplies Jacobians, one per step:

\[ \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{s}} = \prod_{k=s+1}^{t} \operatorname{diag}\big(\phi'(a_k)\big)\, W, \qquad \Big\|\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{s}}\Big\| \le \big(\gamma\,\|W\|\big)^{t-s}, \]

where \(a_k\) is the pre-activation and \(\gamma\) bounds \(|\phi'|\). Unless \(\gamma\|W\|\) stays very close to 1, the influence of an early token on a late gradient shrinks or grows exponentially with the distance between them. Hochreiter (1991) and Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (1994)[8] identified this vanishing-gradient problem. Its consequence is that a vanilla RNN learns short-range dependencies well and long-range ones barely at all.

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) cell of Hochreiter and Schmidhuber[9] fixed this with an architectural trick. Its cell state is updated additively, \(c_t = f_t\odot c_{t-1} + i_t\odot \tilde c_t\), where \(f_t\) and \(i_t\) are learned gates in \((0,1)\). Along the cell path the Jacobian is just the diagonal matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(f_t)\). When the forget gate is open, gradients flow back many steps almost undiminished. LSTMs became the workhorse of sequence modelling.

Recurrence also enabled conditional generation. In 2014 Sutskever, Vinyals and Le[10] used one LSTM to read a source sentence into a vector and a second LSTM to generate its translation from that vector (sequence-to-sequence). The weak point was obvious: an entire sentence, of any length, squeezed into one fixed-size vector. The same year Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio[11] removed the squeeze with attention. At each output step \(s\), the decoder computes a weight for every encoder state \(h_j\), based on how relevant it is to the decoder's current state, and reads a weighted average:

\[ \alpha_{sj} = \frac{\exp(\operatorname{score}(q_{s}, h_j))}{\sum_{k}\exp(\operatorname{score}(q_{s}, h_k))}, \qquad c_s = \sum_j \alpha_{sj}\,h_j, \]

where \(q_s\) is the decoder's state. Attention lets the decoder look back at whichever parts of the source it needs. Notice what this requires: to attend to the past, you must keep the past.

Key property — the inference cost of an RNN

Generating one token with an RNN costs \(O(d^2)\) floating-point operations (a few matrix–vector products). The only memory carried from step to step is \(h_t\): \(O(d)\) numbers per sequence, independent of how long the sequence already is. As an inference machine, the RNN is close to ideal. Its curse is training. Step \(t\) cannot be computed before step \(t-1\), so even when the whole training document is known, the work is a chain of \(T\) dependent steps. Parallel hardware cannot shorten that chain.

1.5 The Transformer's bargain (2017)

By 2016 the most accurate translation systems were stacks of LSTMs with attention, and training them was painfully slow. The authors of "Attention Is All You Need"[12] asked a radical question: if attention can look at any position directly, why keep the recurrence at all? The Transformer computes, for every position, a query \(q_i\), a key \(k_j\) and a value \(v_j\) by linear maps of its input, and lets every position attend to every permitted position:

\[ \operatorname{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}} + M\Big)V , \]

where the rows of \(Q,K,V\) are the queries, keys and values of all positions, \(d_h\) is their dimension, and \(M\) is a causal mask. The mask sets the score to \(-\infty\) whenever a query would look at a later position, so each position sees only its past. Chapter 2 takes this formula apart. What matters here is its consequence for training. For a whole document, the scores of every query against every key are one matrix product, and all \(T\) next-token predictions are computed in a single pass. A computation that was a chain of \(T\) dependent steps became one large, parallel matrix computation, which is exactly the kind of work GPUs are built for. The original model trained in 3.5 days on eight GPUs and set a new state of the art in English–German translation.

The bargain has a second half, which shows up only when we generate. To produce token \(t+1\), the new query must be compared with the keys of all \(t\) earlier tokens, and must average their values, in every layer. Recomputing all those keys and values at every step would make the cost of generating a sequence quadratic in its length. They do not change once computed, so we store them. This store is the key–value cache, or KV cache, and it is the single most important data structure in this book.

Figure 1.1 puts the two architectures side by side, and it is worth studying, because the contrast shapes the whole field. The RNN on the left carries forward only the vector \(h_t\). Whatever it has not squeezed into that vector is lost, but its memory never grows. The Transformer on the right keeps a lossless record: the full list of keys and values, one pair per token per layer. At step five, the new query \(q_5\) is compared with all five keys, and then \(k_5\) and \(v_5\) are appended for future steps. The record makes recall precise and training parallel. It also means the Transformer's working memory grows linearly with the length of the conversation, and so does the amount of data every generation step must read. Most of Parts III and IV of this book are about managing that growth. Chapter 22 shows how recent "state-space" and hybrid models try to recover the RNN's constant-size state without giving up the Transformer's training efficiency.

1.6 Scale (2018–2020)

Parallel training removed the practical ceiling on how much text a model could learn from. Three ideas, arriving in quick succession, turned that capacity into capability.

The first was generative pre-training. OpenAI's GPT-1 (2018, 117 million parameters)[13] pre-trained a decoder-only Transformer, one that simply predicts the next token, on a large corpus of books, then fine-tuned it on downstream tasks. Google's BERT (2018, up to 340 million)[14] used a bidirectional encoder trained to fill in masked words, and briefly dominated every benchmark. But a model that sees both sides of a blank does not define \(p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\) and cannot generate text left to right. The line that leads to today's assistants is the decoder-only one.

The second was zero-shot behaviour. GPT-2 (2019, 1.5 billion parameters)[15] showed that a large enough language model performs tasks it was never trained for, such as summarisation or translation, if the task is phrased as text to be continued.

The third was in-context learning. GPT-3 (2020, 175 billion parameters trained on about 300 billion tokens)[16] showed that a few worked examples placed in the prompt can replace fine-tuning altogether. For inference this was a turning point, though few noticed at the time. Prompts became long, and long prompts mean more computation before the first output token and larger KV caches during generation.

Figure 1.2 plots the parameter counts of landmark models on a logarithmic axis, and two phases are visible. From the Transformer to GPT-3, dense models grew by more than three orders of magnitude in three years, from 65 million to 175 billion parameters. Then the growth of dense models stalled. After 2022 the largest open dense model, Llama 3.1 405B, is only about twice GPT-3's size. Growth continued in a different form: Mixture-of-Experts models such as DeepSeek-V3 (671 billion) and Kimi K2 (1 trillion), shown in orange. These activate only a small fraction of their parameters for each token (37 billion of 671 billion for DeepSeek-V3), so their cost per token is much lower than their size suggests. Why did dense growth stop? Part of the answer is the next section: labs learned they had been training models that were too big for their data. The rest of the answer is the subject of this book: big models are expensive to serve.

1.7 Scaling laws, and why inference changed them

If loss keeps improving with scale, how should a fixed compute budget be split between a bigger model and more training data? Kaplan et al. (2020)[17] measured test loss across many model and data sizes and found smooth power laws, for example \(\mathcal L(N)\propto N^{-0.076}\) in the number of parameters \(N\), holding over seven orders of magnitude. Their compute-optimal recipe recommended growing models much faster than datasets, and the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, trained on only 300 billion tokens, reflects that advice.

Hoffmann et al. (2022)[18] revisited the question with learning-rate schedules tuned to each training length, and reached a different answer. They fitted

\[ \mathcal L(N,D) = E + \frac{c_N}{N^{\alpha_N}} + \frac{c_D}{D^{\alpha_D}}, \qquad E\approx1.69,\;c_N\approx406.4,\;c_D\approx410.7,\;\alpha_N\approx0.34,\;\alpha_D\approx0.28, \]

where \(D\) is the number of training tokens and \(E\) is the irreducible entropy term from §1.1. Training compute is well approximated by \(C\approx 6ND\) FLOPs: about six floating-point operations per parameter per training token, a count we derive in Chapter 2. The question "how should I spend \(C\)?" becomes a clean optimisation problem.

Derivation — the compute-optimal split

Substitute \(D = C/(6N)\) to get a function of \(N\) alone: \(f(N) = c_N N^{-\alpha_N} + c_D\,(6N/C)^{\alpha_D}\). The first term decreases in \(N\) and the second increases, so \(f\) has a unique minimum where \(f'(N)=0\): \[ \alpha_N c_N N^{-\alpha_N-1} = \alpha_D c_D\, 6^{\alpha_D} N^{\alpha_D-1} C^{-\alpha_D} \;\Longrightarrow\; N_{\text{opt}} = G\Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha_D}{\alpha_N+\alpha_D}},\quad D_{\text{opt}} = G^{-1}\Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha_N}{\alpha_N+\alpha_D}}, \] with \(G=(\alpha_N c_N/\alpha_D c_D)^{1/(\alpha_N+\alpha_D)}\). With \(\alpha_N\approx\alpha_D\), both exponents are close to \(\tfrac12\): parameters and data should grow together, in proportion to \(\sqrt{C}\).

Empirically the optimum sits at roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. To test it, Hoffmann et al. trained Chinchilla, a 70-billion-parameter model on 1.4 trillion tokens. It used the same compute as their earlier 280-billion-parameter Gopher, which had seen only 300 billion tokens, and Chinchilla beat Gopher across the board. Models of the GPT-3 era had been badly under-trained.

Inference-aware scaling

Chinchilla minimises the cost of training. But a model is trained once and then served, possibly for years. Each token it processes at inference costs about \(2N\) FLOPs: one multiplication and one addition per parameter, as Chapter 2 shows. If a model will process \(D_{\text{inf}}\) tokens over its lifetime, the total cost is

\[ C_{\text{lifetime}} \;\approx\; \underbrace{6\,N\,D}_{\text{training}} \;+\; \underbrace{2\,N\,D_{\text{inf}}}_{\text{inference}} . \]

When \(D_{\text{inf}}\) is large, the second term dominates, and it penalises \(N\) directly. It then pays to choose a smaller model and train it on more data than Chinchilla recommends. You reach the same loss, pay a one-off premium in training, and every future request becomes cheaper. Smaller models are also faster, which matters for latency, and they fit on fewer GPUs. Sardana and Frankle formalised this trade-off as "beyond Chinchilla-optimal" scaling[19], but practice had already moved there. LLaMA-7B (2023) was trained on one trillion tokens, about 140 per parameter. Llama 3 8B (2024) was trained on more than 15 trillion[20], nearly 1,900 tokens per parameter and almost a hundred times past the Chinchilla ratio.

Figure 1.3 shows the change of regime. Before 2022 the large models sat below the Chinchilla line (GPT-3 at 1.7 tokens per parameter, Gopher at about 1). After 2023, the models most widely deployed sit one to two orders of magnitude above it. The shift is the economics of inference reaching back to reshape training.

Drill 1.1 — When does serving cost more than training?

For Llama 3 8B, \(N=8\times10^{9}\) and \(D=15\times10^{12}\). Training compute is \(6ND\approx 7.2\times10^{23}\) FLOPs. Inference compute equals that when \(2ND_{\text{inf}} = 6ND\), that is \(D_{\text{inf}} = 3D = 45\) trillion tokens. A popular open model, served by many providers, can plausibly process that many tokens within months. In dollars the crossover comes even sooner, because, as we shall see in §1.9, inference runs hardware far less efficiently than training does.

1.8 Inference becomes the bottleneck

Scaled-up models were impressive but awkward to use. They continued text rather than following instructions. InstructGPT (2022)[21] showed that supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, followed by reinforcement learning from human feedback, turns a pre-trained model into an assistant. ChatGPT, released on 30 November 2022, packaged this in a chat interface and became one of the fastest-growing consumer products ever launched. Within weeks, the bottleneck of the AI industry was no longer "can we build it?" but "can we serve it?": how many conversations per second can a GPU sustain, at what latency, and at what cost?

It helps at this point to name the two phases of serving a request, which we will make precise in Chapter 3. When a request arrives, the model first reads the prompt. All prompt tokens are known in advance, so they can be processed together in one parallel pass, like training without the backward pass. This phase is called prefill. The model then writes the answer, one token per pass, each pass depending on the previous one. This phase is called decode. The two phases stress the hardware completely differently, as the next two sections explain.

Figure 1.4 draws the lifetime-compute formula for Llama 3 8B on log–log axes. The training cost is a horizontal line, and the serving cost is a rising one that crosses it at 45 trillion tokens. Frontier providers now report processing trillions of tokens per day, so for any successful model the crossing happens early in its life. Three features of today's API prices follow from the economics we have just described. Prices for a fixed level of capability have fallen by roughly an order of magnitude per year[22], and much of that is inference engineering. Output tokens are typically priced several times higher than input tokens, because, as §1.10 shows, writing is far less hardware-efficient than reading. And repeated prompt prefixes are discounted ("prompt caching"), because their KV cache can be kept and reused (Chapter 10).

1.9 The machine: compute outruns memory

To see why writing is expensive, we need a minimal picture of the hardware. Chapter 5 gives the full story; here is just enough. A modern accelerator such as NVIDIA's H100 has two resources that matter to us. The first is compute: thousands of arithmetic units, including specialised tensor cores that multiply small matrices in a single instruction. Together they perform about \(\pi\approx 989\) trillion floating-point operations per second (TFLOP/s) on 16-bit numbers. The second is memory: 80 GB of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) stacked beside the chip, which holds the model's weights and the KV caches. Data must travel from HBM to the arithmetic units before it can be used, and that link delivers about \(\beta\approx 3.35\) terabytes per second.

Horace He describes this situation as a factory and a warehouse[29], and Figure 1.5 draws it that way. The factory (the compute units) can process material extraordinarily quickly, but all material sits in a warehouse (HBM) connected to the factory by a road of limited capacity. The ratio of the two rates, \(\pi/\beta\approx 989\times10^{12}/3.35\times10^{12}\approx 295\), is the number of arithmetic operations the H100 can perform in the time it takes to fetch one byte. If an algorithm performs fewer than about 295 operations for every byte it fetches, the factory sits idle waiting for deliveries and the algorithm runs at the speed of the road, not the factory. We will call this ratio the ridge point when we formalise it in Chapter 3.

Figure 1.6 makes the divergence vivid: from the P100 to the B200, dense half-precision throughput grew about 100-fold while HBM bandwidth grew about 11-fold. Gholami et al. measured the same trend across two decades of server hardware. Peak FLOP/s grew about 3.0× every two years, DRAM bandwidth 1.6× and interconnect bandwidth only 1.4×[27]. They called it the memory wall. Every generation, algorithms that cannot reuse fetched data many times fall further behind the hardware's arithmetic capability. As we are about to see, writing text is exactly such an algorithm.

1.10 A first estimate: why writing is slow

Consider a single user generating text, so the batch holds one sequence. At each decode step the model must apply every one of its weight matrices to the vector representing the newest token. Applying a matrix to a vector performs two floating-point operations, a multiply and an add, for each matrix entry. And since the weights live in HBM, every entry must be fetched once per step. Write \(N\) for the number of parameters and \(b\) for the bytes used to store each one (\(b=2\) for 16-bit formats). Then, per step,

\[ \text{FLOPs} \approx 2N, \qquad \text{bytes fetched} \approx N b, \qquad \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{byte}} \approx \frac{2}{b} = 1 . \]

One operation per byte, against an H100 that needs 295 to keep its arithmetic units busy. The factory is idle more than 99% of the time, and the speed of generation is set entirely by the road:

\[ t_{\text{step}} \;\gtrsim\; \frac{N\,b + \text{(bytes of KV cache read)}}{\beta}, \qquad \text{tokens per second for one user} \;\lesssim\; \frac{\beta}{N b}. \]
Worked example — the running examples at batch size 1

Llama 3 8B in BF16 has 16 GB of weights. On an H100: \(t_{\text{step}}\ge 16/3350\) s \(\approx 4.8\) ms, so at most about 210 tokens per second.

Llama 3 70B in BF16 has about 141 GB of weights, which does not fit in one H100's 80 GB.

  • On one H200 (141 GB, 4.8 TB/s) it barely fits, with no room left for the KV cache. If it did fit, the bound would be \(141/4800\approx 29\) ms per step, or about 34 tokens per second.
  • On two H100s, each holding and reading half the weights, the aggregate bandwidth is 6.7 TB/s, giving about 21 ms per step or roughly 47 tokens per second, minus the cost of communication between the GPUs (Chapter 18).
  • Stored in 8-bit precision on one B200 (8 TB/s), 70.6 GB of weights gives about 8.8 ms, or roughly 113 tokens per second.

Real systems reach perhaps 60–85% of these bounds. Meanwhile an H100 could perform \(989\times10^{12}\times 0.0048\approx 4.7\times10^{12}\) operations in 4.8 ms, but the 8B model uses only \(1.6\times10^{10}\) of them, about 0.3%.

This one calculation explains a great deal. It explains why output tokens are priced higher than input tokens. During prefill, every weight fetched is applied to all the prompt's tokens at once, thousands of operations per byte, so the factory is busy. During decode, each weight fetched serves just one token per sequence. It explains why a GPU with twice the FLOP/s but the same bandwidth would not make a chatbot write any faster. And it points to the most important remedy: batching. If 64 users' decode steps are processed together, each weight fetched from HBM is used 64 times, so the same road carries 64 times as much useful work, and total throughput rises almost 64-fold. Batching is nearly free until either the arithmetic units become the bottleneck or memory runs out. Memory runs out because each of those 64 users brings a KV cache, and that is the tension at the centre of Chapters 3, 8, 10 and 11.

What interviewers listen for

Asked "how fast can model X run on GPU Y?", strong candidates (1) say which regime applies (memory-bound at small batch), (2) compute weight bytes and divide by bandwidth, (3) add KV-cache bytes for long contexts, (4) apply a realistic efficiency factor, and (5) explain what changes with large batches, quantisation, tensor parallelism or speculative decoding. Weak candidates divide FLOPs by peak FLOP/s and get an answer a hundred times too optimistic.

1.11 The systems response (2019–2026)

Once inference became expensive, a remarkable burst of engineering followed. It is best read not as a list of techniques but as a chain of cause and effect, each idea relieving one bottleneck and exposing the next. The rest of the book follows this chain in detail. Here is its outline.

Shrinking the state. As early as 2019, Noam Shazeer observed that decoding was limited by the bandwidth needed to read keys and values, not by arithmetic. He proposed multi-query attention, in which all attention heads share one key and one value per token, shrinking the cache many-fold[30]. The quality cost led to grouped-query attention (2023), now standard, and DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention (2024) compressed the cache further (Chapter 8).

Reading the state efficiently. For long sequences, the attention computation itself became expensive because it wrote a \(T\times T\) matrix of scores to memory and read it back. FlashAttention (2022) computed exactly the same result in tiles small enough to stay in on-chip memory, never materialising the matrix[31]. Its successors (2023, 2024, 2026) chased each new GPU generation (Chapter 9).

Keeping the factory busy. Batching helps only if requests can be batched. Early servers waited for a whole batch to finish before admitting new requests, so GPUs idled as short answers finished early. Orca (2022) scheduled at the granularity of single decode steps, letting requests join and leave the batch at every iteration[32]. This continuous batching is now universal (Chapter 11).

Not wasting memory. With continuous batching, the KV cache became the limiting resource, and it was being wasted. Servers reserved a contiguous block for each request's maximum possible length. vLLM's PagedAttention (2023) borrowed virtual memory from operating systems, allocating the cache in small pages on demand. Kwon et al. found that earlier systems used only 20–38% of the memory they reserved for actual tokens[33] (Chapter 10).

Fewer bytes per weight. Since decode time is proportional to bytes, storing weights in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16 speeds it up almost proportionally. A series of methods (LLM.int8, SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, then FP8 and FP4 hardware formats) made this practical without ruining accuracy (Chapter 14).

More than one token per step. If the factory is idle during decode anyway, why not verify several guessed tokens per weight fetch? Speculative decoding (2022–23) uses a cheap draft to propose tokens and the large model to check them all in one pass. It is mathematically guaranteed to produce the same distribution of outputs[34] (Chapter 20).

Fewer FLOPs per token. Mixture-of-Experts models (Mixtral 2023, DeepSeek-V2/V3 2024) route each token through a few of many expert sub-networks. They decouple capacity from per-token cost, at the price of new communication patterns between GPUs (Chapter 19).

Separating reading from writing. Prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound, so running both on the same GPUs makes each interfere with the other. Systems of 2024 (Sarathi-Serve, Splitwise, DistServe, Mooncake) split prefills into chunks or moved them onto separate machines, transferring the KV cache between them (Chapters 11 and 13). By 2025 datacenter-scale orchestration such as NVIDIA Dynamo and open engines such as vLLM V1 and SGLang combined all of the above.

1.12 The reasoning era (2024–)

Just as these techniques matured, the workload changed. Wei et al. (2022) showed that prompting a model to write out intermediate steps, a "chain of thought", improves multi-step reasoning[23]. OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025)[24] went further, training models with reinforcement learning to think at length before answering, often producing thousands to tens of thousands of reasoning tokens. Snell et al. (2024)[25] showed that for some problems, spending more computation at test time is more effective than using a larger model. Inference became not just a cost but a lever on capability.

For inference engineering, this was a change of regime. A typical chat turn reads a prompt of a few thousand tokens and writes a few hundred, so most tokens are processed in the efficient prefill phase. A reasoning turn may write twenty thousand tokens, almost all in the memory-bound decode phase. The KV cache now grows during generation to tens of thousands of entries, limiting how many requests fit on a GPU. Compression techniques designed for long inputs often fail on long outputs. Latency becomes capability: a system that decodes three times faster can let a model think three times longer within the same wait. And reinforcement-learning training itself is now dominated by generating rollouts, so inference engines sit inside training clusters. Every chapter of this book has to be read with this workload in mind; Chapter 26 treats it directly.

1.13 Measuring what matters

To optimise inference we need precise words for "fast" and "cheap", and the field has settled on a small vocabulary. Consider a request with \(n_{\text{in}}\) prompt tokens that produces \(n_{\text{out}}\) output tokens, streamed to the user as they are generated.

Figure 1.7 follows a request through time. It may first wait in a queue for capacity. Then the prefill pass processes all \(n_{\text{in}}\) prompt tokens and produces the first output token. The interval from arrival to that moment is the time to first token (TTFT), which is what a user experiences as responsiveness. Each subsequent token then takes one decode step. The gap between consecutive tokens is the inter-token latency (ITL), and its average over a request is the time per output token (TPOT):

\[ \text{TPOT} = \frac{\text{E2E} - \text{TTFT}}{n_{\text{out}}-1}, \qquad \text{E2E} = \text{TTFT} + (n_{\text{out}}-1)\cdot\text{TPOT}, \]

where E2E is the end-to-end latency. Different products care about different terms. A voice assistant lives or dies by TTFT, since silence is noticed within a few hundred milliseconds. A reading user needs TPOT below roughly 100–200 ms, which corresponds to human reading speed. An agent that chains dozens of model calls cares about E2E. An offline job, such as summarising a million documents, cares about none of them, only about throughput: total tokens (or requests) completed per second across the deployment.

Throughput and latency pull against each other. As §1.10 showed, batching more requests raises throughput, but each decode step then does more work, so TPOT rises. Admitting more requests than the system can absorb makes queues grow, so TTFT rises. Raw throughput can therefore be inflated by configurations that make users miserable. DistServe proposed measuring goodput instead: the request rate a system sustains while meeting its service-level objectives (SLOs), for example "90% of requests have TTFT ≤ 500 ms and TPOT ≤ 50 ms"[28]. The percentile matters. Users experience the tail: a service with a median TTFT of 200 ms and a 99th-percentile TTFT of 4 s feels broken, and interviewers will ask which percentile an SLO refers to.

For the mathematician — Little's law

For any queueing system in steady state, the mean number of items in the system equals the arrival rate times the mean time each item spends there: \(\bar n=\lambda W\). The law needs no distributional assumptions. In serving it converts latency into memory. If a deployment receives \(\lambda=20\) requests per second and each spends \(W=8\) s in the system, then on average \(\bar n=160\) requests are in flight, and the deployment must hold 160 KV caches at once.

Finally, everything has a price. A deployment of \(G\) GPUs rented at \(c\) dollars per GPU-hour that sustains a throughput of \(\Lambda\) tokens per second costs

\[ \text{cost per million tokens} = \frac{G\cdot c}{\Lambda\cdot 3600}\times10^{6}\ \text{dollars}. \]
Drill 1.2 — Cost per million tokens

An 8×H100 server rents for $2.50 per GPU-hour and sustains 12,000 output tokens per second at your SLO. The cost is \(\frac{8\times 2.50}{12{,}000\times3600}\times10^{6}\approx \$0.46\) per million output tokens. Doubling throughput through better batching halves it. At billions of tokens per day, such factors are worth millions of dollars a year, which is why the industry hires inference engineers.

  1. Increases both per-user tokens/s and total throughput ~32×
  2. Increases total throughput by nearly 32× while per-user TPOT rises only slightly
  3. Leaves throughput unchanged
  4. Reduces TTFT for all requests
Weight reads are shared by the batch; while still memory-bound, step time grows slowly (mostly due to extra KV reads), so throughput scales almost linearly. Eventually compute or KV memory limits kick in.

Warm-up What is perplexity, and why is it a poor metric for comparing two LLMs with different tokenisers?

Perplexity is the exponentiated per-token cross-entropy on held-out data, \(\mathrm{PP}=\exp\big(-\tfrac1T\sum_t\log p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\big)\): the effective branching factor of the model's predictions. It depends on what a "token" is. A tokeniser with a larger vocabulary produces fewer, more informative tokens, so per-token loss rises even if the model assigns higher probability to the whole text. To compare, normalise the total negative log-likelihood by bytes or characters (bits per byte), or compare on downstream tasks. A strong answer also mentions that perplexity on a held-out corpus is only loosely related to usefulness after instruction tuning, and is sensitive to data contamination.

Warm-up Define TTFT, TPOT and throughput. Give one product where each is the primary concern.
  • TTFT — time from request arrival to the first output token (queueing + prefill). Primary for chat responsiveness and voice assistants, where silence is noticed after a few hundred milliseconds.
  • TPOT — mean time between subsequent output tokens. Primary for long streamed outputs, reasoning models, and agents whose total latency is dominated by generation.
  • Throughput — tokens (or requests) per second for the whole deployment. Primary for offline batch jobs (summarising a document corpus, synthetic data generation, evaluation, RL rollouts), where latency does not matter but cost does.

Mention the trade-off: larger batches raise throughput but hurt TPOT, and queueing hurts TTFT; real systems optimise goodput under P90/P99 SLOs.

Core Why is LLM decoding memory-bandwidth bound? Give the back-of-the-envelope argument.

Each decode step processes one new token per sequence. For every weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) we compute \(Wx\) for a batch of \(B\) vectors: \(2mnB\) FLOPs, but we must read all \(mn\) weights (\(2mn\) bytes in BF16). Arithmetic intensity ≈ \(B\) FLOPs/byte. An H100 delivers ~989 TFLOP/s against 3.35 TB/s, a ridge point of ~295 FLOPs/byte, so for \(B\ll 300\) the arithmetic units wait on memory. Step time ≈ (weight bytes + KV-cache bytes) / bandwidth. In addition, attention over the KV cache has intensity ~1 FLOP/byte per sequence regardless of batch (each sequence has its own cache)—so even large batches remain partially memory-bound in attention. Consequences: quantisation, larger batches, GQA/MLA, and speculative decoding all help; adding FLOPs does not.

Core Estimate the maximum single-stream tokens/s of a 70B dense model in BF16 on 2×H100 with tensor parallelism.

Weights: 140 GB, split across two GPUs (70 GB each). Each GPU reads its 70 GB per step at 3.35 TB/s → ≈ 21 ms per step if both work in parallel. Add KV-cache reads: Llama-2-70B with GQA stores \(2\times80\times8\times128\times2\) B = 320 KB per token, so a 4k-token context adds ≈ 1.3 GB (0.65 GB per GPU) — small. Add two all-reduces per layer (160 total) of a few KB each; with NVLink each costs ~10–20 µs of latency, ≈ 2–3 ms total. Estimate ≈ 23–25 ms per token → ~40–45 tokens/s upper bound; at realistic MBU (~70–80%) roughly 30–35 tokens/s. Then discuss: FP8 roughly doubles it, speculative decoding can give 2–3× more, larger batches raise throughput but not single-stream speed.

Core Contrast RNNs and Transformers from an inference-systems perspective.

RNN: constant-size state \(h_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\) per sequence, \(O(d^2)\) FLOPs per token, independent of history length; trivially batchable; memory per user is tiny. But training is sequential over time and long-range recall is lossy (everything must be compressed into \(h\)).

Transformer: parallel training across positions; exact access to any past token. At inference it must store keys and values for every past token in every layer: \(2\,L\,n_{kv}\,d_h\) values per token, growing linearly with context; attention FLOPs per token also grow linearly. This state limits concurrency (batch size), dominates memory at long context, and is the target of MQA/GQA/MLA, paging, prefix caching, quantisation, eviction and offloading. Modern hybrids (Mamba-style SSM layers interleaved with some attention layers) try to get the best of both.

Core Explain the Chinchilla scaling result and why most production models deliberately violate it.

Hoffmann et al. fitted \(\mathcal L(N,D)=E+c_N N^{-\alpha_N}+c_D D^{-\alpha_D}\) and minimised it under \(C=6ND\), finding \(N_{\text{opt}},D_{\text{opt}}\propto C^{\approx0.5}\), about 20 tokens per parameter; Chinchilla-70B trained on 1.4T tokens beat Gopher-280B at equal compute. But this minimises training FLOPs only. Total cost is \(6ND_{\text{train}}+2ND_{\text{inf}}\) (plus memory and latency costs that also scale with \(N\)). For heavily-served models, a smaller \(N\) trained on many more tokens achieves the same loss with much cheaper inference, lower latency, and the ability to run on fewer or smaller GPUs. Hence LLaMA-7B (1T tokens), Llama 3 8B (15T tokens, ~1,900 tokens/param), and the general "over-training" trend; Sardana & Frankle formalised the inference-aware optimum. Caveat: returns diminish, and data quality/availability becomes the constraint.

Core What is the difference between throughput and goodput, and why might optimising throughput hurt users?

Throughput counts all tokens or requests completed per second. Goodput counts only requests that meet the SLOs (e.g. P90 TTFT ≤ 1 s and P90 TPOT ≤ 50 ms). Maximising throughput pushes toward huge batches and long queues: GPU utilisation goes up, but each step slows (higher TPOT) and new requests wait for capacity (higher TTFT). Beyond some load, throughput still rises while goodput collapses because most requests miss their SLOs. Capacity planning should therefore pick the maximum load at which goodput is still near the arrival rate, and use scheduling (chunked prefill, priorities, disaggregation) to push that point up.

Core Why are output tokens typically priced 3–5× higher than input tokens, and why are cached input tokens cheaper still?

Input tokens are processed in prefill, a single forward pass over all prompt tokens. Each weight read is amortised across thousands of tokens, so prefill is compute-bound and runs near peak FLOP/s. Output tokens are produced one decode step at a time; each step re-reads all weights and the KV cache and is amortised only over the current batch, so GPU-seconds per token are much higher, and long outputs also occupy KV memory for longer, reducing concurrency. Cached input: if a prefix (system prompt, document, conversation history) was recently processed, its KV cache can be reused from GPU/CPU memory or storage, skipping its prefill compute entirely; the provider passes on part of the saving and encourages cache-friendly prompts.

Deep-dive Derive the compute-optimal \(N\) and \(D\) from the Chinchilla loss \(\mathcal L=E+c_NN^{-\alpha_N}+c_DD^{-\alpha_D}\) under \(C=6ND\).

Substitute \(D=C/(6N)\): \(f(N)=c_NN^{-\alpha_N}+c_D(6N/C)^{\alpha_D}\). Then \(f'(N)=-\alpha_Nc_NN^{-\alpha_N-1}+\alpha_Dc_D(6/C)^{\alpha_D}N^{\alpha_D-1}=0\), so \(N^{\alpha_N+\alpha_D}=\frac{\alpha_Nc_N}{\alpha_Dc_D}\left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{\alpha_D}\), giving \(N_{\text{opt}}=G\,(C/6)^{\alpha_D/(\alpha_N+\alpha_D)}\) with \(G=(\alpha_Nc_N/\alpha_Dc_D)^{1/(\alpha_N+\alpha_D)}\), and \(D_{\text{opt}}=C/(6N_{\text{opt}})=G^{-1}(C/6)^{\alpha_N/(\alpha_N+\alpha_D)}\). Because \(f\) is the sum of a decreasing and an increasing power of \(N\), it is unimodal, so this is the minimum. With \(\alpha_N=0.34,\alpha_D=0.28\) the exponents are 0.45 and 0.55; Hoffmann et al.'s other estimation approaches give about 0.5 each. For the inference-aware version, add \(2ND_{\text{inf}}\) to the cost and minimise total cost subject to \(\mathcal L(N,D)=\mathcal L^{*}\). The optimum moves to smaller \(N\) and larger \(D\).

Deep-dive Why do the ridge points of successive GPUs keep rising, and what does that imply for LLM inference algorithm design?

FLOP/s scale with transistor count and specialised units (tensor cores, lower precisions: FP16→FP8→FP4 roughly doubles throughput each time), while bandwidth is limited by the number of pins and stacks, signalling rates and packaging (HBM generations). So peak FLOP/s per byte of bandwidth trends upward (P100 ≈ 29, H100 ≈ 295 in BF16; much higher in FP8/FP4). Implications: (1) algorithms must raise arithmetic intensity—bigger batches, fusion, FlashAttention-style tiling, GQA/MLA (more queries per KV byte), speculative decoding (verify several tokens per weight read); (2) reduce bytes—quantisation of weights and KV; (3) exploit memory hierarchy (SRAM reuse); (4) accept that some phases (decode attention) will be bandwidth-bound and design hardware/scheduling around it, e.g. disaggregating prefill (compute-bound) and decode (bandwidth-bound) onto differently provisioned pools.

Deep-dive How did reasoning models change inference engineering priorities?

(1) Token mix flips from prefill-heavy to decode-heavy; decode is bandwidth-bound, so TPOT and decode throughput dominate cost. (2) KV cache grows during generation to tens of thousands of tokens per request, limiting batch size and making KV compression/offload important—but many eviction methods were designed for long inputs and degrade reasoning accuracy on long outputs. (3) Latency translates into capability: under a fixed time budget, faster decode allows longer thinking; providers expose "reasoning effort" knobs. (4) Speculative decoding becomes more valuable (long sequential generations). (5) Variance in output length grows, complicating scheduling (stragglers, preemption). (6) RL post-training uses massive rollout generation, so inference engines are embedded in training stacks, with weight-sync between trainer and inference engine as a new problem.

Deep-dive Little's law and capacity: you must serve 30 req/s with mean 1,500 input and 500 output tokens, at P50 TPOT ≈ 40 ms. Roughly how many concurrent sequences must the system hold, and what does that imply for KV memory for an 8B GQA model?

Time in system per request ≈ TTFT (say 0.5 s) + 500×0.04 s = 20.5 s. Little: \(L=\lambda W=30\times20.5\approx 615\) concurrent requests. KV cache per token for a Llama-3-8B-like model (32 layers, 8 KV heads, head dim 128, BF16) = \(2\times32\times8\times128\times2=131{,}072\) B = 128 KiB. Average live context ≈ 1,500 + 250 (half the output on average) = 1,750 tokens → ≈ 224 MiB per request → ≈ 134 GiB for 615 requests, plus 16 GB of weights. So you need at least two 80 GB GPUs worth of memory (more in practice for headroom and fragmentation), or FP8 KV cache, or more replicas. Then check compute: 30 req/s × 2,000 tokens × 2×8e9 FLOPs ≈ 1 PFLOP/s, so ~2–4 H100s at realistic utilisation. Conclusion: a handful of GPUs, likely 2–4 replicas; memory, not FLOPs, sets the replica count.

Core Name five inference optimisations from 2019–2025 and the specific bottleneck each attacks.
  • MQA/GQA (2019/2023) — KV cache size and bandwidth: share K/V across query heads.
  • FlashAttention (2022–26) — HBM traffic of materialising the \(T\times T\) score matrix: tile in SRAM with online softmax.
  • Continuous batching (Orca, 2022) — idle slots when batched requests finish at different times: schedule per iteration.
  • PagedAttention (vLLM, 2023) — KV memory fragmentation/over-reservation: fixed-size blocks with a block table.
  • Speculative decoding (2022–25) — sequential, low-intensity decode: draft many tokens cheaply and verify them in one pass, exactly preserving the distribution.
  • Quantisation (GPTQ/AWQ/FP8/FP4) — weight bytes and bandwidth (and compute for activation quantisation).
  • Chunked prefill / disaggregation (2024) — long prefills stalling decodes; prefill and decode needing different resources.
  • MoE (2017–25) — FLOPs per token: activate a small subset of parameters per token.

If generation (TPOT) dominates, as it usually does for long answers, the workload is memory-bandwidth bound at the batch sizes needed for latency; doubling TFLOPs with the same bandwidth would barely help. Design Sketch, at a high level, what happens between a user pressing Enter in a chat app and seeing tokens stream back.
  1. Client sends HTTPS request (messages, parameters) to an API gateway: authentication, rate limiting, routing to a model deployment—ideally to a replica that already holds the conversation's KV cache (prefix-aware routing).
  2. Server applies the chat template and tokenises; checks the prefix cache for reusable KV blocks.
  3. Scheduler admits the request when KV memory is available; runs prefill for the uncached part of the prompt (possibly chunked and interleaved with others' decode steps, or on a separate prefill pool with KV transferred to a decode pool).
  4. The first token is sampled from the logits (temperature/top-p, possibly grammar-constrained); TTFT is measured here.
  5. The request joins the continuous decode batch; each iteration runs one forward pass for all running sequences, appends K/V to paged cache blocks, samples next tokens.
  6. Tokens are incrementally detokenised and streamed (server-sent events); stop conditions (EOS, stop strings, max tokens) end the request; KV blocks are freed or retained in the prefix cache.
  7. Metrics/traces recorded; billing counts input, cached-input and output tokens.

Warm-up What is the KV cache and why does it exist?

During autoregressive generation, each new token's query must attend to the keys and values of every previous token in every layer. Those keys and values depend only on earlier tokens (causal masking), so they never change once computed. The KV cache stores them so each decode step computes K and V only for the new token and reads the rest from memory—turning per-step cost from re-processing the whole prefix into a single-token forward pass plus attention over cached vectors. Its size is \(2\cdot L\cdot n_{kv}\cdot d_h\cdot\text{bytes}\) per token per sequence (Chapter 2), which can exceed the model weights at long contexts or high concurrency, making it the central resource that serving systems manage.
