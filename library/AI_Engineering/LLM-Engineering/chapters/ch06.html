Part II · The Machine — Chapter 6

The Roofline Model and Performance Analysis

A pen-and-paper model that predicts the speed of any kernel, a way to measure how far real code falls short of it, and the habits of mind that turn a profiler trace into a diagnosis.

Why this chapter

Chapter 3 introduced the roofline in its simplest form, one compute ceiling \(\pi\) and one bandwidth slope \(\beta\), and used it to explain why decode is memory-bound. Chapter 5 then showed that the machine has much more structure than two numbers. There are several compute ceilings (tensor cores at different precisions, and the far weaker general-purpose lanes) and several bandwidths (registers, shared memory, L2, HBM, NVLink, the network). Real kernels rarely reach any of these limits, and when a serving system is slower than expected, an engineer needs to know which limit it is up against and how far from it it is. Guessing produces optimisations that do nothing. A model plus measurement produces optimisations that work.

This chapter turns the roofline from an illustration into a working tool. We first restate it precisely and extend it to multiple ceilings and multiple memory levels, the hierarchical roofline. We then place every operation of an LLM on it, and use it to answer one of the most consequential questions in inference: at what batch size, and at what context length, does decoding become compute-bound? The answer, the critical batch size, turns out to depend dramatically on the KV cache. For Llama 3 8B, beyond a context of only a few hundred tokens, decode can never become compute-bound on an H100, however large the batch. We then define the two utilisation metrics everyone quotes, MFU and MBU, and show how to compute them from a benchmark. We close with how profilers expose the gap between model and reality, and with the effects the roofline does not capture.

Learning objectives
  1. State the roofline bound with multiple compute ceilings and explain its log–log geometry.
  2. Build a hierarchical roofline and identify which memory level limits a kernel.
  3. Compute the arithmetic intensity of each LLM operator: GEMM, skinny GEMM, attention in prefill and decode, normalisation, element-wise ops, and communication.
  4. Derive the critical batch size for decode including KV-cache traffic, and the critical context length beyond which decode can never be compute-bound.
  5. Draw up a roofline budget for a whole decode step and choose an operating point on the latency–throughput frontier under a TPOT target.
  6. Compute MFU and MBU from a benchmark and interpret them correctly for prefill and decode.
  7. Read a profiler timeline and diagnose compute-, memory- or overhead-bound behaviour, and recognise effects outside the roofline (wave quantisation, launch latency, clock throttling).

6.1 The roofline, precisely

The roofline model of Williams, Waterman and Patterson (2009)[1] rests on one assumption: a kernel's time is bounded below by the time to do its arithmetic and by the time to move its data, and with enough overlap it approaches the larger of the two. If a kernel performs \(F\) FLOPs and moves \(Q\) bytes between HBM and the chip, then

\[ t \ \ge\ \max\Big(\frac{F}{\pi},\ \frac{Q}{\beta}\Big) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{F}{t}\ \le\ \min\big(\pi,\ I\beta\big),\quad I=\frac{F}{Q}. \]

On log–log axes, with intensity \(I\) horizontal and attainable FLOP/s vertical, \(I\beta\) is a line of slope one and \(\pi\) is a horizontal line. The bound is the lower envelope of the two, a "roof" with a sloped section and a flat section that meet at the ridge point \(I^{*}=\pi/\beta\). Its usefulness comes from what the axes separate. \(I\) is a property of the algorithm and its implementation: how many operations it performs per byte it fetches. \(\pi\) and \(\beta\) are properties of the machine. The roofline tells us, before writing any code, which property limits us.

Chapter 5 showed that a GPU has several compute ceilings, not one. On an H100 the dense tensor-core ceiling is 989 TFLOP/s in BF16 and twice that in FP8, while any work that cannot use tensor cores is limited by the FP32 lanes at about 62 TFLOP/s. The roofline accommodates this naturally. Each class of operation has its own ceiling, and the ridge point is different for each.

Figure 6.1 draws the H100's three main ceilings over a common bandwidth slope. Two observations follow immediately. First, a lower-precision ceiling raises the roof but also moves the ridge to the right. FP8's ridge is at about 590 FLOPs per byte, so more workloads become memory-bound when you switch to FP8, unless the bytes shrink too. That is why FP8 inference stores weights in FP8, which halves \(Q\) and doubles \(I\), instead of only computing in FP8. Second, the FP32 ceiling is low enough that operations which cannot use tensor cores become compute-bound at modest intensities. The exponentials in a softmax, for example, have their own even lower ceiling set by the special-function units, which is the bottleneck FlashAttention-4 was designed around (Chapter 9).

6.2 The hierarchical roofline

The basic roofline counts only bytes moved to and from HBM. But Chapter 5 showed that data also moves between L2 and the SMs, and between shared memory and registers, each with its own bandwidth. A kernel can be limited by any of these. The hierarchical roofline[2] handles this by computing a separate intensity for each level \(\ell\) of the hierarchy, \(I_\ell=F/Q_\ell\), where \(Q_\ell\) is the traffic at that level, and applying the bound at every level simultaneously:

\[ \frac{F}{t}\ \le\ \min\Big(\pi,\ \min_{\ell}\ I_\ell\,\beta_\ell\Big). \]

A well-tiled kernel has very different intensities at different levels. Tiling in shared memory makes HBM traffic small (high \(I_{\text{HBM}}\)), but every tile is read from shared memory into registers many times (lower \(I_{\text{SMEM}}\)). The level that binds is the one where \(I_\ell\beta_\ell\) is smallest.

Figure 6.2 overlays roofs for HBM, L2 and shared memory. Because shared memory is about ten times faster than HBM, its ridge point is about ten times further left, around 32 FLOPs per byte. The two purple points are one hypothetical tiled GEMM. Measured at HBM, where each element is fetched once per tile and reused, its intensity is in the thousands. Measured at shared memory, where each tile element is read into registers once per use within the tile, its intensity is about 64. Both points sit on the flat part of their roofs, so the kernel can in principle reach peak. Had its shared-memory intensity been below 32, shared-memory bandwidth would have been the binding constraint, even though HBM looked comfortable. This is a real situation for small tiles. It is also why fast kernels keep a second level of tiling in registers, and why Blackwell's tensor memory (§5.5) relieves pressure on shared memory. The lesson generalises: a kernel is limited by the weakest level it touches, and "memory-bound" should always be qualified by which memory.

Four lessons are packed into this table. First, only the matrix products of prefill and of large-batch decode are naturally compute-bound. Everything else is memory-bound, so its cost is the bytes it moves, not the FLOPs it performs. Second, the memory-bound element-wise operations (norms, activations, residual additions) each read and write the whole activation tensor. Executed as separate kernels, they each pay a full round-trip to HBM for a handful of FLOPs. Fusing them into the neighbouring matrix product, or into each other, removes those round-trips entirely. This is the single most effective optimisation for them, and the subject of Chapter 7. Third, attention changes character between phases. With FlashAttention, prefill attention reads \(Q\), \(K\) and \(V\) once and never writes the \(T\times T\) score matrix to HBM, so its intensity grows linearly with \(T\) and it is compute-bound. Decode attention has intensity \(g\) regardless of anything else. Fourth, the embedding lookup has zero arithmetic intensity, but it is tiny: one row per token.

Drill 6.1 — Where does a prefill spend its time?

Llama 3 8B, prefill of 4,096 tokens on an H100. Estimate the time of (a) all linear layers, (b) attention, (c) all RMSNorms, at roofline speed.

Solution. (a) \(2\times7.5\times10^{9}\times4096\approx6.1\times10^{13}\) FLOPs ÷ 989 TFLOP/s ≈ 62 ms (compute-bound). (b) \(2Ld\,T^2\approx 2\times32\times4096\times4096^2\approx4.4\times10^{12}\) FLOPs ≈ 4.4 ms. (c) Each of the \(2L+1=65\) RMSNorms reads and writes \(4096\times4096\times2\) B = 32 MiB, so about \(65\times2\times33.5\) MB ≈ 4.4 GB at 3.35 TB/s ≈ 1.3 ms. The norms perform a negligible fraction of the FLOPs yet cost about 2% of the time. Unfused residual additions and activations would add similar amounts. This is why fusion matters even in compute-bound phases.

6.4 The critical batch size, including the KV cache

Batching is the main lever for decode throughput (§3.6). It shares each weight read among \(B\) sequences, raising intensity until compute becomes the limit. At what batch size does that happen? Chapter 3 gave the answer for the weights alone: \(B^{*}\approx I^{*}b/2\), about 295 on an H100. But Chapter 3 also showed that the KV cache adds traffic proportional to \(B\), which batching cannot amortise. Including it changes the answer completely.

For a decode step with \(B\) sequences at context \(t\), set compute time equal to memory time, using the latency model of §3.6 and ignoring the small attention FLOP term:

\[ \frac{2N_{\text{mm}}B}{\pi} = \frac{Nb_w + B\,t\,s_{kv}}{\beta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B^{*}(t) = \frac{Nb_w/\beta}{\;2N_{\text{mm}}/\pi \;-\; t\,s_{kv}/\beta\;} . \]

Read the denominator as a race between two per-sequence costs. \(2N_{\text{mm}}/\pi\) is the compute time each additional sequence adds, and \(t\,s_{kv}/\beta\) is the time to read its cache. If the cache read takes longer than the compute, then each added sequence adds more memory time than compute time, and no batch size, however large, makes the step compute-bound. The denominator vanishes at a critical context length:

\[ t_{\text{crit}} = \frac{2N_{\text{mm}}\,\beta}{\pi\, s_{kv}} = \frac{2N_{\text{mm}}}{I^{*} s_{kv}} . \]

For \(t\ge t_{\text{crit}}\), decode is memory-bound at every batch size.

Worked example — the running examples on an H100

Llama 3 8B (BF16 cache, \(s_{kv}=128\) KiB): \(t_{\text{crit}}=2\times7.5\times10^9/(295\times131{,}072)\approx 388\) tokens. With an FP8 cache, about 775.

Llama 3 70B (\(s_{kv}=320\) KiB, \(N_{\text{mm}}\approx69.5\)B): \(t_{\text{crit}}\approx 1{,}437\) tokens; with an FP8 cache about 2,870.

Typical chat contexts are thousands of tokens and reasoning traces tens of thousands. In production, decode for these models on H100s is memory-bound essentially always. Throughput is governed by bytes, both weights and cache, and by the batch size that memory capacity allows, not by FLOP/s.

Figure 6.3 plots \(B^{*}(t)\) on a logarithmic axis for both models. At zero context, both need a batch of about 300, the weights-only answer. As the context grows, the required batch climbs and then diverges: Llama 3 8B's asymptote is at 388 tokens, and Llama 3 70B's at about 1,437. Two architectural facts explain why the larger model tolerates longer contexts. It has many more weights to amortise per token of cache (\(N_{\text{mm}}/s_{kv}\) is larger), and its larger GQA group (\(g=8\) versus 4) makes its cache relatively smaller. The formula also tells us exactly which levers could move decode back towards compute-bound: a smaller \(s_{kv}\) (GQA, MLA, FP8 or INT4 caches; Chapter 8), more FLOPs per sequence per cache byte (speculative decoding, which verifies several tokens per cache read; Chapter 20), or hardware with a lower ridge point.

A classic interview question

"At what batch size does Llama 70B decode become compute-bound on an H100?" The textbook answer, "about 300", ignores the cache. The strong answer derives \(B^{*}(t)\), notes that for realistic contexts (above about 1,400 tokens in BF16) the answer is "never", and concludes that the operating point is chosen by memory capacity and the TPOT target, not by FLOPs.

6.5 A roofline budget for a whole decode step

The roofline is most useful when applied not to one kernel but to an entire step, because the step's time is the sum of its parts, and optimising a part that is already small cannot help much. This is Amdahl's law, and the way to respect it is to draw up a budget: for each class of operation, its necessary bytes and FLOPs, its roofline time, and its share of the total. Here is that budget for our running example in a representative serving configuration.

The budget makes priorities obvious. Weight reads and cache reads split the step roughly evenly, and everything else is noise in bytes. That does not mean the small operations are free. In an unoptimised implementation each of them is a separate kernel launch, and the roughly 250 launches of a decode step can cost a millisecond or more of overhead (§3.4). But it does mean that, once overhead is handled, the only ways to make this step substantially faster are to read fewer weight bytes (quantisation, Chapter 14), read fewer cache bytes (Chapters 8 and 14), read them faster (better kernels reaching higher MBU, Chapter 9; or faster memory), or produce more than one token per read (Chapter 20). At 80% MBU the step takes about 12 ms, which matches the latency model of §3.6.

Drill 6.2 — The same budget at batch 8 and 32k context

Redo the budget for 8 sequences at 32,768 tokens each.

Solution. Weights: still 15.0 GB, 4.48 ms. Cache: \(8\times32{,}768\times131{,}072\) B ≈ 34.4 GB, 10.3 ms. Total ≈ 14.8 ms, with 70% now in attention. A long-context workload is dominated by the cache, and halving it (FP8) is worth almost 35% of the step. Quantising the weights instead would gain only 15%. The optimal optimisation depends on the workload, which is why the budget must be drawn for production traffic, not for a benchmark.

The MI300X and H200 beat the H100 despite similar or identical compute. Two caveats keep this from being the whole story. First, these are ideal times. Comparing accelerators for inference therefore means comparing bandwidth, capacity and achieved MBU together, and then dividing by price, which is what the cost-per-token calculations of Chapter 27 do.

6.6 Choosing the operating point

The roofline and the budget tell us the time of a step at a given batch size. A serving system must choose the batch size, and with it the trade-off between per-user speed and total throughput introduced in §3.6. We can now make that choice systematically. Suppose the product requires a TPOT of at most 20 ms (50 tokens per second per user, comfortably faster than reading). Using the latency model at a realistic 80% MBU, the step time for Llama 3 8B at 2,048-token contexts is \((16.06+0.268B)/2.68\) ms. Setting this to 20 ms gives \(B\le140\). Within that limit, throughput is maximised at the largest feasible batch, provided memory allows it: about 140 sequences, giving roughly 7,000 tokens per second.

Figure 6.7 draws the whole frontier. Each point is a batch size, plotted by the total throughput it delivers and the TPOT each user experiences. The BF16 curve rises steeply: beyond batch 128 every additional sequence adds almost as much time as it adds throughput. The horizontal line is the SLO, and the best operating point is where each curve crosses it. The orange curve shows the same system with an FP8 cache. Its slope is half as steep, because each sequence adds half as many bytes, so it crosses the SLO at roughly twice the throughput. This is how optimisations should be evaluated: not by how much they speed up an isolated kernel, but by how far they move the frontier at the latency the product requires. The same picture also explains why goodput (§1.13) is the right summary metric. Points to the right of the crossing deliver more raw throughput, but every request served there violates the SLO.

Where GPU time goes across a whole request

The frontier concerns decode, but a serving GPU also runs prefills, and a budget for a whole request reveals something that the per-step view hides. Take the chat workload of §3.8: 2,000 prompt tokens, 500 output tokens, Llama 3 8B on one H100, about 64 sequences decoding concurrently. The request's prefill occupies the GPU for about 63 ms. Its decode is spread over 500 steps of about 13 ms each, but each step is shared with 63 other sequences, so the request's share of decode GPU time is \(500\times13/64\approx102\) ms. Prefill is therefore about 38% of the GPU time the request consumes, even though it is only 1% of the request's latency (63 ms out of 6.6 s).

This asymmetry has two consequences that shape Part IV. First, prefill is not a minor cost to be squeezed in. At realistic input-to-output ratios it is a large fraction of the work, and because it is compute-bound while decode is memory-bound, the two phases contend for different resources of the same GPU. Running them together wastes some of each: during a long prefill the bandwidth sits underused, and during decode the compute does. That observation motivates chunked prefill (Chapter 11) and the disaggregation of prefill and decode onto separate pools of GPUs (Chapter 13). Second, any technique that avoids prefill work, above all reusing cached prefixes (Chapter 10), saves a large share of total GPU time, not just latency. A system prompt of 1,500 tokens shared by every request, if cached, removes three-quarters of this workload's prefill cost.

6.7 Measuring how close you are: MFU and MBU

The roofline gives an upper bound. To know whether an implementation is good, we compare achieved performance with that bound, and two ratios have become standard. Model FLOPs utilisation (MFU), popularised by the PaLM paper[3], is the useful FLOP/s achieved divided by peak:

\[ \text{MFU} = \frac{F_{\text{model}}/t}{\pi}, \]

where \(F_{\text{model}}\) counts only the FLOPs the model mathematically requires (§2.10), not recomputation, padding or wasted work. Memory-bandwidth utilisation (MBU), its analogue for memory-bound phases, is the bytes that must necessarily be moved per second divided by peak bandwidth:

\[ \text{MBU} = \frac{Q_{\text{model}}/t}{\beta},\qquad Q_{\text{model}} = N b_w + \sum_{\text{seq}} t_{\text{seq}}\,s_{kv}\ \text{ per decode step}. \]
Worked example — computing MBU from a benchmark

A benchmark reports Llama 3 8B in BF16 on one H100 decoding 32 sequences of 1,024-token context at 7.9 ms per step. Necessary bytes per step: 16.06 GB of weights plus \(32\times1024\times131{,}072\) B ≈ 4.29 GB of cache, 20.35 GB in total. Achieved bandwidth = 20.35 GB / 7.9 ms ≈ 2.58 TB/s, so MBU ≈ 2.58/3.35 ≈ 77%. MFU for the same step is \(2\times7.5\times10^{9}\times32/0.0079\) ≈ 61 TFLOP/s, about 6%. Both numbers describe the same, well-optimised step.

The example exposes a common error. Reporting "6% GPU utilisation" for a decode step suggests a badly written system. In fact the step is within a quarter of its true limit, which is bandwidth. MFU is the right metric for compute-bound phases (prefill, training) and MBU for memory-bound phases (decode). Good prefill implementations reach 40–70% MFU, and good decode implementations 60–85% MBU. A system that reports both, separately for each phase, is being honest. Neither metric captures latency, which is governed by the batching and scheduling choices of Part IV.

6.8 Profiling: from trace to diagnosis

When a system misses its expected performance, the profiler tells you why. Three tools cover most needs on NVIDIA hardware. Nsight Systems records a timeline of everything that happens: CPU threads, kernel launches, GPU kernels, memory copies, communication. Nsight Compute examines individual kernels in depth: achieved FLOP/s and bandwidth at each memory level, occupancy, stall reasons, and a roofline plot. The PyTorch profiler attributes GPU time to framework operators and Python code. Reading their output is a skill, and the roofline gives the framework for it.

Figure 6.4 contrasts two schematic timelines of a decode step. In the top one, the CPU launches kernels one at a time, and each GPU kernel is so short that the GPU sits idle between launches. The timeline looks like a comb. No amount of kernel optimisation will help, because the kernels are not the bottleneck. In the bottom one, the step has been captured as a CUDA graph and the small kernels fused, so the GPU executes one dense block of work while the CPU prepares the next step in parallel. With that picture in mind, a practical diagnosis proceeds in three questions. Is the GPU busy? If there are gaps, the problem is overhead: use CUDA graphs, fusion, asynchronous scheduling. If it is busy, which kernels take the time? Rank them, and compare each kernel's achieved FLOP/s and bandwidth with its roofline. A kernel far below both roofs is latency-bound (too little parallelism, too many synchronisations), the subject of the next section. A kernel near one roof is as fast as it can be in its current form, and the only remedy is to change the algorithm: raise its intensity, reduce its bytes or its FLOPs.

Figure 6.5 condenses this procedure into a decision tree, and it is worth internalising because it prevents the most common waste of engineering effort: optimising a kernel that is not on the critical path, or tuning a kernel that is already at its roof. A good habit is to write down the roofline prediction before profiling. The size of the gap between prediction and measurement tells you whether you misunderstood the workload or the implementation.

6.9 What the roofline does not see

The roofline is an upper bound, and real kernels can fall short of it for reasons the model ignores. Four matter routinely in LLM inference.

Wave quantisation. A kernel's work is divided into tiles, each processed by one thread block on one SM. An H100 has 132 SMs. If a matrix product produces 133 output tiles, the first 132 run in parallel as one "wave" and the last runs alone in a second wave while 131 SMs idle. The kernel takes two waves' time for 133/264 ≈ 50% of the work it could have done. Small decode matrices are especially prone to this, because their total tile count is small. Remedies include choosing tile sizes that divide the work evenly, splitting the reduction dimension across more blocks ("split-K"), and persistent kernels that schedule tiles dynamically.

Figure 6.6 shows the worst case, and it explains a puzzle that confuses many newcomers: making a matrix very slightly larger can make a kernel almost twice as slow. Launch and synchronisation latency. Every kernel launch costs a few microseconds of CPU time, and every synchronisation (between thread blocks, between the CPU and GPU, between GPUs) costs latency that no bandwidth or FLOP figure captures. At small batch, decode is often a sequence of hundreds of kernels lasting tens of microseconds each, and this latency adds up (§3.4). Clock and power throttling. Peak FLOP/s assumes the rated clock, but a GPU running dense tensor work at its power limit may lower its clock. Sustained throughput can sit 10–20% below the datasheet figure, so measure achieved clocks. Communication. In multi-GPU inference, collectives add their own roofline: bytes divided by link bandwidth, plus a per-message latency that dominates for the small messages of decode (Chapter 17). A tensor-parallel decode step can be limited by the latency of its 160 all-reduces rather than by any bandwidth.

It helps to see these effects add up on a single number. Figure 6.8 decomposes the time of one batch-1 decode step of Llama 3 8B on an H100 into the roofline floor and the losses stacked on top of it. The floor is the weight read alone: 16.06 GB at 3.35 TB/s is 4.8 ms. A good implementation measures about 6.5 ms, an MBU of 74%. The figure is schematic, since the exact split depends on the engine and driver, but its proportions are typical. The largest single loss is that no real kernel streams HBM at the datasheet rate; roughly 90% is achievable, which costs half a millisecond before anything else goes wrong. Launch and synchronisation gaps come next, and they are exactly what CUDA graphs (Chapter 7) remove. Small unfused operators and wave tails account for the rest. Reading the figure from right to left is the profiling procedure of Figure 6.5: first remove idle time, then fuse, then tune tiles, and accept that the last 10% is the hardware's.

For the mathematician — the roofline as a bound, not a model

The roofline is a lower bound on time, \(t\ge\max(F/\pi,Q/\beta)\), derived from two capacity constraints. It becomes a good prediction only when the implementation overlaps compute and data movement perfectly, keeps enough work in flight to hide latency (Little's law, §5.1), and incurs no other costs. Treat it like a lower bound in optimisation: it tells you how much improvement is possible, and which constraint is active, but not how to reach it.

Looking ahead

The roofline told us that most of an LLM's non-matmul operations are memory-bound and that fusing them removes their cost, that tiling raises intensity at one level of the hierarchy and moves the pressure to the next, and that decode spends its time reading bytes. All of these conclusions end the same way: write a better kernel. Fuse these operations, tile that loop, overlap this copy with that multiply. For a mathematician without a systems background, that is where the black box remains. Chapter 7 opens it, showing how GPU kernels are actually written, from a first matrix-multiply in CUDA to the Triton language that most LLM engineers use, and how the online softmax makes fused attention possible.

Chapter summary

  • Roofline: \(F/t\le\min(\pi, I\beta)\), with \(I=F/Q\). Each class of operation has its own ceiling (BF16 989, FP8 1,979, FP32 lanes ≈ 62 TFLOP/s on H100) and therefore its own ridge point.
  • Hierarchical roofline: compute \(I_\ell\) at every memory level and take the minimum of \(I_\ell\beta_\ell\). A kernel is limited by the weakest level it touches.
  • LLM operators: prefill GEMMs and prefill attention are compute-bound; decode GEMMs have \(I\approx B\); decode attention has \(I=2g/b\); norms, activations and softmax have \(I\approx1\), which is why fusion matters.
  • Critical batch size with KV traffic: \(B^{*}(t)=(Nb_w/\beta)/(2N_{\text{mm}}/\pi - ts_{kv}/\beta)\). It diverges at \(t_{\text{crit}}=2N_{\text{mm}}/(I^{*}s_{kv})\): 388 tokens for Llama 3 8B and about 1,437 for Llama 3 70B on H100 (BF16). Beyond it, decode is memory-bound at any batch size.
  • A roofline budget sums per-component times; for Llama 3 8B at batch 64 and 2k context, weights and cache each take about half of a ≈ 9.7 ms ideal step. The operating point is the largest batch that meets the TPOT target; an FP8 cache roughly doubles throughput at the same TPOT.
  • MFU for compute-bound phases, MBU for memory-bound phases. Good values: 40–70% MFU in prefill, 60–85% MBU in decode.
  • Profiling: check for idle gaps (overhead), then compare dominant kernels with their roofs; kernels below every roof are latency-bound.
  • Outside the roofline: wave quantisation, launch and synchronisation latency, clock throttling, and communication latency.

Warm-up Explain the roofline model and how you would use it before writing any code.

Time ≥ max(FLOPs/π, bytes/β), equivalently attainable FLOP/s ≤ min(π, Iβ) with I = FLOPs/byte. Before coding: count the kernel's necessary FLOPs and HBM bytes, compute I, compare with the ridge π/β for the relevant ceiling (tensor cores at the chosen precision, or the FP32 lanes). If I is below the ridge, expected time ≈ bytes/β, so optimise bytes: quantisation, fusion, reuse. If above, time ≈ FLOPs/π, so optimise FLOPs or use faster units. This sets a target to compare against the profiler, and prevents optimising the wrong resource.

Core At what batch size does Llama 3 70B decode become compute-bound on an H100?

Weights alone: intensity ≈ B in BF16, so B ≈ ridge ≈ 295. With the cache: equate \(2N_{\text{mm}}B/\pi\) with \((Nb_w+Bts_{kv})/\beta\), giving \(B^{*}(t)=(Nb_w/\beta)/(2N_{\text{mm}}/\pi-ts_{kv}/\beta)\). For 70B (\(N_{\text{mm}}\approx69.5\)B, \(s_{kv}=320\) KiB), the denominator vanishes at \(t_{\text{crit}}=2N_{\text{mm}}/(I^{*}s_{kv})\approx1{,}437\) tokens (≈ 2,870 with an FP8 cache). At 1,000 tokens \(B^{*}\approx990\), already far beyond memory capacity on a few GPUs. For typical chat or reasoning contexts the answer is "never": decode is bandwidth-bound and the batch is chosen by memory capacity and the TPOT target. (With tensor parallelism both sides scale per GPU, so the conclusion is unchanged.)

Core Define MFU and MBU. Which do you report for prefill and for decode, and why?

MFU = required model FLOPs per second ÷ peak FLOP/s. MBU = required bytes per second (weights + KV cache, read once each) ÷ peak bandwidth. Prefill is compute-bound, so MFU shows how close it is to its limit (40–70% is good). Decode is memory-bound, so its MFU is inherently low (a few percent) even when optimal, and MBU is the meaningful metric (60–85% is good). Report both per phase, at a stated batch size and context length, since both depend strongly on them.

Core What is a hierarchical roofline and when does it change your conclusions?

Compute intensity separately at each memory level (HBM, L2, shared memory, registers) using that level's traffic, and apply \(\min(\pi,\min_\ell I_\ell\beta_\ell)\). It changes conclusions for tiled kernels: tiling raises HBM intensity, but if tiles are small or re-read many times, shared-memory or L2 bandwidth can bind instead. Example: a GEMM with HBM intensity 2,000 but shared-memory intensity 20 is limited at ≈ 20 × 31 TB/s ≈ 620 TFLOP/s on H100. The fix is larger register tiles, or hardware such as Blackwell's tensor memory. It also explains FlashAttention's design choices: keep data in SRAM, but organise it so SRAM bandwidth is not the new limit.

Core Place each Transformer operation on the roofline for both prefill and decode.
  • Linear layers: intensity ≈ number of tokens sharing the weights (BF16). Prefill (thousands): compute-bound. Decode (= B): memory-bound below ≈ 300.
  • Attention: prefill with FlashAttention has intensity ∝ T (compute-bound for long prompts); decode has \(2g/b\) (memory-bound always).
  • Norms, residual adds, activations, softmax: intensity ≈ 1, memory-bound in both phases, so fuse them.
  • Embedding: zero intensity, negligible bytes.
  • LM head: a linear layer; only last-position rows in prefill.
  • Sampling: memory-bound passes over \(B\times|\mathcal V|\) logits.
  • Collectives (multi-GPU): bytes/link bandwidth plus latency, with latency dominating in decode.
Deep-dive Derive the critical context length and discuss what it implies for hardware and model design.

Per extra sequence in a decode batch: compute time \(2N_{\text{mm}}/\pi\), cache-read time \(ts_{kv}/\beta\). If the latter exceeds the former, increasing B adds more memory time than compute time, so the step can never become compute-bound. Hence \(t_{\text{crit}}=2N_{\text{mm}}\beta/(\pi s_{kv})=2N_{\text{mm}}/(I^{*}s_{kv})\). Implications: (1) hardware with a rising ridge point lowers \(t_{\text{crit}}\), so new GPUs make decode more memory-bound unless the cache shrinks; (2) model design should minimise \(s_{kv}\) relative to \(N_{\text{mm}}\) (GQA with larger groups, MLA, sliding windows, KV quantisation); (3) MoE models have small \(N_{\text{act}}\) per token, which lowers \(t_{\text{crit}}\) further, so their caches must be tiny (DeepSeek's MLA); (4) algorithms that raise FLOPs per cache read (speculative verification, multi-query batching of shared prefixes) are valuable.

Core A benchmark shows decode at 8 ms/step for 32 sequences × 1k context, Llama 3 8B on H100. Is it well optimised?

Necessary bytes: 16.06 GB weights + 32 × 1,024 × 128 KiB ≈ 4.3 GB cache ≈ 20.35 GB. Roofline time = 20.35 / 3.35 ≈ 6.1 ms. Achieved 8 ms gives MBU ≈ 76%, which is good. MFU ≈ 6%, irrelevant here. Remaining headroom might come from fusing small kernels, CUDA graphs, better attention kernels (to read the cache at full bandwidth) or overlapping sampling. Bigger wins require changing bytes: FP8 weights and cache would cut the roofline time to about 3 ms.

Core Walk through how you would profile a slow decode step.

(1) Predict: compute the roofline time from bytes and FLOPs. (2) Nsight Systems timeline: are there gaps on the GPU? If so, the step is overhead-bound (Python, launches, synchronisation, host–device copies, CPU sampling), so use CUDA graphs, fusion, asynchronous scheduling, and avoid .item()-style syncs. (3) If the GPU is busy, rank kernels by time. (4) For the top kernels, Nsight Compute: achieved DRAM bandwidth and FLOP/s versus roofs, occupancy, stall reasons, L2 hit rates. Near a roof means change the algorithm (quantise, fuse, better attention kernel); far below means latency-bound (wave quantisation, split-K, tile tuning, persistent kernels). (5) Check clocks and power throttling. (6) Multi-GPU: look at collective time and overlap. Re-measure after each change.

Deep-dive What is wave quantisation, and why does it particularly affect decode?

A kernel's tiles are processed in waves of (number of SMs × blocks per SM). If the tile count slightly exceeds a multiple of the wave size, the last wave runs nearly empty, and efficiency can drop by up to 50%. Decode GEMMs have small M (= B) and often small N per GPU under tensor parallelism, so they produce few tiles, and the quantisation effect is a large fraction of runtime. Mitigations: tile-size selection by autotuning per shape; split-K to create more parallel work along the reduction dimension; stream-K or persistent kernels that distribute work units dynamically; fusing several small GEMMs (e.g. Q, K, V) into one larger one.

Core Why does moving to a GPU with a higher ridge point not always speed up your workload proportionally to its FLOP/s?

Speed-up follows the binding resource. Memory-bound phases (decode, norms, decode attention) scale with bandwidth; compute-bound phases (prefill, large GEMMs) scale with FLOP/s. A higher ridge point means compute grew faster than bandwidth, so the memory-bound fraction of the workload grows, and the overall speed-up is a bandwidth-weighted average (Amdahl's law). Example: H100 → B200 gives about 2.3× BF16 FLOP/s but 2.4× bandwidth, so decode gains about 2.4×. H100 → H200 gives the same FLOP/s with 1.43× bandwidth, so decode gains about 1.4× and prefill about 1.0×.

Design Using roofline reasoning, decide whether FP8 KV cache or FP8 weights help more for Llama 3 8B decode at batch 64 and 4k context.

Bytes per step: weights 16.06 GB; cache 64 × 4,096 × 128 KiB ≈ 34.4 GB; total ≈ 50.4 GB, so about 15 ms at 3.35 TB/s. FP8 weights save 8 GB (−16%), giving 42.4 GB. An FP8 cache saves 17.2 GB (−34%), giving 33.2 GB, and doubles how many sequences fit. The cache is the larger lever at this context and batch; at batch 8 or short contexts weights would dominate. Both together give about 25 GB (≈ 2× faster). Check quality impact (KV quantisation error affects attention scores) and kernel support (attention kernels that read FP8 KV at full bandwidth). The roofline turns this into a two-line calculation.

Warm-up What is the difference between Nsight Systems and Nsight Compute?

Nsight Systems is a system-wide timeline profiler: CPU threads, API calls, kernel launches, GPU kernels, memory copies, NCCL communication, NVTX ranges. Use it to find idle time, synchronisation, overlap problems and which kernels dominate. Nsight Compute profiles individual kernels in depth: achieved throughput at each memory level, FLOP/s, occupancy, warp-stall reasons, instruction mix and a roofline chart. Use it to optimise a specific kernel. Typical workflow: Systems first to find what matters, Compute second to fix it. The PyTorch profiler maps GPU time back to framework operators.

Deep-dive The roofline says a kernel should take 2 ms; it takes 5 ms with the GPU fully busy. List hypotheses and how to test each.
  • Wrong byte or FLOP count (e.g. a hidden re-read, poor caching, an extra copy): measure DRAM bytes in Nsight Compute against the expected figure.
  • A different memory level binds (shared memory, L2 thrashing): check hierarchical metrics and L2 hit rate.
  • Latency-bound: low occupancy, long dependency chains, synchronisation; look at stall reasons and achieved occupancy.
  • Wave quantisation: compare tile count with SM count; try other tile sizes or split-K.
  • Uncoalesced or misaligned access: check sectors per request.
  • Clock throttling: record clocks and power during the run.
  • Non-tensor-core path: verify tensor-core utilisation; dtype or layout may prevent it.
  • Bank conflicts in shared memory.
Core How does the roofline extend to multi-GPU communication?

Treat a collective as moving \(Q_{\text{comm}}\) bytes over links of bandwidth \(\beta_{\text{link}}\), with a per-message latency \(\alpha\): time ≈ \(\alpha\cdot(\text{number of steps})+Q_{\text{comm}}/\beta_{\text{link}}\) (the α–β model, Chapter 17). A "communication intensity" (FLOPs per byte communicated) tells you whether computation can hide communication. In prefill, messages are large and bandwidth-bound, and compute can overlap them. In decode, messages are tiny (\(B\times d\) values per all-reduce), so α dominates, and two all-reduces per layer × 80 layers can cost milliseconds. Mitigations: fewer, larger collectives, fusing communication into kernels, keeping tensor parallelism inside NVLink, or alternative parallelism.

Design You have a fixed budget to improve decode throughput by 2×. Rank candidate investments using roofline reasoning.

Start by measuring MBU and the weight/cache byte split at the production batch and context. Then: (1) if MBU is below 60%, fix overhead and kernels first (CUDA graphs, fusion, better attention kernels), cheap and often worth 1.3–1.8×. (2) Reduce bytes: FP8 weights (if weights dominate) and/or an FP8 cache (if the cache dominates); each can approach 2× on its share, with quality checks. (3) Increase effective batch by freeing memory (FP8 cache, paging; Chapter 10). (4) Speculative decoding, which raises useful tokens per byte read, roughly 1.5–3× at small batch but less at large batch. (5) Hardware with more bandwidth (H200/B200), about 1.4–2.4×, but at capital cost. Choose the combination that reaches 2× at the lowest cost per token while meeting TPOT and quality targets.

Core Compare A100, H100, H200 and B200 for Llama 3 8B decode at batch 64 and 2k context, using a roofline budget.

The step moves ≈ 32.5 GB (15 GB of weights, 17.2 GB of cache) and does ≈ \(10^{12}\) FLOPs. Ideal times ≈ bytes/β: A100 15.9 ms, H100 9.7 ms, H200 6.8 ms, B200 4.1 ms. Compute time is a small fraction on all of them (3.2 ms on A100, under 1 ms on the others), so all are memory-bound and the ranking follows bandwidth. Add capacity (the H200/B200 can hold far larger batches, moving the operating point right), achieved MBU (software maturity), and price per hour to get cost per token. A common surprise is how competitive an A100 or H200 is relative to its FLOP/s for decode-heavy traffic.

Design Given a TPOT SLO of 20 ms, how do you choose the batch size and estimate throughput for a model on a GPU?

Write the step time as \((Nb_w + B\bar t s_{kv})/(\text{MBU}\cdot\beta)\) (plus a compute term if near compute-bound, and overhead). Set it equal to the SLO and solve for \(B_{\max}^{\text{SLO}}\). Also compute \(B_{\max}^{\text{mem}}=(M_{\text{HBM}}-Nb_w-\text{workspace})/(\bar ts_{kv})\). Run at \(\min(B_{\max}^{\text{SLO}}, B_{\max}^{\text{mem}})\), with throughput ≈ \(B/\text{TPOT}\). Example: Llama 3 8B, 2k context, 80% MBU on an H100 gives \(B\le140\) from the SLO and ≈ 238 from memory, so B ≈ 140 and ≈ 7,000 tokens/s. Then account for prefill interference (chunked prefill or disaggregation), length variance (P99 TPOT, not the mean) and headroom, and validate with a load test measuring goodput.
