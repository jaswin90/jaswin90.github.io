Part III · Attention and Memory — Chapter 9

IO-Aware Attention: FlashAttention 1 → 4

How to compute exactly the same attention while moving a fraction of the bytes, and how that one idea was re-engineered for every GPU generation from Ampere to Blackwell, and adapted for decoding long, paged and compressed caches.

Why this chapter

Chapter 8 made the KV cache smaller. This chapter is about reading it, and the rest of attention, with as little memory traffic as possible. The problem is sharpest in prefill. The textbook implementation of attention computes the full \(T\times T\) matrix of scores \(S=QK^{\top}\), writes it to GPU memory, reads it back to apply the softmax, writes the probabilities \(P\), and reads them again to multiply by \(V\). For a single head of Llama 3 8B at an 8,192-token prompt, \(S\) alone is 128 MiB in BF16. Across 32 heads it is 4 GiB per layer, written and read twice. The arithmetic of attention is bounded by the tensor cores, but this implementation spends most of its time shuttling a matrix that exists only as an intermediate. Moreover the matrix grows quadratically, so at long contexts it does not even fit in memory.

In 2022 Tri Dao and colleagues asked whether that matrix ever needs to exist[1]. Their answer, FlashAttention, computes exact attention, not an approximation, in tiles small enough to stay in on-chip memory, using the online softmax of §7.5 to combine tiles without ever forming \(S\). It performs slightly more arithmetic than the textbook version and runs several times faster, a perfect illustration of the roofline lesson that bytes, not FLOPs, are what cost time. Its successors chased the hardware. FlashAttention-2 (2023) reorganised the parallelism. FlashAttention-3 (2024) exploited Hopper's asynchronous tensor cores and FP8. FlashAttention-4 (2026) confronted a Blackwell GPU whose tensor cores had outrun its exponential units. Decode needed its own adaptations: FlashDecoding splits long caches across thread blocks, FlashInfer reads paged and ragged caches, and FlashMLA reads latent caches. Together these kernels are the computational heart of every modern inference engine, and the most frequently discussed kernels in inference interviews.

9.1 What standard attention costs

Consider one head of causal attention over a sequence of length \(T\) with head dimension \(d_h\), in a precision of \(b\) bytes. The arithmetic is two matrix products, \(QK^{\top}\) and \(PV\), about \(4T^2d_h\) FLOPs (halved by causality, but we ignore that constant). The standard implementation runs three kernels: a matrix product writing \(S\), a softmax reading \(S\) and writing \(P\), and a matrix product reading \(P\). Its HBM traffic is

\[ Q_{\text{std}} \approx \underbrace{4Td_h\,b}_{\text{read }Q,K,V\text{; write }O} \;+\; \underbrace{4T^2 b}_{\text{write }S\text{, read }S\text{, write }P\text{, read }P} . \]

For any realistic length the second term dominates: it is larger by a factor of \(T/d_h\), 64 at \(T=8192\). The arithmetic intensity is then about \(4T^2d_h/(4T^2b)=d_h/b=64\) FLOPs per byte in BF16, independent of \(T\), and below the H100's ridge point of 295. Standard attention is therefore memory-bound at every sequence length, limited by moving a matrix that no one needs to keep. And memory capacity runs out too: storing \(S\) for all heads of one layer at \(T=128\text{k}\) would take 32 × 32 GiB, a terabyte.

Figure 9.1 lays out the three kernels and their traffic. The black arrows carry the inputs and output, \(\Theta(Td_h)\) elements. The orange arrows carry the intermediates, four transfers of \(\Theta(T^2)\) elements. The design is the natural one if matrix multiplication and softmax are separate library calls, and it is what deep-learning frameworks did until 2022. The orange arrows are pure overhead. If the three kernels were fused into one, and \(S\) and \(P\) never left on-chip memory, only the black arrows would remain. The obstacle is that each row of the softmax needs the whole row of \(S\) before normalising, and a whole row of length \(T\) does not fit on-chip for long sequences. The online softmax removes exactly that obstacle.

Figure 9.2 quantifies the stakes. On logarithmic axes, standard attention's traffic grows with slope two, while a fused kernel that reads its inputs and writes its output once grows with slope one. At 8,192 tokens the gap is a factor of about 65, and at 128k about 1,000. Removing the quadratic traffic turns attention from a memory-bound operation into a compute-bound one, since the intensity of the fused kernel grows linearly with \(T\) (§6.3).

Worked example — the bytes of standard attention

One layer of Llama 3 8B prefilling a 4,096-token prompt: 32 heads, so the score matrix \(S\) has \(32\times4096^2\approx5.4\times10^{8}\) entries, 1.07 GB in FP16. A standard implementation writes \(S\), reads it for the softmax, writes \(P\), and reads \(P\) for the product with \(V\): about 4.3 GB of HBM traffic, about 1.3 ms at 3.35 TB/s. The two matrix products perform \(4T^2d\approx2.7\times10^{11}\) FLOPs, about 0.28 ms at 989 TFLOP/s. The layer's attention is therefore memory-bound by a factor of about five, entirely because of the intermediate matrices; FlashAttention never writes them, which is the subject of §9.2.

9.2 FlashAttention: tiling with the online softmax

The algorithm partitions \(Q\) into row blocks \(Q_1,\dots,Q_{T_r}\) of \(B_r\) queries each, and \(K,V\) into blocks \(K_1,\dots,K_{T_c}\) and \(V_1,\dots,V_{T_c}\) of \(B_c\) keys each. The block sizes are chosen so that a query block, a key block, a value block and the corresponding \(B_r\times B_c\) tile of scores fit in on-chip memory together. For each query block, the kernel streams through the key and value blocks and maintains, for every query row, three running quantities: the running maximum \(m\), the running normaliser \(\ell\), and the running unnormalised output \(o\in\mathbb R^{d_h}\). These are exactly the summaries of §7.5. One step of the inner loop is:

\[ \begin{aligned} S_{ij} &= Q_iK_j^{\top}/\sqrt{d_h} &&\text{(tile of scores, on-chip)}\\ \tilde m &= \operatorname{rowmax}(S_{ij}),\quad m^{\text{new}} = \max(m,\tilde m) &&\text{(update running max)}\\ \tilde P &= \exp(S_{ij}-m^{\text{new}}) &&\text{(unnormalised probabilities)}\\ \ell &\leftarrow e^{m-m^{\text{new}}}\,\ell + \operatorname{rowsum}(\tilde P) &&\text{(rescale and add)}\\ o &\leftarrow e^{m-m^{\text{new}}}\,o + \tilde P\,V_j &&\text{(rescale and accumulate)}\\ m &\leftarrow m^{\text{new}} \end{aligned} \]

After the last key block: \(O_i = o/\ell\) (per row), written to HBM once. The causal mask is applied inside \(S_{ij}\), and blocks entirely above the diagonal are skipped.

Correctness follows directly from the associativity of the online-softmax merge (§7.5). After processing blocks \(1,\dots,j\), the triple \((m,\ell,o)\) equals the summary of the scores of keys \(1,\dots,jB_c\), so after the last block \(o/\ell\) is exactly \(\sum_k\operatorname{softmax}(s)_kv_k\). No approximation is involved. The only differences from standard attention are in the order of floating-point operations.

Figure 9.3 traces one query block's computation. The block \(Q_i\) is loaded once and stays on-chip. Key and value blocks stream in one at a time. For each, the kernel computes a small tile of scores, exponentiates it relative to the running maximum, rescales the running normaliser and output if the maximum grew, and accumulates the new contribution. The tile of scores lives only in registers and shared memory and is discarded after use. When every key block has been processed, the kernel divides by the normaliser and writes \(O_i\) once. The kernel parallelises over batch, heads and (from FlashAttention-2 on) query blocks.

FlashAttention performs a little extra arithmetic, the rescaling, yet the original paper reported end-to-end training speed-ups of up to about 3× on GPT-2 and up to about 7.6× for the attention computation itself on long sequences, together with memory use linear rather than quadratic in \(T\). The extra work is negligible next to the bytes saved. Memory linear in \(T\) was itself transformative: it is what made 32k-token, and eventually million-token, training feasible.

For the mathematician — why exactness matters

Before FlashAttention, much research on long-context Transformers proposed approximate attention: low-rank, sparse or kernelised approximations of the softmax that reduced FLOPs from \(O(T^2)\) to \(O(T)\). Most delivered little wall-clock speed-up, because they did not reduce memory traffic in practice, and all changed the model. FlashAttention left the mathematical function unchanged and attacked the true cost. The lesson recurs throughout systems work: before approximating a computation, check whether its cost is in the arithmetic or in the data movement.

9.3 How many bytes does FlashAttention move?

The fused kernel does not quite achieve the ideal of reading each input once. For each query block, it streams the whole of \(K\) and \(V\). With \(T_r=T/B_r\) query blocks it reads \(K\) and \(V\) \(T_r\) times, so the traffic is \(\Theta(Td_h + T_r\cdot Td_h)=\Theta(T^2d_h/B_r)\). On-chip capacity \(M\) (in elements) bounds the block size: the query block, key and value blocks and the score tile must fit, giving \(B_r\approx M/d_h\) in the original analysis. Substituting:

Theorem (Dao et al., 2022) — IO complexity

For \(d_h\le M\le Td_h\), FlashAttention performs \(\Theta\!\big(T^2d_h^2/M\big)\) HBM accesses, against \(\Theta(Td_h+T^2)\) for standard attention. Moreover no exact attention algorithm can perform \(o\big(T^2d_h^2/M\big)\) HBM accesses for all \(M\) in that range. FlashAttention is IO-optimal up to constants.

For typical values, \(d_h=128\) and an on-chip budget of about 100k elements (≈ 200 KB in BF16), \(d_h^2/M\approx0.16\), so FlashAttention moves roughly a sixth as many bytes as the \(T^2\) term of standard attention, and far less than its total. The bound also explains why larger on-chip memory makes attention faster, and why Hopper's distributed shared memory and Blackwell's tensor memory matter: more on-chip capacity means larger blocks and fewer re-reads of \(K\) and \(V\).

9.4 FlashAttention-2: parallelism and fewer non-matmul FLOPs

The first FlashAttention reached about 25–40% of an A100's peak, good for attention but far below the 80–90% that a tuned GEMM achieves. Dao's FlashAttention-2 (2023)[2] identified three inefficiencies and fixed each. First, parallelism. FlashAttention-1 parallelised over batch and heads only, and for long sequences with a small batch there were too few work units to fill 108 SMs. FlashAttention-2 also parallelises over query blocks, which is possible because different query blocks are completely independent. Second, work partitioning within a block. FlashAttention-1 split each tile's key dimension across warps, which required the warps to exchange partial results through shared memory. FlashAttention-2 splits the query dimension across warps instead: each warp owns some query rows, sees all keys of the tile, and never needs to communicate. Third, non-matmul arithmetic. Since tensor-core FLOPs are about 16 times cheaper than general-purpose FLOPs on an A100, the rescaling and division must be minimised. FlashAttention-2 defers the division by \(\ell\) to the very end rather than normalising the output after every block.

Figure 9.4 contrasts the two partitionings. On the left, four warps each handle a quarter of the keys for all query rows, so every row's softmax state is split four ways and must be merged through shared memory, with synchronisation. On the right, each warp owns a quarter of the query rows and processes all the tile's keys for them, so each row's running state lives entirely in one warp's registers. Together with the other changes, FlashAttention-2 reached about 50–73% of A100 peak, around 230 TFLOP/s in the forward pass, roughly twice its predecessor.

9.5 FlashAttention-3: asynchrony on Hopper

On Hopper, FlashAttention-2 fell back to about 35% of peak[3]. The kernel had not got worse; the GPU had changed. Hopper's tensor cores are about three times faster than Ampere's, and they are driven differently. Matrix multiplies are issued asynchronously by groups of four warps (wgmma), and tiles are moved by the TMA copy engine (§5.5). A kernel that waits synchronously for each multiply and each load leaves the tensor cores idle much of the time. A second bottleneck also appeared, the exponential. An H100 provides 989 TFLOP/s of dense matrix multiplication but only about 3.9 trillion special-function operations per second, such as \(\exp\): 16 per clock per SM. Attention performs one exponential for every score, against \(4d_h=512\) matmul FLOPs per score at \(d_h=128\). At these rates, the exponentials alone take about half as long as all the matrix multiplies (\(1/3.9\text{T}\) versus \(512/989\text{T}\) seconds per score). Unless they are overlapped with the matmuls, they cost about a third of the kernel's time.

FlashAttention-3 (Shah, Bikshandi, Zhang, Thakkar, Ramani and Dao, 2024) restructured the kernel around asynchrony. Warp specialisation: within each thread block, a "producer" warp issues TMA loads of \(K\) and \(V\) tiles into a circular buffer of shared memory, while "consumer" warpgroups issue matrix multiplies and compute the softmax, so loading overlaps with computing. Ping-pong scheduling: two consumer warpgroups alternate, so that while one performs its softmax (exponentials on the special-function units) the other performs its matrix multiplies on the tensor cores. Intra-warpgroup pipelining: even within one warpgroup, the softmax of one tile overlaps with the multiply of the next. Finally, FlashAttention-3 added FP8 support, using block-wise quantisation and an "incoherent processing" step (multiplying \(Q\) and \(K\) by a random orthogonal Hadamard matrix, which leaves \(QK^{\top}\) unchanged but spreads outliers) to keep accuracy. The FP8 kernel showed 2.6× lower numerical error than a baseline FP8 attention.

Figure 9.5 shows the schedule. The two consumer warpgroups are deliberately out of phase: whenever one is computing a softmax, the other is issuing matrix multiplies, so the tensor cores are rarely idle. A separate producer warp keeps the shared-memory buffer filled ahead of demand. With these techniques FlashAttention-3 reached about 740 TFLOP/s in FP16, 75% of H100 peak, 1.5–2.0× faster than FlashAttention-2 on the same GPU, and close to 1.2 PFLOP/s in FP8.

9.6 FlashAttention-4: when the exponential becomes the bottleneck

Blackwell sharpened the asymmetry that FlashAttention-3 had worked around. Its tensor cores deliver roughly 2.3 times Hopper's dense 16-bit throughput and process much larger tiles, but neither the special-function units that compute exponentials nor shared-memory bandwidth scaled by the same factor. Redo the calculation of §9.5 with Blackwell's numbers and the exponentials take longer than the matrix multiplies. No amount of overlapping can then hide them: the kernel becomes bound by the exponential unit. FlashAttention-4 (2026)[4], by Dao and collaborators at Princeton, Together AI, Meta, NVIDIA and Colfax, was designed around this new bottleneck.

Its central trick is to compute some of the exponentials without the special-function units. It evaluates \(2^{x}\) in software on the general-purpose FMA units, which would otherwise be underused: split \(x\) into integer and fractional parts, evaluate a short polynomial approximation for the fraction, and construct the integer power by manipulating the floating-point exponent bits directly. Running this alongside the hardware exponential raises the effective exponential throughput. The kernel also reduces how often the running output must be rescaled, pipelines its work across even more specialised warps, and uses Blackwell's tensor memory and two-SM cooperative matrix instructions to cut shared-memory traffic. FlashAttention-4 reports about 1.6 PFLOP/s on a B200 in BF16, around 71% utilisation, roughly 1.3× faster than NVIDIA's cuDNN library at the time of release and 2.7× faster than Triton implementations. It is written entirely in CuTe-DSL, which compiles 20–30 times faster than the equivalent C++ templates.

Figure 9.6 places the four kernels against the dense peak of the GPU each targeted. The dashed line jumps with each generation. Each time, the previous kernel's utilisation fell (FlashAttention-2 dropped to about 35% on Hopper), and a new design brought it back to roughly three-quarters. This is the pattern Chapter 5 predicted. As tensor cores accelerate, what limits a kernel moves to everything around them (data movement, synchronisation, exponentials), and the kernel must be re-architected to overlap those. For inference engineers, the practical lessons are to use the attention kernel built for your GPU generation, to expect new hardware to need new kernels before its datasheet performance is reachable, and to note that softmax-free or exponential-light attention variants become relatively more attractive as tensor cores outrun the special-function units.

9.7 Decode attention: FlashDecoding

Everything so far concerned prefill, many queries against many keys. Decode is different: each sequence has a single new query per step, attending over a cache that may hold tens of thousands of keys. FlashAttention's parallelisation is then a poor fit. It parallelises over batch, heads and query blocks, and with one query there is only one query block. For a batch of 4 sequences with 8 KV heads there are 32 independent work units, a quarter of an H100's 132 SMs, and each unit streams through a long cache alone. Most of the GPU sits idle, and the cache is read at a fraction of the available bandwidth.

FlashDecoding (Dao, Haziza, Massa and Sizov, 2023)[5] adds parallelism along the one dimension that is long, the keys. The cache of each sequence and head is split into chunks, each processed by a separate thread block that computes a partial result: its local maximum, normaliser and unnormalised output, exactly the summary of §7.5. A second, small kernel merges the partial results with the associative rule. Because the merge is exact, so is the result. The authors reported up to 8× faster decode attention for very long sequences. This "split-KV" pattern is now standard in every decode kernel.

Figure 9.7 shows the split for one sequence and head. The single query is sent to four thread blocks, each of which reads a quarter of the cache and produces a partial triple \((m,\ell,o)\). A final merge combines them. With enough chunks, every SM is busy reading cache at once, and the kernel approaches the bandwidth roofline that §3.5 said decode attention lives on. The number of chunks is a tuning choice. Too few leaves SMs idle, while too many adds merge overhead and partial results to write. Engines choose it per batch from the number of sequences, heads and context lengths, which is also why the reduction order, and hence the bit-level output, can depend on the batch (§4.8). GQA adds a further consideration: the kernel should load each KV head once and apply it to all \(g\) query heads of its group, as a small matrix product rather than \(g\) separate vector products. That realises the \(g\)-fold intensity gain of §3.5.

Worked example — why decode attention needs splitting

A single sequence of Llama 3 8B decodes at 128k context. A kernel that assigns one thread block per (sequence, query head) launches 32 blocks for 132 SMs, so three quarters of the GPU idles while each block streams its head group's share of 16 GB of cache. Splitting the key/value length into 8 chunks gives 256 blocks, enough to occupy every SM, each computing a partial softmax over its chunk; a final reduction combines the partial maxima, sums and outputs with the online-softmax rule (§7.5). The bytes read are unchanged, but the achieved bandwidth rises roughly fourfold, so the step's attention time falls from about 20 ms to about 5 ms. This is Flash-Decoding (§9.7).

9.8 Production attention kernels

A serving engine needs more than one attention kernel. It must handle prefill and decode, often in the same batch. It must handle variable-length ("ragged") sequences without padding them, read caches stored in pages scattered through memory (Chapter 10), and support GQA, MLA, sliding windows, quantised caches and shared prefixes. Three libraries cover most of this landscape.

FlashInfer (Ye et al., 2025)[6] is an attention library designed for serving, used by SGLang, vLLM and others. It represents paged and ragged caches uniformly as block-sparse matrices, generates specialised kernels from templates just-in-time for each configuration, and balances work across SMs with a scheduler that accounts for each sequence's length. Its cascade attention handles shared prefixes. When many requests share a long system prompt, the attention over the shared part is computed once for all of them (a matrix product of many queries against one cache, which is compute-efficient) and merged with each request's attention over its own suffix, again by the online-softmax rule. That greatly raises intensity for shared-prefix workloads. FlashMLA (DeepSeek, 2025)[7] implements decode attention for multi-head latent attention on Hopper, reading a paged latent cache in blocks of 64 tokens. DeepSeek reported up to 3,000 GB/s in memory-bound configurations and 580–660 TFLOP/s in compute-bound ones on H800 GPUs. That is consistent with §8.3's observation that MLA decode sits near the ridge point. FlexAttention (PyTorch, 2024)[8] lets researchers express attention variants as small Python functions that modify scores (score_mod) or define masks (mask_mod), such as ALiBi biases, sliding windows, document masks or prefix-LM patterns. It compiles them into a fused FlashAttention-style kernel with block-sparse skipping of masked tiles, giving most of the performance of hand-written kernels for new attention patterns.

9.9 Numerical details every attention kernel must get right

Fused attention kernels are short, but they contain several numerical traps that cause real production bugs, and interviewers like to probe them. Four matter most.

Precision of the running state. Inputs may be BF16 or FP8, but the running maximum, normaliser and output accumulator are kept in FP32 registers, and the score tiles are computed with FP32 accumulation inside the tensor cores. The normaliser sums up to \(T\) positive terms, and BF16's 8-bit significand would lose small contributions once the sum grows: adding \(e^{-5}\) to a running sum of 300 in BF16 changes nothing. FP32 accumulation is the reason fused attention is more accurate than a naive BF16 implementation, not less.

Fully masked rows. If every key visible to a query is masked, which happens with padding, some document masks and the first block of certain sliding-window layouts, then every score is \(-\infty\), the running maximum stays \(-\infty\), and the rescaling factor \(e^{m-m^{\text{new}}}=e^{-\infty-(-\infty)}\) is undefined: NaN. A single NaN then propagates through the residual stream and corrupts every later token of that sequence. Correct kernels special-case a maximum of \(-\infty\), treating the rescale factor as 0 and the row's output as 0. Kernels with custom masks must be tested on this edge case explicitly.

The scale and the base of the exponential. Kernels usually compute \(2^{x\log_2e}\) rather than \(e^{x}\), because the hardware's fast exponential is base 2, and they fold the \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\) scale and the \(\log_2e\) factor into a single multiplication of the scores. Models with unusual scales (MLA's \(1/\sqrt{d_h+d_h^R}\), soft-capped logits as in Gemma 2, or attention temperature adjustments for context extension) need this constant set correctly. A wrong scale produces plausible but subtly worse text, the hardest kind of bug to notice.

Reduction order. Split-KV decode and tiled prefill combine partial results in an order that depends on block sizes and split counts, which the engine may choose per batch. The results are equal in exact arithmetic but not bit for bit, the batch-dependence of §4.8. Engines that promise reproducibility fix the split strategy, at some cost in speed.

9.10 A decode-attention budget

To see how much the choice of kernel matters, return to the running example in a long-context setting: Llama 3 8B on one H100, a batch of 4 sequences each with 32,768 tokens of cache. The cache read per decode step is \(4\times32{,}768\times128\) KiB ≈ 17.2 GB, so at the full 3.35 TB/s the attention part of a step takes about 5.1 ms. That is the roofline, and every real kernel is measured against it. The following estimates are illustrative, built from the effects described in this chapter rather than from a specific benchmark, but their ordering is robust.

A naive implementation that expands each KV head \(g=4\) times before attending (as the teaching code of §3.9 does with repeat_interleave) reads and writes four times as many bytes, about 20 ms. A GQA-aware kernel without split-KV has only \(4\times8=32\) independent work units, one per sequence and KV head, on 132 SMs. If each SM can draw perhaps two to three times its fair share of bandwidth, the kernel achieves roughly 32 × (2.5 × 3.35/132) TB/s ≈ 2 TB/s, about 8.5 ms. With split-KV dividing each cache into, say, eight chunks, there are 256 work units, every SM is busy, and the kernel can approach 85–90% of peak bandwidth, about 5.7–6 ms. With an FP8 cache the bytes halve, to about 3 ms. In this regime the gap between the naive and the best kernel is roughly 7×, and it comes entirely from bytes moved and from parallelism, not from arithmetic. That is why decode attention kernels are judged by achieved bandwidth (MBU), and why every serious engine ships split-KV, GQA-aware, paged and quantised variants.

Drill 9.2 — When is split-KV unnecessary?

For the same model, at what batch size does the number of (sequence, KV head) pairs alone reach 132 SMs, and why might split-KV still help beyond it?

Solution. \(B\times8\ge132\Rightarrow B\ge17\). Beyond that every SM has at least one work unit, so splitting is no longer needed for occupancy. It can still help with load balance when sequence lengths differ widely: one 100k-token sequence would otherwise keep a single SM busy long after the others finish, the decode-attention analogue of wave quantisation (§6.7). Load-balanced schedulers such as FlashInfer's split long sequences more finely than short ones for this reason.

Looking ahead

FlashAttention and its decode-time relatives tell us how to read a cache efficiently once it is in memory. They take for granted that the kernel can find each sequence's keys and values, and the production kernels of §9.8 already assume the cache is stored in pages scattered through memory. Why pages? Because sequences grow one token at a time to unpredictable lengths, and allocating each one a contiguous buffer large enough for its maximum length wastes most of the memory the cache is supposed to use. Chapter 10 tells that story: how vLLM borrowed virtual memory from operating systems to manage the KV cache in pages, and how the same machinery enables sharing cached prefixes across requests and moving cold caches to cheaper tiers of storage.

Chapter summary

  • Standard attention writes and reads \(S\) and \(P\) (\(\Theta(T^2)\) bytes per head) through HBM; its intensity is ≈ \(d_h/b\) (64 in BF16), so it is memory-bound, and it needs \(\Theta(T^2)\) memory.
  • FlashAttention tiles \(Q\), \(K\), \(V\); for each query block it streams key/value blocks, keeping per-row running \((m,\ell,o)\) with rescaling by \(e^{m-m^{\text{new}}}\), and writes \(O=o/\ell\) once. It is exact; memory is linear in \(T\).
  • IO complexity \(\Theta(T^2d_h^2/M)\), optimal up to constants; more on-chip memory means fewer re-reads.
  • FA-2: parallelism over query blocks, query-split warp partitioning, deferred normalisation (≈ 73% of A100 peak).
  • FA-3 (Hopper): warp specialisation with TMA producers, ping-pong overlap of softmax and wgmma, FP8 with Hadamard incoherent processing (≈ 75% of H100, ≈ 1.2 PFLOP/s FP8). Exponentials (≈ 3.9 T/s) would otherwise cost about a third of the time.
  • FA-4 (Blackwell): exponentials outrun by tensor cores; software exp2 on FMA units, fewer rescalings, TMEM and 2-CTA MMA (≈ 1.6 PFLOP/s, 71% of B200).
  • Decode: one query per sequence leaves SMs idle; FlashDecoding splits the cache into chunks processed in parallel and merges exactly. GQA kernels load each KV head once for its whole group.
  • Numerics: FP32 running state; special-case fully masked rows (else NaN); fold \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\) and \(\log_2e\) into one scale; reduction order makes results batch-dependent.
  • Decode budget (batch 4, 32k context, Llama 3 8B): ≈ 5 ms roofline; naive head expansion ≈ 20 ms; no split-KV ≈ 8.5 ms; split-KV ≈ 6 ms; FP8 cache ≈ 3 ms.
  • Production: FlashInfer (paged/ragged, load-balanced, cascade attention for shared prefixes), FlashMLA (latent caches), FlexAttention (custom variants compiled into fused kernels).

Warm-up What problem does FlashAttention solve, and how?

Standard attention materialises the \(T\times T\) score and probability matrices in HBM (writing and reading them across three kernels), so it is memory-bound (intensity ≈ \(d_h/b\)) and uses \(\Theta(T^2)\) memory. FlashAttention fuses the whole computation into one kernel. It tiles Q, K, V into blocks that fit on-chip; for each query block it streams key/value blocks, computes score tiles in SRAM, and maintains per-row running max, normaliser and unnormalised output, rescaling them when the max increases (online softmax). It writes only the final output. Result: exact attention, \(\Theta(T^2d_h^2/M)\) HBM traffic (IO-optimal), linear memory, and several-fold speed-ups despite slightly more FLOPs.

Core Write pseudocode for the FlashAttention forward pass and prove it is exact. 
for each query block Q_i (rows r):
    m = -inf; l = 0; o = 0                       # per row
    for each key block j (skip if fully masked):
        S = Q_i @ K_j.T / sqrt(d_h)  (+ mask)
        m_new = max(m, rowmax(S))
        P = exp(S - m_new)
        l = exp(m - m_new) * l + rowsum(P)
        o = exp(m - m_new) * o + P @ V_j
        m = m_new
    O_i = o / l

Invariant: after processing key blocks \(1..j\), \(m\) is the max score over those keys, \(\ell=\sum_ke^{s_k-m}\) and \(o=\sum_ke^{s_k-m}v_k\). The update preserves it: rescaling by \(e^{m-m^{\text{new}}}\) re-expresses old terms relative to the new max, and the new block adds its own terms. After the last block, \(o/\ell=\sum_k\frac{e^{s_k}}{\sum_{k'}e^{s_{k'}}}v_k\), exactly softmax attention.

Core Derive FlashAttention's IO complexity.

Let on-chip memory hold \(M\) elements. Choose key/value block size \(B_c\approx M/(4d_h)\) and query block size \(B_r\approx\min(M/(4d_h),d_h)\) as in the original analysis. Each of the \(T/B_r\) query blocks reads all of K and V (\(2Td_h\) elements), so total traffic ≈ \(Td_h+(T/B_r)\cdot2Td_h+Td_h\) = \(\Theta(T^2d_h/B_r)\). With \(B_r=\Theta(M/d_h)\) this is \(\Theta(T^2d_h^2/M)\). For \(d_h=128\) and \(M\approx10^5\), \(d_h^2/M\approx0.16\), compared with \(\Theta(T^2)\) for standard attention. Dao et al. also prove a matching lower bound for exact attention over \(M\in[d_h,Td_h]\).

Core What did FlashAttention-2 change, and why did it double throughput?

(1) Parallelism over the sequence dimension (query blocks), not just batch × heads, filling all SMs for long sequences with small batches. (2) Work partitioning: split query rows across warps (each warp owns rows and their softmax state) instead of splitting keys (which needed inter-warp reductions through shared memory). (3) Fewer non-matmul FLOPs (deferred normalisation, fewer rescalings), important because non-tensor FLOPs are ~16× more expensive on A100. Result: ≈ 50–73% of A100 peak (≈ 230 TFLOP/s forward) versus 25–40% for FA-1.

Deep-dive Explain why exponentials become a bottleneck in attention on Hopper and Blackwell, and how FA-3 and FA-4 respond.

Per score element, attention does ≈ \(4d_h\) matmul FLOPs (QKᵀ and PV) and one exp. H100: 989 TFLOP/s matmul versus ≈ 3.9 T exp/s (16 per clock per SM), so at \(d_h=128\) exp time ≈ 0.5 × matmul time; sequential execution would spend about a third of time in exp. FA-3 overlaps them: warp specialisation (TMA producer warps; consumer warpgroups), ping-pong scheduling between two warpgroups (one's softmax under the other's wgmma), and intra-warpgroup pipelining. Blackwell's tensor cores are ≈ 2.3× faster but exp units and shared-memory bandwidth did not scale, so exp time would exceed matmul time and cannot be fully hidden. FA-4 adds software exp2 on FMA units (polynomial plus exponent-bit construction), reduces rescaling work, and uses TMEM and 2-CTA MMA to cut shared-memory pressure, reaching ≈ 71% of B200 peak.

Core Why is decode attention different from prefill attention, and how does FlashDecoding address it?

Decode has one new query per sequence attending over a long cache. Intensity is ≈ \(2g/b\) (memory-bound), and parallelism over batch × KV heads may be far below the SM count, so few SMs stream the cache and achieved bandwidth is low. FlashDecoding splits each (sequence, head) cache into chunks processed by separate thread blocks; each computes partial \((m,\ell,o)\); a small kernel merges them exactly with the online-softmax rule. This fills the GPU and reaches near-peak bandwidth; the authors reported up to 8× faster decode attention for very long sequences. Further: GQA-aware kernels share each KV load across the group's query heads; paged layouts need block-table indirection; the split count is tuned per batch.

Core Estimate attention time in prefill for a long prompt with and without FlashAttention.

Llama 3 8B, \(T=32{,}768\), H100. Standard: per head \(4T^2b\approx8.6\) GB of S/P traffic; ×32 heads ≈ 275 GB per layer ≈ 82 ms at 3.35 TB/s, and S alone needs 64 GiB per layer, so it does not fit. FlashAttention: FLOPs \(4T^2d_hn_h\approx1.76\times10^{13}\) per layer (non-causal) ≈ 30 ms at 60% MFU, ≈ 15 ms causal; ×32 layers ≈ 0.5 s. Conclusion: FlashAttention is what makes long prefills possible at all; beyond ~30k tokens attention dominates prefill compute (Chapter 3), motivating context parallelism and sparse attention (Chapter 21).

Deep-dive How does FP8 attention keep accuracy acceptable in FlashAttention-3?

Two techniques. (1) Block quantisation: separate scales per block of Q, K, V rather than per tensor, so local dynamic range is preserved. (2) Incoherent processing: multiply Q and K by a random orthogonal matrix \(H\) (a randomised Hadamard transform, \(O(d\log d)\) to apply); \((QH)(KH)^{\top}=QK^{\top}\), but outlier coordinates are spread across all coordinates, reducing the maximum magnitude relative to the typical one, so FP8's limited range and precision lose less information. Accumulation stays in higher precision and softmax is computed in FP32 registers. The paper reports 2.6× lower numerical error than baseline FP8 attention. Validate end-task quality before deploying FP8 attention, especially for long contexts.

Design Your serving engine must support paged KV caches, ragged batches mixing prefill and decode, GQA and shared system prompts. Which attention kernels and techniques do you use?

A serving attention library (FlashInfer, or the engine's own paged kernels) with: (1) block-table indirection to read paged K/V (Chapter 10); (2) ragged/varlen batching: concatenate tokens of all requests with offsets, with no padding; (3) separate or unified paths for prefill (FlashAttention-style tiling, causal) and decode (split-KV with load balancing across sequences of different lengths); (4) GQA-aware decode, loading each KV head once per group; (5) cascade attention for shared prefixes, computing the shared part once with many queries and merging with per-request suffix attention; (6) FP8 cache support with fused dequantisation; (7) CUDA-graph compatible decode (fixed shapes via padded batch buckets, metadata in persistent buffers); (8) kernel selection per GPU generation (FA-3 on Hopper, FA-4/cuDNN on Blackwell). Validate numerics against a reference and measure MBU for decode, MFU for prefill.

Warm-up Why does FlashAttention perform more FLOPs yet run faster?

It adds small amounts of rescaling arithmetic (multiplying running state by \(e^{m-m^{\text{new}}}\)), plus recomputation in the backward pass during training. But standard attention is memory-bound: its time is dominated by \(\Theta(T^2)\) HBM traffic for S and P, not by FLOPs. Eliminating that traffic makes the kernel compute-bound, close to tensor-core peak, so total time falls several-fold despite the extra FLOPs. It is the roofline principle: optimise the binding resource.

Core How does the FlashAttention backward pass avoid storing the attention matrix?

It stores only the output \(O\) and the per-row log-sum-exp \(L=m+\log\ell\) from the forward pass (\(\Theta(T)\) extra memory). In the backward pass it recomputes score tiles \(S_{ij}=Q_iK_j^{\top}\) block by block, reconstructs \(P_{ij}=\exp(S_{ij}-L_i)\) exactly from the stored statistics, and computes the gradients \(dV\), \(dP\), \(dS=P\odot(dP-D)\) (with \(D_i=\operatorname{rowsum}(dO_i\odot O_i)\)), \(dQ\), \(dK\) tile by tile. Recomputation costs extra FLOPs but avoids \(\Theta(T^2)\) memory and traffic, again faster overall. Inference needs only the forward pass, but this explains FlashAttention's adoption in training, which drove its widespread availability.

Deep-dive Cascade (shared-prefix) attention: derive why it helps and how outputs are combined.

For \(R\) requests sharing a prefix of length \(P\) with private suffixes, standard decode reads the prefix's K/V \(R\) times (once per request), each at intensity ≈ \(2g/b\). Cascade attention computes attention of all \(R\) queries against the shared prefix as one batched operation: the prefix K/V is read once and used by \(R\cdot g\) query heads, so intensity rises by ≈ \(R\) and the operation can become compute-bound. Each request also attends to its own suffix separately. Each partial yields \((m,\ell,o)\); the final output is the online-softmax merge of the prefix part and the suffix part. It is exact, and for large \(R\) and long shared prompts (system prompts, few-shot examples, documents) it can cut decode attention time substantially.

Core What is FlexAttention and when would you use it instead of FlashAttention?

FlexAttention (PyTorch) lets you define attention variants as small Python functions: score_mod(score, b, h, q_idx, kv_idx) to modify scores (ALiBi, soft-capping, relative biases) and mask_mod to define sparsity patterns (sliding windows, document masks, prefix-LM). It compiles them via torch.compile into a fused FlashAttention-style kernel with block-sparse skipping of fully masked tiles. Use it for research or unusual architectures where no hand-written kernel exists, accepting some performance gap to the best hand-tuned kernels; use FlashAttention-3/4 or cuDNN for standard dense attention, and FlashInfer or engine kernels for paged serving.

Design A new GPU arrives with 2× tensor-core throughput but the same exponential throughput. Predict the effect on attention and on the rest of inference.

Attention prefill: matmul time per score halves, exp time is unchanged. On an H100-like balance (exp ≈ 0.5× matmul), exp would equal matmul, so even perfect overlap gives at most ≈ 1.33–1.5× speed-up rather than 2×, and existing kernels without overlap would see even less. Remedies: FA-4-style software exp, polynomial approximations, conditional rescaling, or attention variants with fewer exps. Decode attention: unaffected (memory-bound). GEMMs: up to 2× in prefill if memory can feed them; decode GEMMs unchanged (bandwidth). Overall: prefill improves partially, decode not at all unless bandwidth also improved. This is why spec-sheet FLOP/s rarely translate into proportional inference gains.

Core Why must an inference engine use different attention kernels for different GPU generations?

Each generation changes how tensor cores are fed and what else becomes the bottleneck. Ampere: synchronous MMA plus cp.async, so FA-2-style kernels. Hopper: asynchronous wgmma, TMA and clusters, which need warp specialisation and ping-pong (FA-3); FA-2 runs at ≈ 35% there. Blackwell: tcgen05 with tensor memory, 2-CTA MMA and FP4, with exp and shared-memory bandwidth now limiting (FA-4). Also data formats (FP8/FP4 scales), register and shared-memory budgets, and instruction sets differ. A kernel tuned for one generation leaves much of the next one's performance unused, so engines dispatch per architecture and per configuration (head dim, GQA ratio, dtype, paged/varlen).

Core List the numerical pitfalls of a fused attention kernel and how to test for them.

(1) Accumulation precision: keep \(m\), \(\ell\), \(o\) and score accumulation in FP32; BF16 sums lose small terms. Test by comparing against an FP64 reference on long sequences with many small weights. (2) Fully masked rows (padding, document masks, windows) give \(-\infty-(-\infty)\) = NaN in the rescale; special-case \(m=-\infty\). Test with rows having no valid keys. (3) Scale constants: \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\) (or MLA's \(1/\sqrt{d_h+d_h^R}\)), \(\log_2e\) for exp2, logit soft-capping, temperature for context extension. Test against a reference implementation with the model's real config. (4) Overflow: always subtract the running max. (5) Reduction order: different split counts and tile sizes give last-bit differences. Compare with tolerances, not exact equality, and fix splits if reproducibility is required. (6) FP8 inputs: check scale handling and saturation. Run randomised differential tests across shapes, masks, dtypes and split settings.

Deep-dive Build a decode-attention time budget for 4 sequences × 32k context on Llama 3 8B, H100, and compare kernel designs.

Cache bytes per step: \(4\times32768\times128\text{ KiB}\approx17.2\) GB, so the roofline is ≈ 5.1 ms at 3.35 TB/s. Designs: (a) naive KV-head expansion (\(g=4\)) means ≈ 4× bytes, ≈ 20 ms. (b) GQA-aware without split-KV: only 4 × 8 = 32 work units on 132 SMs; if each SM can pull ~2–3× its fair share, ≈ 2 TB/s, ≈ 8.5 ms. (c) Split-KV with ≈ 8 chunks: 256 units, ≈ 85–90% of peak, ≈ 5.7–6 ms. (d) Plus an FP8 cache: bytes halve, ≈ 3 ms. (e) Plus Quest-style selective reading of, say, 25% of pages: ≈ 1 ms, with an accuracy caveat. The lesson: decode attention is a bandwidth and parallelism problem; measure MBU, choose split counts by occupancy and load balance, and reduce bytes with quantisation or selection.
