Part V · Making Models Smaller — Chapter 15

Pruning, Sparsity and Distillation

Quantisation keeps every parameter and stores it in fewer bits. The alternatives remove parameters, or computation, altogether: individual weights, regular patterns of weights, whole neurons, heads and layers, or, most radically, the large model itself, replaced by a small one trained to imitate it. This chapter asks which of these removals the hardware can turn into speed, and why distillation became the way small models are made.

Why this chapter

Chapter 14 ended with a limit. Below about four bits per weight, quantisation noise grows quickly, and the gains in speed depend on hardware support for the format. A different idea is as old as neural networks: many weights of a trained network contribute little, so remove them. LeCun's "optimal brain damage" (1989) and Hassibi and Stork's "optimal brain surgeon" (1992) already pruned networks by estimating each weight's effect on the loss, and the "lottery ticket" results of 2019 suggested that large networks contain small subnetworks that train just as well. For language models, one-shot pruning methods of 2023 showed that half the weights of a large model can be set to zero with little loss in accuracy.

And yet pruning has been far less important in LLM serving than quantisation. The reason is not accuracy but hardware. A GPU reads memory in contiguous chunks and computes on dense tiles, so a matrix with half of its entries zeroed at random is no cheaper to read or to multiply unless the zeros are arranged in a way the hardware understands. The story of this chapter is therefore one of increasing structure: from unstructured sparsity, which is accurate but slow; to the 2:4 pattern that NVIDIA's tensor cores accelerate; to removing whole neurons, heads and layers, which always speeds things up but damages the model more; and finally to knowledge distillation, which recovers the damage by training the smaller model on the larger one's outputs. Since 2024, pruning followed by distillation has produced some of the best small models, and distillation alone has become the standard way to make them.

Along the way we meet a mathematical thread from the previous chapter. The optimal-brain-surgeon calculation that gave GPTQ its update also gives the best one-shot pruning criterion, and one of the most effective pruning methods turns out to be that criterion with a diagonal approximation. Distillation, in turn, is a choice of divergence between distributions, and the choice of direction, forward or reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence, matters.

15.1 What can be removed, and what becomes faster

The question "which weights can we remove?" has answers at several scales, and the scale determines whether removal saves time. Figure 15.1 arranges them from finest to coarsest. Unstructured sparsity zeroes individual weights anywhere in a matrix. Semi-structured sparsity zeroes weights in a fixed local pattern, the most important being NVIDIA's 2:4 pattern, in which every group of four consecutive weights has at most two non-zeros. Structured (width) pruning removes whole rows or columns, which correspond to FFN neurons, attention heads or embedding channels, so the matrices simply become smaller. Depth pruning removes whole Transformer blocks. Finally, contextual sparsity removes nothing permanently, but skips the parts of the computation that a particular token does not need.

Figure 15.1 makes the central trade-off visible. On the left, the kept weights form no pattern, which gives a pruning algorithm the most freedom to keep the important ones; on the right, entire blocks disappear, which a GPU can exploit trivially but which forces the algorithm to discard important weights along with unimportant ones. To see why the left-hand end gains so little, recall that decode is memory-bound. A sparse matrix must store not only its non-zero values but also where they are, and that metadata costs bytes. With sparsity \(\eta\) (the fraction of zeros), compressed sparse row storage with 16-bit values and 16-bit column indices needs \((1-\eta)\times4\) bytes per original weight, and a bitmap format needs \((1-\eta)\times2+1/8\).

Figure 15.2 plots these costs. At the 50% sparsity that the best one-shot methods reach for large models, compressed sparse row storage is no smaller than the dense matrix, because every surviving 2-byte value carries a 2-byte index. A bitmap does better, at 1.125 bytes, a 44% saving. Dense 4-bit quantisation, by comparison, needs about 0.52 bytes, a 74% saving, and a bitmap format reaches that only at about 80% sparsity. Sparse matrices also compute poorly on tensor cores, which multiply dense tiles; skipping zeros requires gathering the non-zero values into dense tiles, which costs more than it saves at moderate sparsity. This is why quantisation, not pruning, dominates the compression of deployed LLMs. Pruning matters when its structure matches the hardware, or when it is combined with quantisation and training. But understanding how to choose which weights to remove is the foundation for all the structured methods, and the mathematics is a close cousin of GPTQ's.

15.2 Choosing what to prune: from magnitude to OBS

The simplest pruning criterion removes the weights of smallest magnitude. It is cheap, and for networks trained with pruning in mind it works well. For one-shot pruning of a trained LLM without retraining, it fails badly beyond modest sparsity, for the same reason that round-to-nearest fails at 4 bits: a weight's importance depends not only on its size but on the inputs it multiplies. A small weight on an outlier channel (§14.4) can matter more than a large weight on a quiet one.

The layer-wise formulation of §14.6 gives the right criterion. For one output neuron with weight row \(w\) and calibration-input second-moment matrix \(\Sigma=XX^\top\), setting weight \(q\) to zero is a constrained change \(\delta e_q=-w_q\), and allowing the other weights to compensate optimally, the Lagrangian calculation of §14.6 with \(c=-w_q\) gives

\[ \delta\;=\;-\frac{w_q}{[\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}}\,e_q^\top\Sigma^{-1},\qquad\text{error increase}\quad\varepsilon_q\;=\;\frac{w_q^2}{[\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}}. \]

The error increase \(\varepsilon_q\) is the optimal-brain-surgeon (OBS) saliency of weight \(q\): prune the weight whose removal, after the best compensation, costs least. Exactly as with quantisation, the compensating update spreads the damage across the remaining weights in proportion to the input correlations. Applying OBS exactly, one weight at a time, with an inverse-matrix update after each removal, is far too expensive for matrices with tens of millions of entries.

SparseGPT (Frantar and Alistarh, 2023)[1] made it practical with the same devices as GPTQ, from the same group. It processes columns left to right, so that the sequence of \(\Sigma^{-1}\) updates is shared by all rows. It chooses the pruning mask adaptively in blocks of columns, using the OBS saliencies computed with the current inverse, and it applies the compensating updates lazily in blocks. Because the mask selection and the quantisation step have the same structure, SparseGPT can prune and quantise jointly. It pruned OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B to 50–60% unstructured sparsity in a few hours on a single GPU with only a small increase in perplexity, and it was the first to show that the largest models are, in this sense, the most compressible: the relative loss at a given sparsity shrinks as models grow.

A few months later, Sun, Liu, Bair and Kolter proposed Wanda (pruning by weights and activations)[2], which is strikingly simpler. Wanda scores each weight by

\[ S_{ij}\;=\;|W_{ij}|\cdot\|X_j\|_2, \]

the magnitude of the weight times the norm of its input feature over the calibration set, removes the lowest-scoring weights within each output row, and performs no weight update at all. Its accuracy is close to SparseGPT's at 50% sparsity, at a fraction of the cost. The OBS saliency explains why. If we approximate \(\Sigma\) by its diagonal, ignoring correlations between input features, then \([\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}=1/\Sigma_{qq}=1/\|X_q\|_2^2\), and the saliency becomes \(\varepsilon_q=w_q^2\|X_q\|_2^2=S_q^2\). Wanda is OBS with a diagonal Hessian and no compensation. The comparison within each output row also matters: it ensures every neuron keeps the same fraction of its inputs, which prevents some outputs from being pruned to uselessness. That a diagonal approximation suffices says something about LLM activations: the dominant effect is the very uneven scale of input features, which the diagonal captures, rather than their correlations.

Worked example — magnitude, Wanda and OBS disagree

A neuron has weights \(w=(0.5,\;0.2,\;0.3)\), and its three input features have calibration norms \(\|X_j\|_2=(1,\;10,\;1)\), uncorrelated, so \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(1,100,1)\). We must prune one weight. Magnitude removes \(w_2=0.2\), the smallest. Wanda scores \((0.5,\;2.0,\;0.3)\) and removes \(w_3\). OBS with diagonal \(\Sigma\) has saliencies \(w_q^2\Sigma_{qq}=(0.25,\;4.0,\;0.09)\), also removing \(w_3\), at an output error of 0.09 against 4.0 for magnitude pruning: the "small" weight multiplies a feature ten times larger than the others. With correlated inputs, OBS and Wanda can disagree, and OBS's compensating update can reduce the error further; that is where SparseGPT's extra cost buys accuracy.

Both methods produce excellent unstructured sparse models that, as §15.1 showed, are of limited use for speed. Both also support a constrained mask in which every group of four weights keeps two, and it is this constraint that the hardware can exploit.

15.3 Semi-structured sparsity: the 2:4 pattern

With the Ampere generation in 2020, NVIDIA added sparse tensor cores that accelerate a specific pattern: in every group of four consecutive weights along the reduction dimension, at most two are non-zero[3]. The matrix is stored compressed, with the two surviving values of each group and a 2-bit index for each, saying which of the four positions it came from. During the matrix product, the hardware uses the indices to select the matching two activations of each group of four and performs half the multiply-adds. The pattern is regular enough to be handled entirely inside the tensor core, and the speed-up is up to 2× in math throughput, at exactly 50% sparsity.

Figure 15.3 shows the storage for two groups. The bytes are easy to count: per group of four original weights, two 16-bit values and two 2-bit indices, 36 bits, or 9 bits per original weight, 56% of dense BF16. With FP8 values it is 5 bits per weight. The format thus saves about as much memory as FP8 quantisation and offers up to twice the compute throughput, which makes it attractive for compute-bound prefill. For memory-bound decode, the saving is the 44% in bytes, and real end-to-end speed-ups reported for LLMs have been modest, typically 1.1–1.5×, because only the linear layers are accelerated and kernels rarely reach the full 2×.

The accuracy cost of the 2:4 constraint is also larger than for unstructured sparsity at the same 50%, because the mask can no longer put more survivors in important regions of a row. One-shot 2:4 pruning with SparseGPT or Wanda typically loses noticeably more than unstructured pruning, especially on smaller models, and recovering the loss requires retraining. Work such as "Sparse Llama" showed that 2:4-sparse Llama models can recover nearly full accuracy with continued pre-training on billions of tokens and then be quantised as well, combining both savings. But this is no longer a cheap post-training technique, and in the economics of 2024–25, the effort was more often spent on removing whole structures, where the speed-up is guaranteed.

15.4 Structured pruning: width and depth

Structured pruning removes entire units and leaves a smaller dense model. Every engine, kernel and hardware generation runs it at full efficiency, and its savings are predictable: removing a fraction of parameters removes the same fraction of bytes and FLOPs. There are two directions. Width pruning removes FFN neurons (rows of \(W_1\) and \(W_3\) with the matching columns of \(W_2\)), attention heads (with their slices of \(W_Q\), \(W_K\), \(W_V\) and \(W_O\)), or embedding channels (one coordinate of the residual stream, everywhere it appears). Depth pruning removes whole blocks, which shrinks the KV cache as well, since a removed layer stores no keys or values.

The question is again which units matter, and the answer is again measured on calibration data. For width, a standard importance score of an FFN neuron or head is the magnitude of its activation, averaged over calibration tokens; for embedding channels, the magnitude of the normalised residual-stream coordinate. For depth, a useful observation is that many layers of a trained LLM change the residual stream very little. If \(x_\ell\) is the residual stream entering block \(\ell\), the block's effect can be measured by the cosine similarity between its input and output. The block influence of ShortGPT[4] is

\[ \mathrm{BI}_\ell\;=\;1-\mathbb E_t\Big[\frac{x_{\ell,t}^\top x_{\ell+1,t}}{\|x_{\ell,t}\|\,\|x_{\ell+1,t}\|}\Big], \]

and blocks with the smallest \(\mathrm{BI}\), which rotate the residual stream least, are removed first. Several 2024 studies found that a substantial fraction of the deeper layers of large models can be removed with surprisingly little effect on knowledge benchmarks such as MMLU, though with larger effects on generation and reasoning[5]. Depth pruning has an engineering advantage: removing layers reduces latency directly, since layers execute sequentially, whereas width pruning makes each layer cheaper but leaves the number of sequential steps unchanged.

Worked example — removing a quarter of Llama 3 8B's layers

Remove 8 of Llama 3 8B's 32 blocks. The blocks hold about 6.98B of the 8.03B parameters (the rest are embeddings and the LM head), so removing a quarter of them removes about 1.75B parameters, 22% of the model. Decode bytes: 3.5 GB fewer weight bytes per step in BF16 (16.06 → 12.56 GB). KV cache: 24 layers instead of 32, so 96 KiB instead of 128 KiB per token, 25% less, which also raises the memory-limited batch by a third. Latency at batch 1: about 22% lower. Accuracy: without retraining, a noticeable drop, larger on generative tasks than on multiple-choice benchmarks; with a short distillation phase (§15.6), most of it can be recovered. The same 22% could be had from width pruning, but the cache would shrink only if KV heads were removed, and latency would fall slightly less because the 32 sequential steps remain.

Structured pruning without retraining damages models more than unstructured pruning at the same parameter count, because it removes information in large pieces. The damage is not permanent, though: the pruned network is a good initialisation, much better than random, and a modest amount of further training heals it. The natural training signal for healing is the original model's own predictions, which brings us to distillation.

15.5 Knowledge distillation

Training a language model with the usual next-token loss tells it, at each position, which single token came next in the data. But a trained model knows more than that: its predictive distribution says that after "The capital of France is", the token "Paris" is overwhelmingly likely, "the" is plausible, and "Lyon" is possible but wrong. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015)[6] called this information "dark knowledge" and proposed knowledge distillation: train a small student model to match the full output distribution of a large teacher, rather than only the one-hot data labels.

Write \(p_{\text{te}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\) and \(p_{\text{st}}(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\) for the teacher's and student's next-token distributions, both computed at temperature \(\tau\) from logits \(z\) as \(p(v)\propto\exp(z_v/\tau)\) (§4.3). The distillation loss at one position is the Kullback–Leibler divergence

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{KD}}\;=\;\tau^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\text{te}}\,\Vert\,p_{\text{st}}\big)\;=\;\tau^2\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}p_{\text{te}}(v)\log\frac{p_{\text{te}}(v)}{p_{\text{st}}(v)}, \]

usually mixed with the ordinary next-token loss on the data. Its gradient with respect to the student's logits has the simple form \((p_{\text{st}}(v)-p_{\text{te}}(v))/\tau\) per vocabulary entry (Drill 15.1), compared with \(p_{\text{st}}(v)-\mathbb 1[v=x_t]\) for the data loss. Two things follow. First, the distillation gradient is non-zero for every vocabulary entry, so each position teaches the student about the relative plausibility of thousands of alternatives rather than one; this is why distilled students learn faster per token than students trained on data alone. Second, for large \(\tau\) the difference \(p_{\text{st}}-p_{\text{te}}\) itself shrinks like \(1/\tau\), so the gradient scales as \(1/\tau^2\); multiplying the loss by \(\tau^2\) keeps its magnitude comparable across temperatures. A temperature above 1 flattens both distributions and exposes more of the teacher's preferences among unlikely tokens; for LLMs, \(\tau=1\) is common, since their distributions are already informative.

Figure 15.4 illustrates a choice that classical distillation made without comment. The forward divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{te}}\Vert p_{\text{st}})\) heavily penalises the student for assigning low probability where the teacher assigns high probability, so a student that cannot represent the teacher's distribution exactly spreads its mass to cover everything the teacher considers plausible, including the low-probability valleys between modes. When the student then generates, it samples from those valleys and produces text the teacher would not. The reverse divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{st}}\Vert p_{\text{te}})\) penalises the student for placing mass where the teacher has little, so it concentrates on the teacher's main modes. MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2023)[7] argued that reverse KL is better for generative students and showed gains in generation quality. Intermediate choices, such as a weighted mixture or the generalised Jensen–Shannon divergence, interpolate between the two behaviours.

A second choice is which sequences the loss is computed on. In word-level distillation, the student is trained on fixed sequences (data or teacher outputs), matching the teacher's distribution at each position. In sequence-level distillation (Kim and Rush, 2016), the teacher generates complete outputs, and the student is trained on them with the ordinary next-token loss, which needs only the teacher's samples, not its logits; this is what "fine-tuning on outputs of a larger model" means, and it is how DeepSeek distilled its R1 reasoning model into small Qwen and Llama models, using about 800,000 generated samples[8]. In on-policy distillation (GKD, Agarwal et al., 2024)[9], the student generates sequences and the teacher scores every position. This matters because at inference time the student conditions on its own previous tokens, not the teacher's; training only on teacher-written prefixes leaves it unprepared for its own mistakes, the train–inference mismatch known as exposure bias. On-policy distillation trains on exactly the distribution of prefixes the student will face, at the cost of running both models during training.

15.6 Prune, then distil: Minitron and Llama 3.2

Pruning gives a small model that starts close to a large one but has been damaged; distillation gives a way to train a small model efficiently from a large one. Combining them is natural, and in 2024 NVIDIA's Minitron work (Muralidharan et al.)[10] turned the combination into a recipe for producing model families. Starting from Nemotron-4 15B, it estimated the importance of layers, heads, FFN neurons and embedding channels from activations on a small calibration set, pruned to 8B and 4B parameters along the best combination of axes, and then retrained each pruned model by distillation from the original, using logits as targets.

Figure 15.5 summarises the pipeline. Its striking result was economic. Minitron's pruned-and-distilled models needed up to 40 times fewer training tokens than models of the same size trained from scratch, and matched or beat comparable open models; across the whole family, the training cost fell by about 1.8×. Several empirical lessons came with it: width pruning generally outperformed depth pruning for the same parameter count after retraining, logit distillation outperformed fine-tuning on data, and pruning iteratively in small steps beat pruning in one large step only modestly. A follow-up applied the recipe to Llama 3.1 8B, producing a 4B model[11]. Meta used a similar approach for Llama 3.2's 1B and 3B models (September 2024), which were initialised by pruning Llama 3.1 8B and trained with logits from the 8B and 70B models as targets.

The reason pruning helps distillation is worth stating carefully. Distillation from scratch must learn everything the teacher knows through a narrow channel, the output distribution, one token at a time. A pruned initialisation inherits most of the teacher's internal representations directly: its embedding, most of its attention patterns and the important FFN neurons. Retraining only has to repair the damage and redistribute function among the surviving units. In this sense the two methods are complementary: pruning transfers structure, and distillation transfers behaviour.

15.7 Distillation as the way small models are made

By 2025 distillation had stopped being a compression trick and become standard practice for small models. Google trained Gemma 2's 2B and 9B models with distillation from a larger model instead of plain next-token prediction[12], arguing that the richer per-token signal lets small models benefit from training on more tokens than they could otherwise use efficiently. DeepSeek's R1 distillations showed that reasoning behaviour, learned by a large model through reinforcement learning, could be transferred to models of 1.5B to 70B parameters simply by fine-tuning on the large model's reasoning traces, with the distilled models outperforming small models trained with reinforcement learning directly. Most frontier providers' small, fast models are believed to be distilled from their large ones.

For inference engineers, the important consequence is a change in the economics of serving. The cost of generating a token scales roughly with active parameters (Chapter 3): a 70B model reads and computes about 8.8 times as much per token as an 8B model. If a distilled 8B model matches the 70B model on a given task, serving it costs about an eighth as much, and runs at several times lower latency. Distillation itself has a cost, a training run over billions of tokens with the teacher's logits computed for each, but it is paid once and amortised over every token the student ever serves.

Worked example — when does distilling pay for itself?

A product serves \(D_{\text{inf}}=10^{12}\) output tokens per month with Llama 3 70B. A team proposes distilling an 8B student on 100 billion tokens of teacher logits. Distillation cost: teacher forward passes, about \(2\times70.6\times10^{9}\times10^{11}\approx1.4\times10^{22}\) FLOPs, plus student training at about \(6\times8\times10^{9}\times10^{11}\approx4.8\times10^{21}\) FLOPs, roughly \(1.9\times10^{22}\) in all, or about 9,000 H100-hours at 60% MFU. Serving saving: decode is memory-bound, so cost per token scales with bytes per step; the student needs roughly 1/8 of the GPU time per token at the same batch. If the 70B deployment sustains about 3,000 output tokens per second per H100 in FP8, it needs \(10^{12}/3000\approx3.3\times10^{8}\) GPU-seconds, about 93,000 H100-hours per month; the student needs about 12,000. Break-even: the saving of about 81,000 H100-hours per month repays the 9,000-hour distillation in under four days. The calculation is dominated by \(D_{\text{inf}}\): distillation pays whenever a model serves enough tokens, which is why it became routine.

The caveat is the phrase "matches on a given task". Distilled small models inherit the teacher's behaviour where the training data exercises it, and degrade elsewhere; their knowledge capacity is limited by parameter count. Distillation is therefore most effective for products with a well-defined distribution of tasks, and evaluation must cover that distribution. Distillation also combines with everything else in Part V: a distilled 8B model can be quantised to FP8 or 4 bits, and pruned further if needed.

Drill 15.1 — The distillation gradient

Show that the gradient of \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{te}}\Vert p_{\text{st}})\) with respect to the student's logit \(z_v\), at temperature \(\tau\), is \((p_{\text{st}}(v)-p_{\text{te}}(v))/\tau\), and explain the factor \(\tau^2\) in the loss.

Solution. Write \[\mathrm{KL}=\sum_u p_{\text{te}}(u)\log p_{\text{te}}(u)-\sum_u p_{\text{te}}(u)\log p_{\text{st}}(u);\] only the second term depends on the student. With \(\log p_{\text{st}}(u)=z_u/\tau-\log\sum_w e^{z_w/\tau}\), we have \(\partial\log p_{\text{st}}(u)/\partial z_v=(\mathbb 1[u=v]-p_{\text{st}}(v))/\tau\). So \[\frac{\partial\mathrm{KL}}{\partial z_v}=-\sum_up_{\text{te}}(u)\,\frac{\mathbb 1[u=v]-p_{\text{st}}(v)}{\tau}=\frac{p_{\text{st}}(v)-p_{\text{te}}(v)}{\tau}.\] For large \(\tau\), both distributions approach uniform and their difference shrinks as \(1/\tau\) (expanding \(e^{z/\tau}\approx1+z/\tau\)), so the gradient scales as \(1/\tau^2\); multiplying the loss by \(\tau^2\) keeps its gradients comparable to those of the data loss as \(\tau\) changes.

15.8 Contextual sparsity: skipping work per token

All the methods so far remove the same parameters for every input. A different form of sparsity is dynamic: for each token, many FFN neurons produce outputs close to zero, so the corresponding rows of \(W_1\), \(W_3\) and columns of \(W_2\) contribute almost nothing and need not be read. In models with ReLU activations this sparsity is exact and large; OPT models activate only a few per cent of FFN neurons per token. Deja Vu (Liu et al., 2023)[13] trained small predictors that guess, from the input to each layer, which neurons and heads a token will need, and skipped the rest, reducing the latency of OPT-175B by more than 2× at batch 1. PowerInfer exploited the same effect on consumer hardware, keeping frequently active ("hot") neurons on the GPU and rarely active ones on the CPU. Modern LLMs use SwiGLU rather than ReLU, whose outputs are small but rarely exactly zero, but training-free methods such as TEAL (2024) threshold small activations and still reach 40–50% sparsity with speed-ups of about 1.5–1.8× in single-sequence decoding.

There is a catch, and it is decisive for serving. Contextual sparsity saves bytes only if a weight row can be skipped for every sequence in the batch, because the row is read once per step for all of them. If each token activates each neuron independently with probability \(p_a\), the fraction of neurons needed by at least one of \(B\) tokens is

\[ 1-(1-p_a)^B. \]

Figure 15.6 plots the union for three activation rates. With 50% of neurons active per token, a batch of four already needs 94% of the weights; even with ReLU-like sparsity of 5%, a batch of 32 needs 81%. Real activation patterns are correlated across tokens, with some neurons active for most inputs and many rarely active, so the union grows somewhat more slowly than the independent model predicts, and PowerInfer's hot/cold split exploits exactly this skew. But the qualitative conclusion stands. In a data-centre engine running batches of hundreds, contextual sparsity saves almost nothing in bytes, while for a single user on a laptop or phone, where batch size is one and memory bandwidth is scarce, it can be worth a factor of two (Chapter 29). The same logic will reappear for mixture-of-experts models (Chapter 19), which are, in a sense, contextual sparsity made structured and trained in: each token uses a few experts, and large batches touch all of them.

Drill 15.2 — Choosing between depth pruning and 2:4 sparsity

A team serving Llama 3 8B at batch 128 and 4k context on H100s, with an FP8 KV cache, wants 20% more throughput. Option A removes 8 of 32 layers and heals with distillation. Option B applies 2:4 sparsity to all linear layers with continued training. Which is likely to deliver the target, and why?

Solution. With an FP8 cache (a BF16 cache would not fit), per-step bytes are about 16.06 GB of weights plus \(128\times4096\times64\) KiB \(\approx34.4\) GB of cache, so the cache dominates (68%). Option A removes 22% of weight bytes and 25% of cache bytes, about 24% of total bytes, giving about 1.3× in the memory-bound regime, and it also lets 33% more sequences fit in memory. Option B reduces only weight bytes, by 44%, which is about 14% of total bytes, so at best about 1.16× and less with real kernels. Option A is far more likely to deliver, provided distillation recovers accuracy. Structured removals that shrink the cache are the ones that help at large batch and long context; for weight bytes alone, FP8 quantisation would be the simpler choice.

15.9 Choosing a compression strategy

Part V has described a toolbox, and it is worth closing with how the tools combine. The decisive questions are where the bytes and FLOPs of the workload are, what the hardware accelerates, and how much training budget is available. The table summarises the main options.

A few patterns follow from the table and from the arguments of this chapter. Quantisation is the first tool to reach for, because it needs no training and its savings apply everywhere. For large-batch, long-context serving, the cache dominates bytes, so the techniques that shrink it (FP8 caches, fewer layers or KV heads, and the architectural changes of Chapter 8) matter more than any reduction in weights. Unstructured sparsity is a research tool and a component of combined methods rather than a deployment technique on GPUs. And when a product's traffic is large and its tasks well defined, distilling a smaller model, possibly from a pruned initialisation, and then quantising it, usually beats compressing the large model ever more aggressively.

Looking ahead

Part V has so far made a fixed model cheaper to run: fewer bits per weight, fewer weights, or a smaller student trained to imitate a larger teacher. Distillation, the last of these, already changed the weights, and it raises a question we have not yet faced. Most deployments do not serve a base model as released. They serve a model adapted to a task, a domain, a house style or a customer, and adapting a model means training it. Full fine-tuning of Llama 3 8B needs the weights, their gradients and the optimiser's state in memory at once, well over 100 GB in mixed precision, and it produces a complete 16 GB copy of the model for every task. A provider with a thousand customers cannot store, let alone serve, a thousand such copies. Chapter 16 shows that the change fine-tuning makes is usually far smaller than the model, so small that it can be written as a product of two thin matrices. That observation, low-rank adaptation (LoRA), and its relatives make adaptation cheap enough to train on a single GPU and cheap enough to serve thousands of variants from one copy of the base model.

Chapter summary

  • Sparsity comes in scales: unstructured, semi-structured (2:4), width (neurons, heads, channels), depth (layers) and contextual (per token). More structure is easier to accelerate and harder to keep accurate.
  • Unstructured sparsity rarely speeds up GPUs: indices cost bytes (CSR at 50% saves nothing; a bitmap saves 44%), tensor cores need dense tiles, and dense 4-bit quantisation saves more (≈ 0.52 bytes per weight).
  • OBS saliency \(\varepsilon_q=w_q^2/[\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}\), with compensating update \(\delta=-w_q\,e_q^\top\Sigma^{-1}/[\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}\). SparseGPT applies it with GPTQ's machinery (50–60% sparsity on 175B models). Wanda's \(|W_{ij}|\,\|X_j\|_2\) is OBS with a diagonal \(\Sigma\) and no update.
  • 2:4 sparsity: two non-zeros per group of four with 2-bit indices (9 bits per weight with BF16 values); up to 2× math on sparse tensor cores, ≈ 1.1–1.5× end to end, and needs retraining for accuracy.
  • Structured pruning removes neurons, heads, channels or layers (ranked by activation importance or block influence \(1-\mathbb E[\cos(x_\ell,x_{\ell+1})]\)); savings are proportional and real, and depth pruning also shrinks the KV cache and latency.
  • Distillation minimises \(\tau^2\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{te}}\Vert p_{\text{st}})\); its gradient \((p_{\text{st}}-p_{\text{te}})/\tau\) teaches every vocabulary entry. Forward KL covers modes; reverse KL (MiniLLM) seeks them. Sequence-level distillation trains on teacher outputs (DeepSeek-R1 distills); on-policy distillation (GKD) trains on student-generated prefixes to avoid exposure bias.
  • Prune-then-distil (Minitron, Llama 3.2 1B/3B) needs up to 40× fewer tokens than training from scratch; pruning transfers structure, distillation behaviour. Distillation pays for itself quickly at high serving volume.
  • Contextual sparsity (Deja Vu, PowerInfer, TEAL) helps at batch 1, but the union of active neurons, \(1-(1-p_a)^B\), approaches 1 quickly with batch size.

Warm-up Why doesn’t 50% unstructured sparsity make LLM decode twice as fast on GPUs?

Decode is memory-bound, and a sparse matrix must store indices as well as values: CSR with 16-bit values and 16-bit indices at 50% sparsity takes exactly as many bytes as dense BF16, and a bitmap still takes 1.125 bytes per weight (a 44% saving, not 50%). Compute is also a problem: tensor cores multiply dense tiles, so irregular sparsity requires gathering non-zeros, which costs more than it saves at moderate sparsity. Real speed-ups need either very high sparsity (beyond what LLMs tolerate without retraining), hardware-supported patterns like 2:4, or structured removal. Dense 4-bit quantisation saves about 74% of bytes with no metadata problem, which is why it dominates.

Warm-up What is knowledge distillation, and why does it work better than training a small model on data alone?

Distillation trains a small student to match a large teacher's output distribution, typically minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{te}}\Vert p_{\text{st}})\) at each position, often mixed with the data loss. A one-hot label says only which token occurred; the teacher's distribution says how plausible every alternative is, so each position supplies a gradient \((p_{\text{st}}-p_{\text{te}})/\tau\) on every vocabulary entry. The student therefore learns more per token, generalises better, and can be trained on more tokens efficiently. Variants: sequence-level distillation (train on teacher outputs), on-policy distillation (teacher scores student generations), and reverse-KL objectives for generative students. It is how most small production models are made (Gemma 2, Llama 3.2 small models, DeepSeek-R1 distills).

Core Derive the OBS pruning criterion and show how Wanda relates to it.

For output neuron weights \(w\) and calibration inputs \(X\), output error is \((w-\hat w)\Sigma(w-\hat w)^\top\) with \(\Sigma=XX^\top\). Pruning weight \(q\) means a change \(\delta\) with \(\delta e_q=-w_q\); minimising \(\delta\Sigma\delta^\top\) under that constraint by Lagrange multipliers gives \(\delta=-w_q\,e_q^\top\Sigma^{-1}/[\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}\) and error \(w_q^2/[\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}\), the OBS saliency: prune the smallest. SparseGPT applies this at scale with GPTQ's tricks (fixed column order shared by rows, Cholesky of \(\Sigma^{-1}\), adaptive mask blocks, lazy updates). If \(\Sigma\) is approximated by its diagonal, \([\Sigma^{-1}]_{qq}=1/\|X_q\|^2\) and the saliency becomes \(w_q^2\|X_q\|^2\), whose square root is Wanda's score \(|W_{ij}|\,\|X_j\|_2\); Wanda also skips the compensating update. That it works nearly as well shows that the dominant effect in LLMs is the uneven scale of input features rather than their correlations.

Core Explain 2:4 semi-structured sparsity: storage, speed-up and accuracy.

Each group of four consecutive weights along the reduction dimension keeps at most two non-zeros. Storage: the two values plus a 2-bit position index each, so 9 bits per original weight with BF16 values (56% of dense) or 5 bits with FP8. Ampere and later sparse tensor cores use the indices to select the matching activations and do half the multiply-adds, up to 2× math throughput at exactly 50% sparsity. In practice LLM speed-ups are about 1.1–1.5× end to end, since only linear layers benefit and decode is memory-bound (44% fewer weight bytes, nothing for the cache). Accuracy: the constraint is harsher than unstructured 50%, so one-shot 2:4 (SparseGPT or Wanda with the constrained mask) loses noticeably more; continued pre-training can recover most of it and the result can be quantised too.

Core Compare width pruning and depth pruning.

Width pruning removes FFN neurons, attention heads or embedding channels, ranked by activation-based importance on calibration data; the model keeps its depth, so latency falls less than proportionally, and the KV cache shrinks only if KV heads (or channels feeding them) are removed. Depth pruning removes whole blocks, ranked for example by block influence \(1-\mathbb E[\cos(x_\ell,x_{\ell+1})]\); it reduces sequential steps, so latency falls proportionally, and it shrinks the KV cache by the fraction of layers removed. Without retraining, depth pruning often preserves knowledge benchmarks surprisingly well but hurts generation and reasoning more. After distillation-based healing, Minitron found width pruning generally gave better accuracy for the same parameter count. Both give real, predictable speed-ups because the result is a smaller dense model.

Core Explain forward versus reverse KL for distilling a generative model.

Forward \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{te}}\Vert p_{\text{st}})=\sum p_{\text{te}}\log(p_{\text{te}}/p_{\text{st}})\) is large when the student assigns low probability where the teacher assigns high, so a capacity-limited student spreads mass to cover all teacher modes, including the low-probability regions between them (mode-covering). Reverse \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{st}}\Vert p_{\text{te}})\) is large when the student puts mass where the teacher has little, so the student concentrates on the teacher's main modes (mode-seeking). For generation, forward-KL students sample implausible tokens from the valleys; reverse-KL students (MiniLLM) produce more precise text but may lose diversity. Reverse KL requires samples from the student, so it is naturally on-policy. Mixtures and generalised Jensen–Shannon divergences interpolate; GKD allows choosing the divergence and the data source (fixed, teacher or student generated).

Core What is contextual sparsity, and why does it not help large-batch serving?

For a given token, many FFN neurons (and some heads) contribute almost nothing; with ReLU only a few per cent are active. Deja Vu trains small predictors to choose the needed neurons per token and skips reading the corresponding weight rows and columns; PowerInfer keeps frequently active neurons on GPU and others on CPU; TEAL thresholds small activations in SwiGLU models without training. At batch 1, skipping weights saves memory bandwidth and gives 1.5–2× or more. But a weight row must be read if any sequence in the batch needs it: with independent activation probability \(p_a\), the needed fraction is \(1-(1-p_a)^B\), which exceeds 80% at \(B=32\) even for \(p_a=5\%\). Correlations slow this somewhat, but in data-centre serving at large batch the savings essentially vanish, making contextual sparsity an edge and single-user technique. MoE is the trained, structured version of the same idea.

Deep-dive Describe how Minitron produces a family of smaller models, and why pruning plus distillation beats either alone.

Start from a trained teacher (Nemotron-4 15B, or Llama 3.1 8B). Run a forward pass on a small calibration set to estimate importance of layers, heads, FFN neurons and embedding channels from activation magnitudes. Search over pruning configurations (which axes and how much) to reach target sizes (8B, 4B), optionally with a short retraining to compare candidates. Then retrain the chosen pruned model with knowledge distillation from the teacher's logits, using a few per cent of the original training tokens (up to 40× fewer than training from scratch). Pruning alone damages the model; distillation alone must transfer everything through the output distribution starting from random weights. The pruned initialisation inherits embeddings, attention patterns and important neurons directly, so distillation only repairs and redistributes. Lessons reported: width over depth after retraining, logits over data labels, and modest gains from iterative pruning. Meta's Llama 3.2 1B/3B used a similar recipe.

Deep-dive When does distilling a smaller model pay for itself? Give a quantitative argument.

Distillation cost is roughly the teacher's forward FLOPs on the distillation tokens, \(2N_{\text{te}}D\), plus student training, \(6N_{\text{st}}D\) (forward and backward). For \(N_{\text{te}}=70\)B, \(N_{\text{st}}=8\)B, \(D=10^{11}\): about \(1.9\times10^{22}\) FLOPs, about 9,000 H100-hours at 60% MFU. Serving cost per token scales with bytes per step, roughly with \(N\), so the student costs about 1/8 per token. For a product serving \(10^{12}\) tokens/month with the 70B model at about 3,000 tokens/s per GPU, that is about 93,000 H100-hours/month versus 12,000 for the student, so the distillation pays back in days. The general condition: distil when \(D_{\text{inf}}\times(\text{cost per token}_{\text{te}}-\text{cost per token}_{\text{st}})\) exceeds the training cost, provided the student meets quality on the product's task distribution. Low-volume or open-ended products may not justify it.

Deep-dive Why might a pruned model look fine on MMLU but degrade in production?

Multiple-choice knowledge benchmarks score a single token or short answer given a prompt, so they mostly test whether factual associations survive. Removing layers or neurons can preserve these while damaging capabilities that require many coherent steps: long-form generation, multi-step reasoning, instruction following, code, long-context retrieval and calibration of probabilities. Errors compound over hundreds of generated tokens, and the student's own mistakes put it into states the original model rarely visited. Studies of layer pruning found exactly this asymmetry. Evaluation must therefore include generative tasks scored on outputs, long contexts, and the product's real prompts, with the unpruned model as reference, and healing (distillation, ideally on-policy) is usually needed before deployment.

Deep-dive How would you combine pruning, distillation and quantisation for a 1B on-device model?

Start from a strong teacher family (for example an 8B model). Prune to about 1–1.5B along width (FFN neurons, heads, embedding channels) and modest depth, using activation importance on representative on-device data. Heal by logit distillation from the 8B (and possibly a larger) teacher, ending with on-policy distillation on the target tasks to reduce exposure bias. Then quantise: 4-bit weights (AWQ or GPTQ, groups of 32–128) or quantisation-aware training if accuracy at 4 bits falls short, keeping embeddings or the LM head at higher precision if memory allows (the vocabulary matrix is a large share of a 1B model). Use a quantised KV cache and modest context. Optionally exploit contextual sparsity at batch 1. Evaluate on-device latency (tokens/s, time to first token), memory, battery and task accuracy against the teacher.

Design A team proposes to cut serving cost of a 70B model by pruning it 50% with SparseGPT. What do you advise?

Ask where the cost is. Unstructured 50% sparsity will not make GPU decode faster: indices make the matrix almost as large (CSR) or only 44% smaller (bitmap), and dense kernels cannot skip zeros. 2:4 sparsity gives perhaps 1.1–1.5× and needs retraining to keep accuracy. Better options in order: FP8 W8A8 with FP8 KV (about 2× fewer bytes and 2× compute ceiling, near lossless, no training); 4-bit weights if batch is small or memory is the constraint; then, if volume justifies it, distil a smaller model (possibly from a pruned initialisation) for the product's tasks and quantise it. If the workload is large-batch and long-context, focus on cache bytes. Use SparseGPT only as part of a combined pipeline (for example 2:4 plus quantisation with continued training) and measure real kernel speed-ups before committing.

Design Design an evaluation and rollout plan for replacing a 70B model with a distilled 8B model in a customer-support product.

Offline: assemble a test set from real traffic (anonymised), stratified by intent, language, length and difficulty, with tool calls and multi-turn conversations. Score both models with task metrics (resolution, correctness against references, tool-call validity, policy compliance), pairwise preference by a strong judge plus human spot checks, and safety evaluations. Compute confidence intervals and per-segment results; set gates (for example no segment worse by more than 2 points). Check behavioural statistics: response length, refusal rate, hallucination rate on knowledge questions. Online: shadow traffic, then a small A/B test with business metrics (resolution, escalations, satisfaction), with automatic rollback. Keep the 70B as a fallback, routing hard or low-confidence cases to it (a cascade), which often captures most of the saving with little risk. Monitor drift and retrain the student periodically on new traffic with on-policy distillation.

Design How would you decide between pruning layers and adopting an FP8 KV cache for a long-context workload?

Compute per-step bytes: weights plus cache. For long contexts at moderate batch, cache bytes dominate. An FP8 KV cache halves cache bytes with negligible accuracy loss and no training, and is a configuration flag in most engines; it should almost always come first. Removing a quarter of the layers removes about a quarter of cache bytes and about a fifth of weight bytes, but requires healing by distillation and careful evaluation, especially for long-context retrieval, which pruning can damage. The two combine: after FP8 KV, if more throughput or memory is needed, consider depth or KV-head pruning with distillation, or architectural changes (GQA/MLA, sliding windows) in the next model generation. Quantify each option with the roofline and memory-limited batch before running experiments.

Core What are sequence-level and on-policy distillation, and when would you use each?

Sequence-level distillation: the teacher generates complete responses to a set of prompts, and the student is fine-tuned on them with ordinary next-token loss. It needs only teacher samples (not logits), works across different tokenisers and even through APIs, and is how reasoning traces were transferred from DeepSeek-R1 to small models. On-policy distillation (GKD): the student generates responses, and the teacher provides per-token distributions (or scores) on the student's own sequences; the student minimises a divergence on them. It fixes exposure bias, because the student learns to recover from its own mistakes, and is natural for reverse-KL objectives, but requires running teacher inference during training and a shared tokeniser for logit-level targets. Typical pipeline: logit or sequence-level distillation for bulk transfer, then on-policy distillation on target tasks.

Core Why does depth pruning reduce the KV cache but width pruning of FFN neurons does not?

The KV cache stores, for every layer, each token's keys and values: \(s_{kv}=2Ln_{kv}d_hb_{kv}\). Removing layers reduces \(L\) and hence the cache proportionally (8 of 32 layers removes 25%). FFN neurons do not participate in attention, so removing them reduces weight bytes and FLOPs but leaves \(s_{kv}\) unchanged. Width pruning reduces the cache only if it removes KV heads (reducing \(n_{kv}\)) or shrinks the head dimension. Since cache bytes dominate at large batch and long context, depth pruning, or KV-head reduction, is the structural pruning that helps most in that regime.
