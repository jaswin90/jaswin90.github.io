Part VI · Distributed Inference — Chapter 19

Mixture of Experts at Inference

A mixture-of-experts model stores many feed-forward networks per layer and sends each token through only a few of them. It decouples parameter count from per-token compute, which is why the largest open models of 2024–25 use it, but it also changes every quantity this book has computed: bytes per step, arithmetic intensity, memory, load balance and communication. This chapter develops MoE inference from the layer up to the 320-GPU deployment of DeepSeek-V3.

Why this chapter

We have met DeepSeek-V3 throughout the book as the model with the unusually small KV cache (Chapter 8) and the 671-gigabyte weights (Chapter 2). The second fact is the subject of this chapter. DeepSeek-V3 has 671 billion parameters, yet each token uses only 37 billion of them, because 654 billion sit in 14,848 small feed-forward networks, 256 per layer in 58 layers, of which each token visits eight. Its quality is that of a very large model, and its compute per token is that of a medium one. The same design underlies Mixtral, Qwen3's largest models, Llama 4, Kimi K2 (a trillion parameters) and OpenAI's gpt-oss, and is widely believed to underlie most frontier closed models.

For an inference engineer, mixture-of-experts (MoE) models are where everything learned so far is tested at once. A dense model reads all of its weights at every step; an MoE model at small batch reads only the experts its tokens choose, but at large batch reads nearly all of them, and each expert sees only a fraction of the batch, so the arithmetic intensity that Chapter 6 computed per weight is divided by the number of experts per token. The model is too large for one server, but tensor parallelism is a poor fit for experts that are each small. And the routing of tokens to experts creates a new communication pattern, the all-to-all, and a new failure mode, load imbalance, in which one overloaded expert sets the pace for hundreds of GPUs. The systems that serve MoE models well, above all DeepSeek's own, are the most sophisticated inference deployments in existence, and their design follows from a few calculations we can do.

19.1 Conditional computation

The scaling laws of Chapter 1 say that loss falls predictably with parameters and with training tokens. In a dense model, parameters and per-token compute are tied: every parameter participates in every token, so doubling parameters doubles the cost of training and of inference. Conditional computation breaks the tie. If a model can choose, for each token, which of its parameters to use, it can hold far more parameters than it uses at once, gaining capacity for knowledge without paying for it in FLOPs per token.

The modern form of the idea is due to Shazeer and colleagues (2017)[1], who inserted a sparsely gated mixture-of-experts layer into an LSTM language model: thousands of small feed-forward networks, the experts, and a trainable gate that sent each token to a few of them. Their models had up to 137 billion parameters, enormous for the time, at modest compute. GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) brought MoE to Transformers at 600 billion parameters with top-2 routing and a fixed capacity per expert. The Switch Transformer (Fedus, Zoph and Shazeer, 2021)[2] simplified routing to a single expert per token and trained models with 1.6 trillion parameters, showing large gains in quality per FLOP. For several years MoE remained largely a training-efficiency technique of a few large labs, because it was hard to train stably and hard to serve.

Two open releases changed that. Mixtral 8×7B (Mistral AI, December 2023)[3] replaced each FFN of a Mistral-7B-like model with eight experts and top-2 routing: 46.7 billion parameters in total, 12.9 billion active per token, matching or beating Llama 2 70B at a fraction of the compute. DeepSeekMoE (January 2024)[4] introduced fine-grained experts, many small experts instead of a few large ones, so that each token can combine more specialised pieces, and shared experts that every token uses, capturing common knowledge so the routed experts need not duplicate it. DeepSeek-V2 (May 2024) and V3 (December 2024)[5] scaled these ideas to 256 routed experts per layer with top-8 routing. The table summarises the main open MoE models.

That ratio is the source of MoE's economy and of its serving difficulty, as the rest of the chapter shows. First we need the layer itself.

19.2 The MoE layer

An MoE layer replaces the FFN of a Transformer block. It has \(n_{\text{exp}}\) routed experts, each an ordinary SwiGLU FFN with its own weights \(W_1^{(i)},W_3^{(i)}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d_e}\) and \(W_2^{(i)}\in\mathbb R^{d_e\times d}\), optionally some shared experts of the same form, and a router matrix \(W_r\in\mathbb R^{d\times n_{\text{exp}}}\). For a token with normalised hidden state \(x\), the router computes affinities \(s_{i}=\sigma\big((xW_r)_i\big)\) (DeepSeek-V3 uses the sigmoid; earlier models used a softmax over all experts), selects the \(k_r\) experts with the highest affinities, and normalises their affinities to gating weights \(g_i\) that sum to one. The layer's output is

\[ y\;=\;\sum_{j\in\text{shared}}\operatorname{FFN}_j(x)\;+\;\sum_{i\in\operatorname{TopK}(s,\,k_r)}g_i\,\operatorname{FFN}_i(x),\qquad g_i=\frac{s_i}{\sum_{i'\in\operatorname{TopK}}s_{i'}}. \]

Only the selected experts are evaluated. The router is tiny, \(d\times n_{\text{exp}}\) parameters per layer, and its computation negligible; all the cost is in the experts.

Figure 19.1 shows the data flow for one token. Counting is straightforward. Each expert has \(3dd_e\) parameters. For DeepSeek-V3, with \(d=7168\) and \(d_e=2048\), that is 44.0 million per expert, 11.3 billion per layer for the 256 routed experts, and 654 billion over 58 MoE layers; the shared experts add 2.6 billion, and attention, embeddings and the three dense layers about 15 billion more, for 671 billion in total. Per token, eight routed experts and the shared expert in each MoE layer contribute 23 billion parameters, and with the rest the active count is 37 billion, so each token costs about \(2\times37\times10^{9}\) FLOPs, like a 37B dense model.

The choice of fine-grained experts deserves a comment, because it shapes serving. With \(n_{\text{exp}}\) experts and top-\(k_r\) routing there are \(\binom{n_{\text{exp}}}{k_r}\) possible combinations per token per layer, about \(4\times10^{14}\) for DeepSeek-V3's \(\binom{256}{8}\) against 28 for Mixtral's \(\binom82\). That combinatorial flexibility is what DeepSeekMoE found improved quality at a fixed active size. But each expert is small, \(d_e=2048\) against Llama 3 70B's \(d_{ff}=28{,}672\), so each expert's matrix products are thin, and each expert sees only a small share of the batch. Both facts will matter.

19.3 Routing and load balance

A router left to itself tends to collapse: a few experts that happen to be slightly better early in training receive more tokens, improve faster and attract still more, while the others starve. Load balance is therefore an explicit training objective. The Switch Transformer added an auxiliary loss. With \(f_i\) the fraction of a batch's tokens routed to expert \(i\) and \(\bar p_i\) the mean router probability assigned to expert \(i\),

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{aux}}\;=\;\alpha_{\text{aux}}\,n_{\text{exp}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{exp}}}f_i\,\bar p_i, \]

which, since \(f_i\) and \(\bar p_i\) each sum to one, is minimised when both are uniform, at value \(\alpha_{\text{aux}}\). The \(f_i\) are not differentiable, but the \(\bar p_i\) are, and the gradient pushes probability away from overloaded experts. Training systems also imposed an expert capacity, a maximum number of tokens per expert per batch, typically a capacity factor of 1.0–1.25 times the average, and dropped tokens that exceeded it, passing them through the residual connection unchanged. Dropping is tolerable in training; at inference it would change outputs depending on what else was in the batch, so serving systems process every token and accept uneven loads.

The auxiliary loss has a cost: it trades model quality for balance, since it penalises the router for making the choices it would otherwise make. DeepSeek-V3 introduced auxiliary-loss-free balancing. Each expert has a bias \(\omega_i\) that is added to its affinity only for the purpose of selecting the top \(k_r\), not in the gating weights. After each training step, the bias of each overloaded expert is decreased by a small constant and that of each underloaded expert increased. The selection is nudged towards balance without any gradient interfering with what the experts learn, and DeepSeek reported better quality than with auxiliary losses (a very small sequence-level auxiliary loss is kept to prevent extreme imbalance within individual sequences). V3 also uses node-limited routing: each token's experts must lie on at most four of the eight nodes of its training deployment, which bounds communication, a point we return to in §19.5.

Training-time balance is a statement about averages over large batches of training data. At inference, the batch is whatever requests happen to be running, and balance holds only statistically. Even a perfectly balanced router distributes tokens randomly, and the busiest expert in each step receives more than the mean. Because every expert-parallel GPU must finish its experts before the next layer can proceed, the step time is set by the most loaded one. The expected imbalance is easy to estimate: if each of \(n_{\text{exp}}\) experts receives a Poisson number of tokens with mean \(m_e=Bk_r/n_{\text{exp}}\), the maximum is roughly \(m_e+\sqrt{2m_e\ln n_{\text{exp}}}\), so the ratio of maximum to mean load is about

\[ 1+\sqrt{\frac{2\ln n_{\text{exp}}}{m_e}}. \]

Figure 19.2 shows simulated imbalance for DeepSeek-V3's 256 experts with top-8 routing, measured as the busiest expert's load divided by the mean. With a uniform router, random fluctuation alone makes the busiest expert carry 2.1 times the mean at 256 tokens per step and 1.26 times at 4,096, close to the Poisson formula. Real routers are not uniform on real traffic: some experts are popular for a given mix of requests. With mildly skewed popularity, the imbalance stops falling with batch size and settles near 2.3, because it is no longer noise but structure. Replicating the 32 most popular experts on extra GPUs, so that their load is shared, brings it down to about 1.5. Since the step waits for the busiest GPU, an imbalance of 2.3 means that, on average, GPUs spend more than half of each MoE layer waiting. Balancing at deployment time is therefore not a refinement but a requirement, and §19.6 describes how it is done. First we need to understand why MoE deployments must run such large batches in the first place.

19.4 The arithmetic of MoE inference

Recall the arithmetic intensity of decode for a dense model (§6.3): a step with batch \(B\) reads each weight once and uses it for \(B\) tokens, so the linear layers perform about \(2B\) FLOPs per weight read, \(2B/b_w\) FLOPs per byte, and become compute-bound when this exceeds the ridge point. For an MoE layer the same reasoning applies to each expert separately. An expert's weights are read once per step if any token is routed to it, and used for the \(m_e\) tokens that are, so its intensity is about \(2m_e/b_w\), with

\[ m_e\;\approx\;\frac{Bk_r}{n_{\text{exp}}}. \]

For DeepSeek-V3, \(k_r/n_{\text{exp}}=1/32\), so each expert sees one thirty-second of the tokens in the step. For an expert to reach the ridge point in FP8 on an H100 (\(\pi=1{,}979\) TFLOP/s, \(b_w=1\), ridge \(\approx590\) FLOP/B), it needs \(m_e\approx295\) tokens, which means \(B\approx295\times32\approx9{,}400\) tokens per step across the whole expert-parallel group. A dense model reaches its ridge at a batch of about 300; DeepSeek-V3's experts need thirty-two times that.

Figure 19.3 draws the consequence for several models. Each line is \(m_e\) as a function of the total batch, and the horizontal line marks the ridge. The sparser the model, the further right it crosses: Mixtral at about 1,200 tokens per step, DeepSeek-V3 at about 9,400, Kimi K2 at about 14,000. Below the crossing, the experts are memory-bound, and each step's time is set by reading expert weights that serve few tokens each. This is the central fact of MoE serving: the sparsity that makes MoE cheap in FLOPs makes it expensive in bytes, unless the batch is enormous.

The same calculation explains the behaviour at small batch. At batch 1, a token touches only \(k_r\) experts per layer, so only the active parameters are read, about 37 GB in FP8 for DeepSeek-V3, and the model runs like a 37B dense model. As the batch grows, the number of distinct experts touched grows as \(n_{\text{exp}}\big(1-(1-k_r/n_{\text{exp}})^B\big)\), the same union formula as contextual sparsity (§15.8), and approaches all of them: at 128 tokens, 98% of DeepSeek-V3's experts are touched in each layer. In the middle range, from a few dozen to a few thousand tokens, the step reads nearly all 671 GB of weights while each expert does very little work, the worst of both worlds. MoE serving therefore wants either very small batches, for latency on a dedicated replica, or very large ones, spread over enough GPUs that each holds few experts and receives many tokens. The second is what makes MoE economical, and it requires expert parallelism.

Worked example — DeepSeek-V3 on one 8×H200 server

FP8 weights (671 GB) fit in eight H200s (141 GB each, 1,128 GB in total), leaving about 400 GB for MLA cache and activations, roughly 6 million cached tokens at 68.6 KiB each. Batch 256 at 4k context: each step reads nearly all 671 GB of weights (all experts are touched) plus \(256\times4096\times68.6\) KiB ≈ 72 GB of cache, 743 GB in total, about 93 GB per GPU, 19 ms at 4.8 TB/s, plus communication: a TPOT of perhaps 25 ms for 256 sequences, about 1,300 tokens/s per GPU. Each expert sees \(m_e=256\times8/256=8\) tokens per step, far below the ridge. Batch 1,024: the weight term is unchanged, the cache grows to about 295 GB (larger batches at this context no longer fit), and the step takes about 25 ms plus communication, perhaps 30 ms in all, for 1,024 sequences: about 4,300 tokens/s per GPU, with \(m_e=32\). Throughput per GPU rises almost linearly with batch until the cache dominates, exactly as for a dense model, but the weight term is that of a 671B model, not a 37B one. Single-server MoE serving is memory-bandwidth-bound on expert weights at all practical batch sizes.

19.5 Expert parallelism

Tensor parallelism would split each expert's matrices across GPUs. For small experts that is wasteful: a 7168 × 2048 matrix split eight ways gives each GPU a 7168 × 256 slice, too thin for efficient GEMMs, and every MoE layer would need its all-reduce. Expert parallelism (EP) instead places whole experts on different GPUs: with \(n_{\text{EP}}\) GPUs, each holds \(n_{\text{exp}}/n_{\text{EP}}\) experts of every layer. A token's hidden state is sent to the GPUs holding its chosen experts (dispatch), each expert processes the tokens it received as one batched GEMM, and the results are sent back to the token's home GPU and summed with the gating weights (combine). Dispatch and combine are all-to-all collectives (§17.2), one pair per MoE layer.

Figure 19.4 shows the standard arrangement, in which expert parallelism for the MoE layers is combined with data parallelism for attention. Each GPU holds a full copy of the attention weights, which are small for MoE models, and runs attention only for its own requests, with their KV caches. This is the data-parallel attention of §18.6, and it is natural here for two reasons: it avoids replicating MLA's single latent cache, and it means the whole expert-parallel group, every GPU, contributes tokens to every expert, so the effective expert batch is the sum of all GPUs' batches. With \(n_{\text{EP}}\) GPUs each running \(B_{\text{gpu}}\) sequences, each expert sees \(m_e\approx n_{\text{EP}}B_{\text{gpu}}k_r/n_{\text{exp}}\) tokens, and each GPU holds only \(n_{\text{exp}}/n_{\text{EP}}\) experts per layer, so its weight bytes per step shrink as \(1/n_{\text{EP}}\). Wide expert parallelism attacks both halves of the problem of §19.4 at once.

The price is the all-to-all. Each token sends its hidden state, \(d\) values, to up to \(k_r\) GPUs, and receives \(k_r\) results back. For DeepSeek-V3, a token's dispatch is \(8\times7168\) values, 57 KB in FP8, and its combine 115 KB in BF16, about 172 KB per token per MoE layer, 10 MB per token per step over 58 layers. Two techniques reduce the inter-server part. Node-limited routing restricts each token's experts to a few servers, so a token is sent across the network at most once per destination server and then forwarded within the server over NVLink, cutting inter-server traffic by up to half for top-8 routing over four nodes. And low-precision dispatch sends FP8 activations, as above. DeepEP (§17.6) implements these patterns with kernels tuned for both phases: high-throughput kernels that forward between NVLink and RDMA domains for prefill, and low-latency kernels using pure RDMA for decode, which do not occupy SMs while waiting so that computation can proceed.

Worked example — can the all-to-all be hidden?

Consider DeepSeek-V3 decode, per token and per MoE layer. Expert compute: 8 routed and 1 shared expert, \(9\times2\times44\times10^{6}\approx0.8\) GFLOPs, about 1.3 µs at 600 TFLOP/s per GPU, if the experts were compute-bound. Communication: 172 KB per token; with node-limited routing, perhaps half crosses the network, 86 KB at 50 GB/s, about 1.7 µs, with the rest on NVLink. Attention: MLA decode at 4k context reads \(4096\times68.6\) KiB/61 layers ≈ 4.6 MB of cache per token per layer, about 1.4 µs at 3.35 TB/s. Communication is therefore comparable to the computation it must hide behind, and serial execution would roughly double the MoE layer's time. Hence the two-batch overlap of §17.7: one micro-batch's dispatch and combine run while the other computes attention and experts.

Expert parallelism interacts with the other strategies in characteristic ways. Within a server, MoE models can also use tensor parallelism for the experts, which avoids all-to-all at the cost of thin GEMMs; this is common for models with few, large experts like Mixtral on eight GPUs. Across servers, wide EP with data-parallel attention is standard for fine-grained models. And because the all-to-all's volume depends only on the tokens, not on the number of GPUs, EP scales unusually well: doubling \(n_{\text{EP}}\) halves the experts per GPU and doubles each expert's share of the total batch, while the bytes each GPU sends per token stay about the same. What does not scale automatically is balance.

19.6 Balancing experts at deployment time

Figure 19.2 showed that structural imbalance, popular experts that receive more than their share, persists at any batch size, and that random imbalance is large at moderate batch. At deployment, the remedy is to decide where experts live, using statistics of their recent load. DeepSeek's expert-parallel load balancer (EPLB)[6], released in 2025, does two things. It creates redundant experts: the most heavily loaded experts are replicated on additional GPUs, and their tokens are split among the replicas. And it packs experts onto GPUs so that each GPU's total expected load is as even as possible, a bin-packing problem solved greedily. Because expert popularity drifts with the mix of traffic, the placement is recomputed periodically from recent statistics and applied by moving weights between GPUs.

Figure 19.5 shows the two moves on a toy example of eight experts and four GPUs. Packing is a classical problem with a clean formulation. Given expected loads \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots\) for the experts (or expert replicas, after splitting hot experts' loads among their copies) and \(n_{\text{EP}}\) GPUs, assign experts to GPUs to minimise the maximum total load. This is the multiprocessor scheduling problem, NP-hard in general, but the greedy rule of placing each expert, heaviest first, on the currently least-loaded GPU (the "longest processing time" rule) is guaranteed to be within a factor \(4/3\) of optimal, and in practice is much closer when there are many experts per GPU. DeepSeek's balancer applies it hierarchically, first assigning groups of experts to servers, so that node-limited routing still holds, and then experts to GPUs within each server.

Redundancy has a memory cost, the replicated experts' weights, and a choice of how many replicas to create. DeepSeek reported deploying 32 redundant experts in prefill, and in decode an arrangement in which each of 256 GPUs hosts one routed expert and 64 more GPUs host redundant and shared experts[5]. The simulation of Figure 19.2 shows the effect of a comparable step: replicating the 32 hottest of 256 experts cut the steady-state imbalance from about 2.3 to about 1.5. Further gains come from more replicas, finer statistics and faster re-placement; the remaining imbalance is the irreducible randomness of each step, which only larger batches reduce.

Drill 19.1 — Imbalance and throughput

An EP deployment of DeepSeek-V3 decodes 4,096 tokens per step over 256 GPUs, one routed expert per GPU. Using the Poisson estimate, what is the expected max-to-mean load ratio for a uniform router, and what fraction of the MoE layer's GPU time is spent waiting? How large must the batch be to bring the ratio below 1.1?

Solution. \(m_e=4096\times8/256=128\), so the ratio is about \(1+\sqrt{2\ln256/128}=1+\sqrt{11.1/128}\approx1.29\), close to the simulated 1.26. If the step waits for the busiest GPU, the average GPU is busy \(1/1.29\approx78\%\) of the MoE layer and waits 22%. For a ratio of 1.1 we need \(\sqrt{11.1/m_e}\le0.1\), so \(m_e\ge1{,}110\) and \(B\ge35{,}500\) tokens per step. With real, skewed popularity, redundancy and placement are needed as well, since structural imbalance does not shrink with batch size.

19.7 Case study: serving DeepSeek-V3

DeepSeek's own deployment of V3 and R1, described in the V3 technical report and in an overview published in March 2025[7], brings together almost every idea in this book, and it is worth reading as a worked design. The system is disaggregated (Chapter 13): prefill and decode run on separate units with different parallel configurations, sized for their phases. Both use H800 GPUs, the export variant of the H100 with reduced NVLink bandwidth, which made inter-GPU communication an even more pressing constraint than on H100s.

Figure 19.6 summarises the two units. The prefill unit spans 4 nodes and 32 GPUs. Attention runs with four-way tensor parallelism and sequence parallelism, replicated eight ways; the MoE layers use 32-way expert parallelism, so each GPU holds 8 routed experts per layer, with 32 redundant copies of hot experts chosen by the load balancer. Prefill batches are large and compute-bound, so the main concern is hiding the all-to-all, which the system does by processing two micro-batches at once, overlapping one's communication with the other's attention and expert computation. The decode unit spans 40 nodes and 320 GPUs. The MoE layers use 320-way expert parallelism: 256 GPUs each hold one routed expert of every layer, and 64 hold redundant experts and the shared experts. Attention is data-parallel across 80 groups of four GPUs. Decode is latency-sensitive, so the all-to-all uses DeepEP's low-latency kernels, which communicate by RDMA directly from the GPU without occupying SMs, again with two micro-batches overlapped.

The decode design is the calculation of §19.4 carried to its conclusion. With one routed expert per GPU, each GPU's expert weight bytes per step are those of a single 44-million-parameter FFN per layer, about 2.6 GB over 58 layers in FP8, so almost all of the GPU's memory is available for MLA caches, and the batch per GPU can be large. With 320 GPUs each running their own requests' attention, the aggregate batch is hundreds of times the per-GPU batch, so each expert's \(m_e\) can approach the ridge of Figure 19.3. In this regime the experts finally do enough work per weight byte to use the tensor cores, and the model's sparsity pays off in cost.

The overview reported the results in production terms. Each H800 node delivered on average about 73,700 input tokens per second during prefill (including prefix-cache hits) or about 14,800 output tokens per second during decode, and the company reported a theoretical cost-profit margin of over 500% at its API prices[7]. Other techniques from earlier chapters complete the picture: FP8 computation throughout (Chapter 14), an on-disk prefix cache (Chapter 10), and multi-token-prediction modules used for speculative decoding (Chapter 20). Open-source engines followed within months: SGLang reproduced a large-scale deployment with prefill–decode disaggregation and wide expert parallelism on 96 H100s (§13.8), and vLLM, TensorRT-LLM and Dynamo added comparable support.

Drill 19.2 — The decode unit's numbers

A decode node of eight GPUs produces about 14,800 output tokens per second. Assuming a TPOT of about 50 ms, estimate the per-GPU batch, the aggregate batch over 320 GPUs, and the tokens per expert per step. Is the expert computation memory- or compute-bound? Check the network load per GPU.

Solution. Per GPU, \(14{,}800/8=1{,}850\) tokens/s; at 50 ms per step that is about 92 sequences per GPU, or about 29,600 tokens per step over 320 GPUs. Each of the 256 routed experts (plus replicas) receives about \(29{,}600\times8/256\approx925\) tokens per step, above the 295-token ridge, so the expert GEMMs are compute-bound: the deployment has reached the regime in which sparsity pays. Network: each token's dispatch and combine move about 10 MB per step over 58 layers, so \(1{,}850\times10\) MB ≈ 18.5 GB/s per GPU in total dispatch and combine, of which a fraction crosses the network, well within 50 GB/s per GPU. (The 50 ms TPOT is an assumption; the conclusion holds for any TPOT between about 20 and 100 ms.)

19.8 MoE at smaller scales

Few deployments have 320 GPUs for one model, and most MoE serving happens on one or two servers. There the arithmetic of §19.4 dominates. On a single node, the full set of expert weights must be read at nearly every step once batches exceed a few dozen, so the relevant speed is that of a dense model of the total size, and MoE's advantage over a dense model of the same quality lies in its lower compute and, at small batch, lower bytes. For models with few, large experts, such as Mixtral, tensor parallelism of the experts within a server is common and avoids all-to-all; for fine-grained models, expert parallelism within the NVLink domain is usual, with all-to-all over NVSwitch, which is fast enough that overlap matters less.

Quantisation is especially profitable for MoE. Experts hold almost all the parameters and, in the memory-bound regime, almost all the bytes per step, so 4-bit experts with higher-precision attention and router (§14.9) nearly halve the bytes relative to FP8 at little accuracy cost; gpt-oss's release with MXFP4 experts made a 117-billion-parameter model fit on a single 80 GB GPU. For local inference, a further trick is expert offloading: keep attention and shared layers on the GPU, keep the experts in CPU memory, and move or compute only the experts a token needs. At batch 1 only \(k_r\) experts per layer are needed, so this works far better for MoE than offloading does for dense models, and it is how very large MoE models are run on workstations (Chapter 29).

MoE models are, finally, not always the better choice for a given product. At low and moderate traffic, a deployment cannot accumulate the batches that make experts efficient, and it pays for the memory of all experts while using few; a dense model of similar quality, or a distilled one (Chapter 15), may be cheaper. At high traffic and with a large cluster, MoE models with wide expert parallelism are the most cost-efficient way known to serve frontier-quality models, which is why the largest providers use them. The choice is an instance of the recurring theme of this book: the right design depends on where the bytes and FLOPs of the actual workload are.

Drill 19.3 — Dense or MoE for a small deployment

A product needs quality comparable to a 70B dense model and expects about 20 concurrent users, with 2k-token contexts. Compare Llama 3 70B (FP8, TP = 2 on two H100s) with an MoE of 235B total and 22B active parameters (FP8, on four H100s), at batch 20.

Solution. Dense: each step reads 35.3 GB of weights and a little cache per GPU, about 11 ms, TPOT ≈ 12 ms, on 2 GPUs. MoE: at 20 tokens with top-8 of 128 experts, the expected fraction of experts touched per layer is \(1-(1-1/16)^{20}\approx73\%\), so a step reads about 8 GB of always-active weights plus 73% of the roughly 227 GB of routed experts, about 175 GB, or 44 GB per GPU on four GPUs, about 13 ms plus communication. The MoE is no faster per token, uses twice the GPUs and is memory-bound on experts it barely uses. At this traffic, the dense model is the better choice; the MoE would win at large batch on a larger deployment, where its 22B-active compute and amortised expert reads pay off.

Looking ahead

Part VI showed how to spread a model across many GPUs and keep them busy: collectives and their costs, tensor, pipeline and context parallelism, and expert parallelism for sparse models. All of it accepts one constraint we have not challenged since Chapter 3: autoregressive decoding produces one token per sequence per forward pass, so per-token latency is at least the time to read the weights that token needs. Part VII asks how to break that constraint. Chapter 20 begins with speculative decoding, which guesses several tokens cheaply and verifies them in a single forward pass of the large model, turning idle compute in memory-bound decode into extra tokens, and which DeepSeek-V3's multi-token prediction modules were partly designed to serve.

Chapter summary

  • MoE decouples parameters from per-token compute: Shazeer (2017) → GShard, Switch (2021) → Mixtral (2023) → DeepSeekMoE/V2/V3 (fine-grained and shared experts) → Qwen3, Llama 4, Kimi K2, gpt-oss. Total-to-active ratios rose from ≈ 4 to ≈ 20–30.
  • MoE layer: shared experts plus the gated sum \(\sum_{i\in\operatorname{TopK}}g_i\operatorname{FFN}_i(x)\) of the top-\(k_r\) routed experts; DeepSeek-V3: 256 routed experts of \(3dd_e=44\)M parameters, top-8, 1 shared; 671B total, 37B active.
  • Balance: Switch auxiliary loss \(\alpha_{\text{aux}}n_{\text{exp}}\sum f_i\bar p_i\), capacity factors (dropping is not acceptable at inference), DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free bias \(\omega_i\) used only for selection, node-limited routing. At inference, max/mean load ≈ \(1+\sqrt{2\ln n_{\text{exp}}/m_e}\) from randomness alone, plus structural skew.
  • Arithmetic: each expert sees \(m_e\approx Bk_r/n_{\text{exp}}\) tokens and has intensity \(2m_e/b_w\); DeepSeek-V3 needs ≈ 9,400 tokens per step for experts to reach the FP8 ridge. Distinct experts touched grow as \(n_{\text{exp}}(1-(1-k_r/n_{\text{exp}})^B)\), so mid-sized batches read almost all weights while each expert does little.
  • Expert parallelism: whole experts per GPU, dispatch and combine all-to-all per MoE layer (≈ 172 KB per token per layer for V3), data-parallel attention so all GPUs feed all experts, node-limited routing and FP8 dispatch to cut network bytes, DeepEP kernels, two-batch overlap.
  • Deployment balancing: EPLB replicates hot experts and packs experts greedily (heaviest first onto the least-loaded GPU, within 4/3 of optimal), hierarchically by node, recomputed as popularity drifts.
  • DeepSeek-V3: disaggregated; prefill on 32 GPUs (attention TP4+SP × DP8, EP32, 32 redundant experts); decode on 320 GPUs (EP320, one routed expert per GPU, DP80 attention); ≈ 73.7k input or 14.8k output tokens/s per H800 node.
  • At small scale MoE is memory-bound on all experts; quantise experts, use TP or EP within NVLink, offload experts for local use, and prefer dense or distilled models when traffic cannot fill large batches.

Warm-up What is a mixture-of-experts layer and why do frontier models use it?

An MoE layer replaces the FFN with many expert FFNs and a router. For each token, the router scores all experts (\(s=\sigma(xW_r)\) or a softmax), selects the top \(k_r\), and the output is the gated sum of those experts' outputs, plus any shared experts. Only selected experts run, so parameters grow with the number of experts while per-token compute grows only with \(k_r\). Since quality scales with parameters and training data, MoE gives large-model quality at medium-model training and inference FLOPs: DeepSeek-V3 has 671B parameters but 37B active, Kimi K2 about 1T and 32B. The costs are memory for all experts, load balancing, and communication when experts are spread over GPUs.

Warm-up What is expert parallelism?

Instead of splitting each expert's matrices across GPUs (tensor parallelism), EP places whole experts on different GPUs: with \(n_{\text{EP}}\) GPUs, each holds \(n_{\text{exp}}/n_{\text{EP}}\) experts of every MoE layer. Each token's hidden state is dispatched (all-to-all) to the GPUs holding its \(k_r\) experts, each GPU runs its experts as batched GEMMs over the tokens it received, and the results are combined (all-to-all) back on the token's home GPU with the gating weights. Usually attention is data-parallel, with each GPU serving its own requests. EP avoids thin GEMMs and per-layer all-reduces and lets every GPU's tokens feed every expert, so experts see larger batches.

Core Derive the arithmetic intensity of an expert in decode and its consequence for batch size.

An expert's weights are read once per step if any token routes to it and used for \(m_e\) tokens; with random routing \(m_e\approx Bk_r/n_{\text{exp}}\). Its linear layers perform about \(2m_e\) FLOPs per weight, so intensity is \(2m_e/b_w\) FLOPs per byte, the dense formula with \(B\) replaced by \(m_e\). To reach the ridge (about 590 FLOP/B for FP8 on H100, so \(m_e\approx295\) with \(b_w=1\)), DeepSeek-V3 (\(k_r/n_{\text{exp}}=1/32\)) needs about 9,400 tokens per step across the EP group, Kimi K2 about 14,000, Mixtral about 1,200, against about 300 for a dense model. Below that, experts are memory-bound and the step reads most of the total weights for little work. Hence wide EP over many GPUs with data-parallel attention and large aggregate batches.

Core How does load balancing work in training, and why is it still a problem at inference?

Training uses auxiliary losses (Switch: \(\alpha n_{\text{exp}}\sum_if_i\bar p_i\), minimised by uniform routing), capacity factors with token dropping, or DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free bias added to affinities for selection only and adjusted by observed load, plus a tiny sequence-level loss. These balance load on average over training data. At inference, each step's batch is a random sample of current traffic: even a uniform router gives max/mean load of about \(1+\sqrt{2\ln n_{\text{exp}}/m_e}\) (≈ 2.1 at 8 tokens per expert, ≈ 1.26 at 128), and real traffic skews popularity, giving persistent imbalance (≈ 2.3 in our simulation). Since every MoE layer waits for the busiest GPU, imbalance directly wastes throughput, so deployments replicate hot experts and pack experts by load (EPLB) and use large batches; dropping tokens is not acceptable because outputs would depend on batch composition.

Core Explain auxiliary-loss-free balancing in DeepSeek-V3.

Each routed expert \(i\) has a bias \(\omega_i\). For selection, the router ranks \(s_i+\omega_i\) and takes the top 8; the gating weights use the unbiased \(s_i\) (normalised over the selected experts). After each training step, for each expert, if its load exceeded the average, \(\omega_i\) is decreased by a small step \(\gamma\); if below, increased. The bias steers selection towards balance like a feedback controller, without adding a loss term whose gradient would push the router away from its preferred, quality-improving choices. DeepSeek reported better model quality than with auxiliary-loss balancing; a very small sequence-wise auxiliary loss remains to prevent extreme imbalance within single sequences.

Core Why does data-parallel attention pair naturally with expert parallelism?

In an MoE model, attention (with its KV cache) is per-request state and its weights are small, while the experts are shared weights that dominate memory. Data-parallel attention gives each GPU its own requests and caches, avoiding the replication of MLA's single latent cache that tensor-parallel attention would require, and avoiding attention all-reduces. Then all GPUs dispatch their tokens into the same expert-parallel MoE layers, so each expert receives tokens from every GPU: its effective batch is \(n_{\text{EP}}B_{\text{gpu}}k_r/n_{\text{exp}}\), which is what makes the experts efficient. Each sub-layer is partitioned along the axis that suits its state: requests for attention, experts for the FFN.

Core How much communication does an MoE layer need, and how is it reduced?

Per token per MoE layer, dispatch sends the hidden state to \(k_r\) expert GPUs and combine returns \(k_r\) results: \(2k_rd\) values. For DeepSeek-V3, \(8\times7168\) values each way: 57 KB in FP8 dispatch and 115 KB in BF16 combine, about 172 KB per token per layer, 10 MB per token per step over 58 layers. Reductions: node-limited routing (experts on at most a few nodes) plus sending each token once per destination node and forwarding over NVLink; FP8 dispatch; DeepEP kernels optimised for NVLink/RDMA forwarding (prefill) and low-latency pure RDMA (decode) that do not occupy SMs while waiting; and two-batch overlap to hide what remains behind attention and expert compute.

Deep-dive Walk through DeepSeek-V3’s production deployment and explain each design choice.

Disaggregated (Chapter 13). Prefill unit: 4 nodes, 32 H800s; attention TP4 with sequence parallelism, DP8; MoE EP32 with 32 redundant experts from EPLB; two micro-batches overlap all-to-all with compute. Prefill is compute-bound with large batches, so moderate EP suffices and the focus is hiding communication. Decode unit: 40 nodes, 320 GPUs; attention TP4+SP, DP80; MoE EP320 with one routed expert per GPU on 256 GPUs and 64 GPUs for redundant and shared experts; DeepEP low-latency RDMA all-to-all; two-batch overlap. Decode is memory-bound and needs \(m_e\) near the ridge: one expert per GPU minimises per-GPU weight bytes (freeing memory for MLA caches and large per-GPU batches), and 320-way DP attention multiplies the aggregate batch so each expert sees hundreds of tokens per step. FP8 throughout, prefix cache on disk, MTP for speculation. Reported ≈ 73.7k input tokens/s per node in prefill and ≈ 14.8k output tokens/s per node in decode.

Deep-dive Quantify load imbalance for EP decode and propose mitigations.

With \(m_e\) tokens per expert, Poisson fluctuation gives max/mean ≈ \(1+\sqrt{2\ln n_{\text{exp}}/m_e}\): for 256 experts, 2.1 at \(m_e=8\), 1.5 at 32, 1.26 at 128, 1.1 at about 1,100. Skewed popularity adds a persistent component. Since each layer waits for the busiest GPU, efficiency ≈ 1/(max/mean). Mitigations: larger aggregate batches (more DP attention ranks, larger per-GPU batches enabled by small per-GPU expert bytes); redundant replicas of hot experts with load split among replicas; load-aware packing (heaviest-first onto least-loaded GPU, hierarchically by node to respect node-limited routing), recomputed periodically from recent statistics; placing several cold experts per GPU and fewer hot ones; and overlapping communication so waiting time can absorb other work. Measure per-layer, per-GPU expert loads in production to drive placement.

Deep-dive When is a large MoE model cheaper to serve than a dense model of similar quality?

MoE saves FLOPs per token (active parameters) but costs memory for all experts and, in the mid-batch regime, bytes for nearly all experts per step. It is cheaper when traffic is high enough to form very large aggregate batches over a wide EP deployment, so each expert's weights are amortised over hundreds of tokens and the GPU is compute-efficient on a model whose FLOPs per token are a fraction of the dense equivalent's; and when the cluster has the network and software (DeepEP, overlap, EPLB) to make all-to-all cheap. At batch 1 it is also fast (only active weights read), useful for single-user latency. It is more expensive at moderate traffic on a few GPUs, where every step reads most of the total weights for a small batch: a dense or distilled model of similar quality then wins (Drill 19.3).

Deep-dive Compare tensor parallelism and expert parallelism for the experts of Mixtral and of DeepSeek-V3.

Mixtral has 8 large experts (\(d_{ff}=14{,}336\)), top-2. On 8 GPUs, TP shards each expert into 1,792-wide slices, still reasonable GEMMs, with one all-reduce per MoE layer and perfectly balanced work; EP would put one expert per GPU, where with top-2 of 8 each GPU sees a quarter of tokens and imbalance from 8-way random routing is significant. TP is common. DeepSeek-V3 has 256 small experts (\(d_e=2048\)), top-8: TP over 8 GPUs gives 256-wide slices, very thin GEMMs, and an all-reduce per layer; EP places 32 experts per GPU (or one per GPU at EP256+), keeping GEMMs whole, with all-to-all instead, and scales to hundreds of GPUs. EP is standard. Hybrids use TP within groups and EP across.

Deep-dive Why does MoE serving at moderate batch behave like serving a dense model of the total size?

Each token touches \(k_r\) experts per layer, but a batch of \(B\) tokens touches \(n_{\text{exp}}(1-(1-k_r/n_{\text{exp}})^B)\) distinct experts, which approaches all of them quickly (98% of DeepSeek-V3's at 128 tokens). Each touched expert's weights must be read once per step. So for batches of a few dozen tokens or more, the step reads nearly all expert weights, like a dense model of total size, while each expert does work for only \(m_e=Bk_r/n_{\text{exp}}\) tokens. Compute is low (active parameters) and bytes are high (total parameters): the step is memory-bound, and its time scales with the total model size divided by aggregate bandwidth, until batches are large enough to make \(m_e\) approach the ridge.

Design Design the serving system for a 1T-parameter MoE (384 experts, top-8, MLA) for a high-traffic API.

Disaggregate prefill and decode. Weights in FP8 (≈ 1 TB) or 4-bit experts with FP8 attention. Decode: wide EP over hundreds of GPUs (or NVL72 racks to keep all-to-all on NVLink), one or a few experts per GPU, data-parallel MLA attention with large per-GPU batches, aiming for \(m_e\) near the ridge (≈ 14,000 tokens per step for 8/384 at the FP8 ridge); DeepEP-style low-latency all-to-all with FP8 dispatch and node-limited routing; two-batch overlap; EPLB with redundant hot experts recomputed from production statistics. Prefill: smaller EP units, large batches, overlap, prefix cache with a tiered KV store and KV-aware routing. Speculative decoding (MTP heads) for latency. Planner to rebalance prefill/decode pools and replicas; monitoring of per-expert load, all-to-all latency, imbalance and TPOT. Start from a smaller EP prototype and validate against the model of §19.4.

Design A startup wants to self-host Qwen3-235B-A22B for an internal tool with about 30 users. Advise.

At 30 concurrent users, batches are small: with top-8 of 128 experts, 30 tokens touch about \(1-(15/16)^{30}\approx86\%\) of experts per layer, so each step reads most of the ~235 GB (FP8) of weights while each expert serves about 2 tokens: memory-bound on the total size. On four H100s (FP8), expect TPOT around 15–20 ms; on 8 GPUs with EP or TP, lower. Options: quantise experts to 4 bits (≈ 120 GB) to fit fewer GPUs and halve bytes; use a single 8-GPU node with EP within NVLink; or choose a dense 70B-class or distilled model, which at this traffic is likely cheaper for similar quality. If latency per user matters more than cost, the MoE at very small batch reads mainly active weights and can be fast on adequate hardware.

Design How would you implement and validate EPLB-style expert placement in an engine?

Collect per-layer, per-expert token counts every step (cheap counters on the router output), aggregate over a sliding window. Periodically compute a placement: choose redundant replicas for the experts with the highest expected loads (subject to memory), split their load among replicas, then pack experts to GPUs heaviest-first onto the least-loaded GPU, hierarchically by node to respect node-limited routing, minimising maximum expected load. Apply by loading new expert weights into spare memory and switching routing tables at a step boundary, then freeing old copies, with no pause in serving. The dispatcher must choose among replicas (round-robin or by current queue). Validate: measure per-GPU expert time per layer before and after (max/mean), end-to-end TPOT and throughput, and stability under traffic shifts; guard against oscillation with hysteresis.

Core What changed from Mixtral to DeepSeek-V3 in MoE design, and what did it mean for serving?

Mixtral (2023): 8 large experts, top-2, no shared experts; total/active ≈ 3.6. DeepSeek-V3 (2024): 256 fine-grained experts (\(d_e=2048\)), top-8, one shared expert, auxiliary-loss-free balancing, node-limited routing, MLA attention, FP8 training, MTP; total/active ≈ 18. For serving: far higher total memory per unit of compute, so multi-node deployment is unavoidable; experts too small for TP, so wide EP with all-to-all; each expert sees 1/32 of tokens, so huge aggregate batches are needed for efficiency; load imbalance across 256 experts requires EPLB; and MLA's compact cache frees memory for those large batches. Serving systems evolved accordingly: DeepEP, two-batch overlap, DP attention, disaggregation.
