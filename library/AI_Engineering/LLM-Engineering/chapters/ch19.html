Part VII · Faster Decoding and Longer Contexts — Chapter 20

Speculative Decoding

A decode step leaves the tensor cores almost idle, so checking several tokens costs little more than producing one. Speculative decoding exploits this by guessing a few tokens cheaply and verifying them all in one forward pass of the large model, with a rejection rule that makes the output distribution exactly that of the large model. This chapter proves the exactness, derives the speed-up, surveys where good guesses come from, from small models to EAGLE and DeepSeek's multi-token prediction, and explains why the gains depend on batch size and context length.

Why this chapter

Since Chapter 3 one constraint has governed the speed of generation: each forward pass of the model produces one token per sequence, and in decode that pass is memory-bound, its time set by reading the weights and the cache. At batch 1, Llama 3 70B on eight H100s performs about \(1.4\times10^{11}\) FLOPs per token while the GPUs could perform about \(4\times10^{13}\) in the same 6 milliseconds; more than 99% of the compute is idle. Batching fills that idle compute with other users' tokens, which raises throughput but does nothing for the latency of any one of them. Can the idle compute be used to make a single sequence faster?

The answer, found independently by groups at Google and DeepMind in late 2022, is yes, and the idea is pleasingly simple. Suppose a cheap process, a small model or a few extra layers, guesses the next \(\gamma\) tokens. The large model can then process all \(\gamma\) guesses in one forward pass, exactly as it processes a prompt in prefill, and in that single pass compute its own predictions at every position. Where the guesses agree with what the large model would have produced, they are kept; at the first disagreement, the large model's own token is used instead. If the guesses are often right, several tokens are produced for the price of one large-model step. The surprise, and the reason the technique is used everywhere, is that a carefully designed acceptance rule makes the output distribution identical to sampling from the large model alone, at any temperature. Speculative decoding is a pure speed-up, with no approximation.

This chapter develops the method in the order an inference engineer needs it. We prove exactness, derive the expected speed-up and the optimal number of guesses, and then turn to the practical question of where guesses come from, a field that moved from separate draft models (2023) to heads and feature-level drafters (Medusa, EAGLE, 2024–25) to drafting modules trained into the model itself (DeepSeek-V3's multi-token prediction). We end with speculation in a serving engine, where batching changes the economics: the idle compute that speculation consumes is the same idle compute that batching wants.

20.1 Idle compute in decode

Recall from Chapter 6 the time of a decode step as the larger of its memory time and its compute time. For a batch of \(B\) sequences with \(m\) new tokens each, the linear layers perform about \(2NBm\) FLOPs and read the weights once, while attention reads each sequence's cache once regardless of how many new tokens it processes. In ordinary decode \(m=1\). If instead each sequence processes \(m=\gamma+1\) tokens in one pass, the bytes are essentially unchanged and the FLOPs grow by a factor \(\gamma+1\). As long as the step stays memory-bound, which for Llama 3 8B on an H100 means \(B(\gamma+1)\) below about 300 (§6.4), the pass takes almost exactly as long as an ordinary decode step. Processing five tokens costs the same as processing one.

The difficulty is that autoregressive generation does not have five tokens to process: token \(t+1\) depends on token \(t\), which has not yet been produced. This is where guessing comes in. The idea has a history. Stern, Shazeer and Uszkoreit's blockwise parallel decoding (2018) trained extra output heads to predict several future tokens and verified them with the base model, accepting the longest prefix that matched the base model's greedy choices. It worked for greedy decoding but not for sampling. In November 2022, Leviathan, Kalman and Matias at Google[1], and in February 2023 Chen and colleagues at DeepMind[2], independently introduced speculative sampling, which generalises the idea to sampling with an exact acceptance rule, and showed 2–3× speed-ups on T5-XXL and on Chinchilla 70B. Within a year it was in every major serving engine.

Figure 20.1 compares the two timelines. In the ordinary one, four target passes produce four tokens. In the speculative one, a drafter proposes four tokens, the target model verifies them in one pass, three are accepted, and the target contributes the fourth token itself, so four tokens cost one target pass and four cheap draft steps. Everything depends on two numbers: how often drafts are accepted, and how cheap drafting is. Before computing the speed-up, though, we must say exactly what "accepted" means, because a naive rule, keeping drafts that match the target's most likely token, would change the distribution of the output whenever we sample.

20.2 Speculative sampling is exact

Fix a prefix and consider one position. The target model's next-token distribution is \(p(v)\), after all sampling transformations (temperature, top-\(p\) and so on; §4.3–4.5), and the drafter's is \(q(v)\). The drafter has sampled a token \(\hat x\sim q\). We want a rule that either accepts \(\hat x\) or replaces it, such that the final token is distributed exactly as \(p\).

Speculative sampling at one position

Accept \(\hat x\) with probability \(\min\!\big(1,\;p(\hat x)/q(\hat x)\big)\). If it is rejected, sample a replacement from the residual distribution

\[ r(v)\;=\;\frac{\max\big(0,\,p(v)-q(v)\big)}{\sum_{u}\max\big(0,\,p(u)-q(u)\big)}. \]
Theorem 19.1 — Exactness

The token produced by this rule is distributed according to \(p\). The probability that the draft is accepted is \(a=\sum_v\min\big(p(v),q(v)\big)=1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\), where \(\mathrm{TV}\) is the total-variation distance.

Proof. The output equals \(v\) either because the draft was \(v\) and was accepted, or because some draft was rejected and \(v\) was drawn from \(r\). The first has probability \(q(v)\min(1,p(v)/q(v))=\min(p(v),q(v))\). The total rejection probability is \(1-\sum_u\min(p(u),q(u))=1-a\). Since \[\sum_u\max\big(0,p(u)-q(u)\big)=\sum_u\big(p(u)-\min(p(u),q(u))\big)=1-a,\] which is exactly the total rejection probability, the second has probability \[(1-a)\,r(v)=\max\big(0,p(v)-q(v)\big)=p(v)-\min\big(p(v),q(v)\big).\] Adding the two gives \(p(v)\). The acceptance probability is \(\sum_vq(v)\min(1,p(v)/q(v))=\sum_v\min(p(v),q(v))\), and \(\sum_v\min(p,q)=1-\tfrac12\sum_v|p-q|=1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\). \(\square\)

Figure 20.2 shows the decomposition on five tokens. The accepted drafts deliver probability \(\min(p,q)\) at each token, which is too little wherever the drafter under-proposes. Rejections happen where the drafter over-proposes, and the residual distribution places its mass exactly on the shortfall. The decomposition is the coupling that achieves the total-variation distance: no rule that always samples from \(q\) first can accept more often than \(1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\) while preserving \(p\), so speculative sampling's acceptance rate is optimal for a single draft.

Over several positions the rule is applied sequentially. The drafter proposes \(\hat x_1,\dots,\hat x_\gamma\), each sampled from \(q\) given the prefix and the previous drafts. The target computes \(p\) at all \(\gamma+1\) positions in one pass. The verifier accepts or rejects \(\hat x_1\); if accepted, it moves to \(\hat x_2\), whose target distribution was computed conditional on \(\hat x_1\), which is now known to be correct; and so on. At the first rejection, it samples the replacement from that position's residual and discards all later drafts, whose conditioning was wrong. If all \(\gamma\) drafts are accepted, the target's distribution at position \(\gamma+1\) is already computed, and one more token is sampled from it for free. By induction over positions, the whole sequence is distributed as if sampled from \(p\) token by token. For greedy decoding, the rule reduces to the obvious one: accept a draft if it equals the target's argmax.

Exactness has an important practical consequence. Speculative decoding is safe to deploy without any evaluation of output quality: the model's outputs, their statistics and their quality are unchanged. What changes is only speed, and speed depends on how well the drafter predicts the target, a property we can now quantify.

20.3 How much faster?

Model the acceptance of each draft as an independent event with probability \(a\), the per-token acceptance rate. A verification step with \(\gamma\) drafts produces \(n_{\text{acc}}\) tokens: the accepted prefix plus the one token the target always contributes. The number of accepted drafts is \(i\) with probability \(a^i(1-a)\) for \(i<\gamma\) and \(a^\gamma\) for \(i=\gamma\), so

\[ \mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{\gamma}a^i\;=\;\frac{1-a^{\gamma+1}}{1-a}. \]

The expectation is bounded by \(1/(1-a)\) however many drafts we propose: with \(a=0.8\), at most five tokens per step on average. Now the cost. A step costs \(\gamma\) draft steps, each a fraction \(c_d\) of a target decode step, plus one verification pass, which in the memory-bound regime costs about one target step. The speed-up over ordinary decoding is therefore

\[ S(\gamma)\;=\;\frac{1-a^{\gamma+1}}{(1-a)\,(1+\gamma c_d)}. \]

Figure 20.3 plots the speed-up for four acceptance rates with a draft that costs 5% of a target step. Each curve rises steeply for the first few drafts, then flattens, because each additional draft is accepted only if all previous ones were, with probability \(a^\gamma\), while its cost \(c_d\) is paid regardless; eventually the curve turns down. The optimum moves right as \(a\) grows: about \(\gamma=4\) for \(a=0.6\), 8 for \(a=0.8\), beyond 10 for \(a=0.9\). The ceiling is set by acceptance: even a free drafter cannot exceed \(1/(1-a)\), so a drafter accepted 60% of the time can at most give 2.5×, and to reach 4× requires \(a\) above 0.75 with many drafts. Most of the engineering of speculative decoding is therefore about acceptance rate, and secondarily about the cost of drafting.

Worked example — a small draft model for Llama 3 70B

Use Llama 3.2 1B as a draft for Llama 3 70B (they share a tokeniser), both on the same eight H100s with TP = 8, batch 1. Costs: the target step is about 6.1 ms (Chapter 18); the 1B draft reads 2.5 GB in BF16, but with TP = 8 its step is dominated by its 32 all-reduces and kernel launches, perhaps 0.6 ms, so \(c_d\approx0.1\). Acceptance: suppose \(a=0.7\) on chat traffic. Optimum: \(S(3)=(1-0.7^4)/(0.3\times1.3)\approx1.95\), \(S(4)\approx1.98\), \(S(5)\approx1.96\): about 2× at \(\gamma=4\), cutting TPOT from 6.1 ms to about 3.1 ms. If the draft ran on one GPU without tensor parallelism its step would be about 0.8 ms of memory time, similar; the lesson is that a separate draft model's overheads, not its FLOPs, set \(c_d\).

The independence assumption deserves a caveat. Real acceptance is not constant: it is high on predictable text (code boilerplate, repeated phrases, formatting) and low on creative or information-dense text, and it falls with temperature, because sampling at high temperature spreads \(p\) and makes it harder to match. Measured speed-ups therefore vary across workloads by a factor of two or more, and engines report the average accepted length, \(\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]\), as the key metric. With it, the question becomes: what makes a drafter that is both cheap and well matched to the target?

20.4 Where drafts come from

The first speculative systems used a separate small model from the same family as the target, sharing its tokeniser: T5-small for T5-XXL, a 4B model for Chinchilla 70B, later Llama 3.2 1B for Llama 3 70B. Small models agree with large ones on most easy tokens, giving acceptance rates of 0.6–0.8 on typical text. Their drawbacks are operational: a second model to deploy, keep in memory, schedule and keep in sync with the target's versions, with its own KV cache, and a mismatch in quality that cannot be improved without training a better small model.

At the other extreme are drafts that need no model at all. Prompt lookup or n-gram drafting searches the prompt and the text generated so far for the most recent occurrence of the last few tokens, and proposes the tokens that followed it. For tasks that copy from the input, such as summarisation, retrieval-augmented question answering, code editing and rewriting, this is remarkably effective and almost free. Retrieval-based drafting (REST) extends the idea to a datastore of common continuations, and lookahead decoding (Fu et al., 2024) generates n-gram candidates from the model's own parallel Jacobi iterations.

The most effective modern drafters are small networks attached to the target model itself. Medusa (Cai et al., 2024)[3] adds several extra output heads to the target's final hidden state; head \(k\) predicts the token \(k+1\) positions ahead, all in parallel, from the same feature. The heads are cheap (a single layer each), trained on the target's own outputs with the target frozen, and they share the target's KV cache and its deployment. Their weakness is that head \(k\) must predict token \(t+k+1\) without knowing tokens \(t+1,\dots,t+k\), so its accuracy falls quickly with \(k\). EAGLE (Li et al., 2024)[4] fixed this by drafting autoregressively at the feature level: a one-layer Transformer takes the target's second-to-top-layer feature vector together with the embedding of the token just sampled, and predicts the next feature vector, from which the target's own LM head produces the draft token, and the process repeats. Because each draft step conditions on the previous draft token, accuracy decays slowly with depth. EAGLE reported 2.7–3.5× speed-ups on Llama 2 Chat 70B. EAGLE-2 (2024) added draft trees whose shape adapts to the drafter's confidence (§20.5), and EAGLE-3 (2025)[5] removed the feature-prediction objective, fused features from several target layers, and trained the drafter on its own predictions ("training-time test"), so that acceptance improved further as training data grew, reporting speed-ups up to about 6.5× on some tasks.

Figure 20.4 places these drafters side by side. The rightmost option closes the loop: train the model to draft for itself. DeepSeek-V3 was trained with a multi-token prediction (MTP) objective[6]: in addition to the main next-token loss, one extra Transformer block, sharing the embedding and output head, takes the main model's representation at position \(t\) and the embedding of token \(t+1\), and predicts token \(t+2\). The MTP objective was introduced to improve training, densifying the learning signal; at inference, the MTP block becomes a drafter that is exactly matched to the model. DeepSeek reported that its second-token prediction was accepted 85–90% of the time across topics, giving about 1.8× more tokens per second. Several later open models ship MTP layers for this purpose, and engines such as SGLang and vLLM run them as EAGLE-style drafters.

Worked example — prompt lookup for a code edit

Setup. A user asks the model to rename a variable in a 300-line file, and the response is the whole file with a few lines changed. About 90% of the output tokens are copies of the prompt. Prompt-lookup drafting proposes the \(\gamma=8\) tokens that followed the last matched n-gram in the prompt, at no cost.

Copied stretches. When the output is copying, each drafted token is accepted with probability about \(a=0.95\), so a step yields \((1-a^{\gamma+1})/(1-a)=(1-0.95^9)/0.05\approx7.4\) tokens. The 90 copied tokens of every 100 take about \(90/7.4\approx12\) steps.

Edited stretches. Where the text changes, the lookup finds no match or a wrong one, and each step yields a single token: the 10 changed tokens of every 100 take about 10 steps. Verifying eight wrong drafts is almost free in the memory-bound regime of §20.1.

Speed-up. 100 tokens in about 22 steps instead of 100, a speed-up of about \(4.5\times\), with no draft model, no training and no extra memory. On open-ended chat the same method finds few matches and gives almost nothing, which is why engines enable it per request or combine it with a learned drafter. The example also shows that speed-up depends on the workload as much as on the method, so a speculative-decoding benchmark must name its data.

20.5 Verifying trees

A chain of drafts is fragile: if the first draft is wrong, all the others are wasted. But the drafter usually has a good second choice. If at the first position it is unsure between two tokens, why not draft continuations of both, and let the target decide? Tree-structured speculation, introduced in SpecInfer (Miao et al., 2023)[7] and used by Medusa and EAGLE, drafts a tree of candidate continuations and verifies the whole tree in one target pass.

The mechanism that makes this possible is a tree attention mask. Flatten the tree's nodes into a sequence of candidate tokens appended to the prefix. Each candidate must attend to the prefix and to its own ancestors in the tree, but not to its siblings or cousins, which belong to different hypothetical continuations. That is a mask like the causal mask of §2.4, except that the allowed pattern follows the tree's ancestor relation rather than the linear order. With it, one forward pass computes the target's distribution at every node, conditional on the path to that node, and the verifier walks down the tree, accepting the longest path that the acceptance rule allows. For sampling, the rule generalises to several candidates per position by applying the rejection step to each candidate in turn with an updated residual, which preserves exactness.

Worked example — a tree beats a chain at an uncertain first token

Suppose the drafter's top choice at the first position is accepted with probability 0.5, but its top two together cover 0.8, and later positions are each accepted with probability 0.8. Compare three drafts with the same budget of verification positions. Chain of 4: expected accepted drafts \(0.5\,(1+0.8+0.8^2+0.8^3)=0.5\times2.95\approx1.48\), so \(\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]\approx2.48\). Chain of 8: \(0.5\times(1-0.8^8)/0.2\approx2.08\) accepted, \(n_{\text{acc}}\approx3.08\). Tree of 8 nodes (two first-position candidates, each followed by a chain of three): the first position is accepted with probability 0.8, so \(0.8\times2.95\approx2.36\) accepted and \(n_{\text{acc}}\approx3.36\). With the same eight verification positions, the tree beats the longer chain because it spends its budget where the drafter is unsure, rather than deep in a chain that is unlikely to be reached.

Figure 20.5 shows a six-node tree and its mask. The tree costs six positions of verification instead of the three a single chain of the same depth would cost, and the mask's sparsity means attention does slightly redundant work, but in the memory-bound regime the extra positions are nearly free, and the expected accepted length rises because a wrong first guess no longer ends the round. Choosing the tree's shape is an optimisation problem: with a budget of \(n\) nodes, place them where the drafter's probability of the path being accepted is highest. Static trees (Medusa) fix the shape in advance from statistics; EAGLE-2 builds the tree dynamically each step, expanding the nodes whose estimated path probability, the product of the drafter's confidences along the path, is largest, which approximates acceptance probability well because EAGLE's drafter is well calibrated. Trees of 30–60 nodes are common, which is affordable only because verification of 60 positions still fits under the memory-bound roof at small batch. At large batch, it does not.

20.6 Speculation in a serving engine

Inside an engine, speculation touches every component of Chapter 12. The scheduler must budget for the extra positions: in vLLM V1's unified scheduler (§12.3), a speculating sequence simply asks for \(\gamma+1\) (or tree-size) tokens in the step's token budget, alongside prefill chunks and ordinary decodes. The KV-cache manager must write keys and values for draft tokens and discard those of rejected ones; with paged caches (Chapter 10) this is cheap, since rejected positions are simply not committed and the block's fill pointer is rolled back. The sampler must apply the same transformations (temperature, top-\(p\), penalties, grammar masks from structured output; §4.7) to both \(p\) and \(q\) before the acceptance test, or exactness is lost. And overlapped scheduling (§12.2) must cope with a variable number of accepted tokens per sequence per step.

The most important interaction is with batching. Speculation spends idle compute, and so does batching. At batch 1 nearly all of the GPU's compute is idle, so verifying \(\gamma+1\) positions is free. As the batch grows, the verification pass processes \(B(\gamma+1)\) tokens, which reaches the compute roof sooner; beyond that point every rejected draft is paid for in real compute, and speculation can slow down the system. What determines where that happens is not the batch size alone but the balance of bytes and FLOPs in the step, and here the KV cache plays a surprising role. The verification pass reads each sequence's cache once, however many draft positions it verifies, so long contexts, which make the step more memory-bound, leave more room for speculation.

Figure 20.6 shows the effect for Llama 3 8B with an EAGLE-like drafter. At a context of 256 tokens, the step is weight-dominated, verification of five positions per sequence becomes compute-bound at a batch of about 64, and above a batch of about 200 speculation reduces throughput. At 2k context, the cache makes up most of the bytes, and speculation still gives more than 2× at batch 1,024. At 16k, it is free at every batch size in this model. This observation, made by MagicDec (Chen et al., 2024)[8], reversed the earlier belief that speculative decoding is only for small batches: for long-context serving, which is increasingly common with reasoning models and agents (Chapter 26), speculation remains valuable at high throughput.

Engines handle the interaction adaptively. They reduce \(\gamma\), or switch speculation off, when the batch is large and contexts short, and raise it when the system is lightly loaded; some choose \(\gamma\) per sequence from its recent acceptance rate. SmartSpec framed this as maximising goodput (§13.2) and chose the draft length per step from a model of acceptance and step cost. And because acceptance depends on the content, monitoring the mean accepted length per workload is part of operating speculation in production.

Drill 20.1 — Choosing γ

An EAGLE drafter has \(c_d=0.04\) and acceptance \(a=0.75\) on a workload. Find the speed-up for \(\gamma=3,5,7\) in the memory-bound regime, the best of these, and the ceiling for an ideal free drafter.

Solution. \(S(\gamma)=(1-0.75^{\gamma+1})/(0.25(1+0.04\gamma))\). \(\gamma=3\): \((1-0.316)/(0.25\times1.12)=0.684/0.28\approx2.44\). \(\gamma=5\): \((1-0.178)/(0.25\times1.2)=0.822/0.3\approx2.74\). \(\gamma=7\): \((1-0.100)/(0.25\times1.28)=0.900/0.32\approx2.81\). The best of the three is \(\gamma=7\), and the gain beyond 5 is small. With a free drafter the ceiling is \(1/(1-a)=4\). In a busy engine with short contexts, a smaller \(\gamma\) would be chosen to limit wasted compute on rejected drafts.

Drill 20.2 — Exactness with temperature and top-p

An engine applies temperature 0.7 and top-\(p\) 0.9 to the target's logits, but computes the draft distribution \(q\) at temperature 1 without top-\(p\). Is the output still distributed as the user requested? What is lost?

Solution. Theorem 19.1 holds for any \(q\): the output is distributed as whatever \(p\) the verifier uses, so if \(p\) is the processed target distribution (temperature 0.7, top-\(p\) 0.9), the output is exact. Correctness requires only that the draft tokens were actually sampled from the \(q\) used in the ratio \(p/q\). What is lost is acceptance: \(q\) at temperature 1 is much broader than the processed \(p\), so \(\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\) is larger and \(a\) smaller, and the speed-up falls. Applying the same processing to the draft improves acceptance without affecting exactness.

20.7 Self-speculation: the model drafts for itself

Every drafter so far is a separate set of weights, however small. A further idea is to let the target model draft with a cheaper version of itself. Draft-and-Verify (Zhang et al., 2023) skipped a subset of the target's intermediate layers to produce drafts, then verified them with the full model; LayerSkip (Elhoushi et al., 2024) trained models with layer dropout and an early-exit loss so that the first few layers, followed by the shared LM head, form a usable drafter, and verified with the remaining layers. Self-speculation has two attractions. It needs no extra parameters, so there is nothing to deploy or keep in sync. And the draft and verification passes share computation: the early layers' keys, values and activations computed during drafting are exactly those the verification pass needs, so they are reused rather than recomputed, and the cost of drafting is partly recovered.

The cost is accuracy. An early exit after a quarter of the layers is a much weaker predictor than a trained one-layer EAGLE head reading the target's final features, because it has not seen the later layers' refinements. Acceptance rates are correspondingly lower unless the model was trained for early exit, which is why self-speculation has been most successful in models built for it. Multi-token prediction can be read as the same idea taken to its natural end: the model is trained from the start so that a cheap extension of its own final representation predicts the next tokens well. Between external drafters, attached heads, early exits and trained MTP layers, the trend is towards drafting capacity that is designed into the model rather than bolted on, because a drafter that shares the model's representations and training is the cheapest way to make \(q\) close to \(p\).

Drill 20.3 — A residual distribution by hand

Over three tokens, the target distribution is \(p=(0.6,0.3,0.1)\) and the draft distribution is \(q=(0.3,0.6,0.1)\). Compute the acceptance probability, the residual distribution, and verify that the output distribution equals \(p\).

Solution. \(\min(p,q)=(0.3,0.3,0.1)\), so \(a=0.7\) and \(\mathrm{TV}(p,q)=0.3\). The residual numerators \(\max(0,p-q)=(0.3,0,0)\) sum to \(0.3=1-a\), so \(r=(1,0,0)\): every rejection is replaced by token 1. Output probabilities: token 1, \(\min(0.6,0.3)+0.3\times1=0.6\); token 2, \(\min(0.3,0.6)+0=0.3\); token 3, \(0.1\). This equals \(p\). Intuitively, the drafter over-proposes token 2 (0.6 against 0.3), half of those drafts are rejected (\(\min(1,0.3/0.6)=0.5\)), and the rejected mass, \(0.3\), is redirected to token 1, which the drafter under-proposes.

20.8 Beyond draft-and-verify

Speculative decoding keeps the autoregressive model and accelerates it. A family of related ideas asks whether the sequential dependence itself can be relaxed. Jacobi decoding treats the next \(n\) tokens as unknowns of a fixed-point equation, each equal to the model's greedy prediction given the others, and iterates all of them in parallel from an initial guess until they stop changing; it converges to the greedy output, often in fewer than \(n\) iterations, and lookahead decoding uses the n-grams that appear along the way as drafts. Consistency training (CLLMs, 2024) fine-tunes models so that Jacobi iteration converges in very few steps. Further afield, diffusion language models generate or refine many tokens at once over a fixed number of denoising steps, a genuinely non-autoregressive approach that began to show competitive results in 2025, though not yet at the frontier. These directions share speculative decoding's motivation, the idle compute of memory-bound generation, and are likely to matter more as models are trained with them in mind, as DeepSeek did with multi-token prediction.

For today's serving systems, the conclusion is practical. Speculation is exact, cheap to deploy when the model ships with a drafter or MTP layers, and gives 2–3× lower latency at small batch and long contexts. It is most valuable exactly where the next chapter's problems lie: long contexts, where every decode step reads an enormous cache.

Looking ahead

Speculation showed that long contexts change the economics of decode: the cache, not the weights, becomes the dominant cost of each step. Contexts of 128k tokens are now standard, and a million tokens is offered by several providers. At those lengths the cache of Llama 3 8B exceeds the memory of any GPU, prefill takes minutes, attention dominates both phases, and even the model's positional encoding must be extended beyond the lengths it was trained on. Chapter 21 takes up long-context inference: how positional encodings are extended (position interpolation, NTK scaling, YaRN), what long prompts cost and how they are distributed, and how sparse and approximate attention, cache compression and retrieval trade accuracy for the bytes of a million-token context.

Chapter summary

  • Memory-bound decode leaves compute idle, so verifying \(\gamma+1\) tokens costs about one decode step. Speculative decoding drafts \(\gamma\) tokens cheaply and verifies them in one target pass (Leviathan et al.; Chen et al., 2022–23; precursor: blockwise parallel decoding, 2018).
  • Speculative sampling: accept \(\hat x\sim q\) with probability \(\min(1,p/q)\), else sample from \(r\propto\max(0,p-q)\). The output is exactly distributed as \(p\); acceptance is \(a=\sum\min(p,q)=1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\), optimal for one draft. Greedy reduces to matching the argmax.
  • \(\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]=(1-a^{\gamma+1})/(1-a)\le1/(1-a)\); speed-up \(S(\gamma)=\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]/(1+\gamma c_d)\) in the memory-bound regime. Acceptance sets the ceiling; draft cost sets the optimal \(\gamma\).
  • Drafters: small sibling models (0.6–0.8 acceptance, operational cost), prompt lookup and n-grams (copying tasks), Medusa heads (parallel, decaying accuracy), EAGLE 1–3 (feature-level autoregressive drafting, dynamic trees, training-time test), and MTP modules trained with the model (DeepSeek-V3: 85–90% acceptance, ≈ 1.8× tokens/s).
  • Tree speculation (SpecInfer, Medusa, EAGLE-2) verifies many candidate paths in one pass using a tree attention mask in which each node attends to its ancestors; dynamic trees expand the most probable paths.
  • In engines: token-budget scheduling, KV rollback for rejected drafts, identical sampling transforms for \(p\) and \(q\), adaptive \(\gamma\). Batching consumes the same idle compute, so with short contexts speculation stops paying at moderate batch; long contexts keep it nearly free (MagicDec).
  • Related directions: Jacobi and lookahead decoding, consistency models, diffusion language models.

Warm-up Explain speculative decoding in two minutes.

Decode is memory-bound: each step reads all weights and the cache to produce one token per sequence, leaving tensor cores mostly idle. Because processing several tokens in one forward pass costs about the same, a cheap drafter (a small model, extra heads, an EAGLE drafter, MTP layers, or n-gram lookup) guesses the next \(\gamma\) tokens; the target model scores all \(\gamma+1\) positions in one pass; drafts are accepted left to right with probability \(\min(1,p/q)\), the first rejection is replaced by a sample from the residual \(\propto\max(0,p-q)\), and if all are accepted a bonus token is taken. The output distribution is exactly the target's, so quality is unchanged, and latency falls by \(\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]/(1+\gamma c_d)\), typically 2–3×.

Warm-up Why is speculative decoding exact? State the theorem.

With target \(p\), draft \(q\), draft \(\hat x\sim q\) accepted with probability \(\min(1,p(\hat x)/q(\hat x))\) and otherwise replaced by a sample from \(r(v)=\max(0,p(v)-q(v))/(1-a)\), the output is distributed as \(p\). Proof: P(output=v) = P(draft v and accepted) + P(reject)·r(v) = \(\min(p(v),q(v))+(1-a)\cdot\max(0,p(v)-q(v))/(1-a)=\min(p,q)+(p-\min(p,q))=p(v)\), using that the total rejection probability is \(1-\sum\min(p,q)=\sum\max(0,p-q)\). The acceptance rate \(a=\sum\min(p,q)=1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\) is the best achievable for one draft (the maximal coupling). Applied position by position, the whole sequence is exact.

Core Derive the expected speed-up and the optimal number of draft tokens.

With independent acceptance probability \(a\) per draft, accepted drafts follow a truncated geometric distribution, and each step contributes one target token, so \(\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]=\sum_{i=0}^{\gamma}a^i=(1-a^{\gamma+1})/(1-a)\le1/(1-a)\). Cost per step: \(\gamma\) draft steps at relative cost \(c_d\) plus one verification, about one target step while memory-bound. Speed-up \(S(\gamma)=(1-a^{\gamma+1})/((1-a)(1+\gamma c_d))\). Maximise over integer \(\gamma\): increasing \(\gamma\) adds \(a^{\gamma+1}\) tokens at cost \(c_d\); stop when the marginal gain ratio falls below the cost ratio. With \(c_d=0.05\): optimum about 4 for \(a=0.6\) (1.9×), 8 for \(a=0.8\) (3.1×), above 10 for \(a=0.9\). Acceptance sets the ceiling; draft cost sets the optimum; in compute-bound regimes verification cost also grows with \(\gamma\), shrinking the optimum.

Core Compare draft model, Medusa, EAGLE and MTP drafters.

Draft model: a small sibling sharing the tokeniser; no training if available; acceptance 0.6–0.8; costs a second model, its own KV cache, scheduling and versioning. Medusa: extra heads on the target's final hidden state predicting tokens at offsets +1, +2, …, in parallel; cheap and easy to train with the target frozen; but head \(k\) cannot see earlier draft tokens, so accuracy decays quickly with offset; uses static trees. EAGLE: a one-layer drafter that autoregressively predicts the target's features from previous features and the sampled token, reusing the target's LM head; much higher acceptance at depth; EAGLE-2 adds confidence-driven dynamic trees; EAGLE-3 fuses multi-layer features and trains on its own predictions, scaling with data. MTP: extra prediction blocks trained jointly with the model (DeepSeek-V3), well matched by construction (85–90% second-token acceptance), deployed like EAGLE drafters.

Core How does tree-based verification work, and why does it help?

Instead of one chain of drafts, the drafter proposes a tree: several candidates at uncertain positions, each extended further. All nodes are flattened into one sequence after the prefix and verified in a single target pass with a tree attention mask in which each node attends to the context, its ancestors and itself, so the target computes the distribution at each node conditional on that node's path. The verifier walks down the tree, accepting the longest acceptable path (with a multi-candidate rejection rule for exact sampling). Trees help because a wrong top guess no longer wastes the whole round: probability mass of second choices is captured. With the same number of verification positions, a well-shaped tree yields more accepted tokens than a long chain (worked example: 3.36 versus 3.08 for 8 positions). Trees cost more verification compute, so they suit small batches.

Core How does speculative decoding interact with batching?

Both consume the idle compute of memory-bound decode. Verification processes \(B(\gamma+1)\) tokens per step; while the step remains memory-bound, the extra positions are almost free; once \(B(\gamma+1)\) exceeds the critical batch, every rejected draft costs real compute, and speculation can reduce throughput. The crossover depends on the bytes in the step: weights are read once per step, and each sequence's cache once regardless of \(\gamma\), so long contexts (cache-dominated steps) stay memory-bound and keep speculation profitable at large batch (MagicDec), while short contexts lose the benefit at moderate batch (in our model, below 1× above about 200 sequences at 256-token contexts). Engines therefore adapt \(\gamma\) to load, context length and measured acceptance, or turn speculation off.

Core What must an engine do to integrate speculative decoding correctly?

Scheduler: allocate \(\gamma+1\) (or tree-size) positions per speculating sequence within the step's token budget, alongside prefill and decode. Drafter execution: run draft steps (separate model, heads or EAGLE/MTP layers) with their own caches or shared target features. KV cache: write target keys/values for all draft positions, commit only accepted ones and roll back the rest (cheap with paged blocks). Sampling: apply identical sampling transforms (temperature, top-\(p\)/\(k\), penalties, grammar masks) to the target distribution, sample drafts from the \(q\) used in the ratio, use proper random-number handling for reproducibility. Stop conditions and streaming must handle several tokens per step. Overlapped scheduling must handle variable accepted lengths. Metrics: acceptance rate and mean accepted length per workload.

Deep-dive Prove that the acceptance probability \(1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\) cannot be improved by any scheme that proposes from \(q\) and outputs exactly \(p\).

Consider any scheme that draws \(\hat x\sim q\), then outputs \(y\) with \(y\sim p\) marginally, and counts as accepted when \(y=\hat x\). The pair \((\hat x,y)\) is a coupling of \(q\) and \(p\). For any coupling, \(\Pr[\hat x=y]=\sum_v\Pr[\hat x=y=v]\le\sum_v\min(q(v),p(v))\), since \(\Pr[\hat x=y=v]\) is at most each marginal. And \(\sum_v\min(p,q)=1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\). Speculative sampling attains this bound (it accepts with probability exactly \(\sum\min(p,q)\)), so it is the maximal coupling. To exceed it one must change the proposal: better drafters (smaller TV), several candidates per position (trees), or proposals that depend on more context.

Deep-dive Estimate the latency benefit of an EAGLE drafter for Llama 3 70B on 8 H100s at batch 1, and what limits it.

Target step at TP = 8 about 6.1 ms (memory plus all-reduces). An EAGLE drafter (one Transformer layer plus the LM head) costs a few per cent of a target step in memory, but it runs on the same TP group, so each draft step pays kernel launches and at least one all-reduce: perhaps \(c_d\approx0.05\)–0.1. With \(a\approx0.8\) on chat and a dynamic tree, mean accepted length of 3–4 tokens is typical, so speed-up about 2.5–3×, TPOT about 2–2.5 ms. Limits: acceptance on the actual traffic and temperature; draft overheads dominated by launch and communication latency rather than FLOPs (CUDA graphs help); verification of a large tree with TP adds attention work; CPU overhead per step (Chapter 12) becomes a larger fraction as steps produce more tokens; and under load, batching competes for the same compute.

Deep-dive Why do acceptance rates vary across workloads, and how would you exploit that?

Acceptance \(a=1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\) is high when the next token is predictable and the drafter captures the target's distribution: code boilerplate, JSON, repeated phrases, copying from context, formulaic chat, greedy or low-temperature decoding. It is low for creative or high-entropy text, high temperatures (broad \(p\)), rare domains or languages absent from the drafter's training data, and after distribution shift (target updated, drafter stale). Exploit it by choosing \(\gamma\) and tree size per request or per sequence from recent acceptance; combining drafters (n-gram lookup for copying tasks, EAGLE otherwise); training drafters on the product's own traffic (EAGLE-3 scales with data); keeping drafters in sync with model versions; and monitoring mean accepted length per route to detect regressions.

Deep-dive How does multi-token prediction in DeepSeek-V3 differ from Medusa heads?

Medusa heads are added after training to a frozen model; each head predicts a future token at a fixed offset directly from the final hidden state of the current position, in parallel, without seeing intermediate draft tokens, so accuracy falls with offset. DeepSeek-V3's MTP modules are trained jointly with the model as an auxiliary objective: an MTP block takes the main model's representation at position \(t\) combined with the embedding of the true token \(t+1\), passes it through a Transformer block (sharing embedding and output head), and predicts \(t+2\); multiple modules can chain sequentially, so each prediction conditions on the previous token, like EAGLE. Training jointly densifies the training signal and produces a drafter well matched to the model; at inference it gives 85–90% acceptance for the second token, about 1.8× tokens per second.

Deep-dive When can speculative decoding make things worse, and how do you detect it?

When verification is compute-bound (large batches, short contexts), rejected drafts consume real compute, reducing throughput and possibly raising latency for everyone; when acceptance is low (high temperature, unusual content, stale drafter), draft cost is wasted; when draft overheads are large (separate model with its own TP group, CPU overheads, no CUDA graphs), \(c_d\) erodes gains; with very large trees at moderate batch; and when it reduces the batch the scheduler can admit because of extra KV positions. Detect with A/B load tests at realistic traffic, monitoring mean accepted length, step time breakdown (draft, verify), tokens/s per GPU and TPOT percentiles with speculation on and off, per workload and load level. Adaptive control should disable it automatically under heavy short-context load.

Design Your provider’s API is heavily loaded with short chat requests. Should you enable speculative decoding?

Probably not by default for the loaded steady state. Short contexts and large batches make verification compute-bound, so rejected drafts waste compute and throughput per GPU falls; the benefit appears mainly at low load or for latency-sensitive tiers. Options: enable speculation adaptively, only when the batch is below a threshold or for requests that pay for low latency; use small \(\gamma\) (1–2) with a high-acceptance integrated drafter (MTP/EAGLE), whose cost is small; route latency-sensitive traffic to lightly loaded replicas with speculation on; and quantify with load tests at the real arrival rate, looking at per-GPU goodput (TTFT and TPOT SLO attainment), not single-request latency.

Design How would you train and maintain an EAGLE-style drafter for a deployed model?

Data: prompts representative of production traffic (with privacy safeguards), with the target model's own generations (and features) at the production sampling settings, since the drafter must match the target's distribution, not human text. Train the one-layer drafter on target features and sampled tokens (EAGLE-3: multi-layer feature fusion, training-time test where the drafter consumes its own predictions), with the target frozen. Evaluate offline by acceptance rate and mean accepted length per domain and temperature, and online by TPOT and throughput. Maintain: retrain whenever the target model is updated (even small fine-tunes shift \(p\)), monitor acceptance per route for drift, and keep versioned drafter-target pairs in deployment so they never mismatch. Core Why does high sampling temperature reduce speculative speed-ups?

Acceptance is \(a=\sum_v\min(p(v),q(v))\). At low temperature the target's distribution is peaked, and a drafter that predicts the top token gets most of the mass; at greedy, acceptance is the probability that the drafter's argmax matches. At high temperature \(p\) spreads over many tokens, and even a good drafter's \(q\) overlaps it less (the minimum of two broad distributions sums to less), so \(a\) falls, and with it \(\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]\) and the optimal \(\gamma\). Applying the same temperature to \(q\) helps match shapes but cannot recover the predictability lost. Hence speed-ups reported at temperature 0 overstate what high-temperature creative workloads will see.

Core What is self-speculative decoding, and what are its trade-offs against EAGLE-style drafters?

Self-speculation drafts with a cheaper version of the target itself: skipping intermediate layers (Draft-and-Verify) or exiting early after the first few layers through the shared LM head (LayerSkip, which trains with layer dropout and an early-exit loss), then verifying with the full model. Advantages: no extra parameters to deploy, version or keep in sync, and the early layers' keys, values and activations computed while drafting are reused by verification, so part of the draft cost is recovered. Disadvantages: an early exit is a weaker predictor than a trained head that reads the target's final features, so acceptance is lower unless the model was trained for early exit, and implementations are model-specific. EAGLE-style drafters and MTP layers achieve higher acceptance at a small parameter cost; the trend is towards drafting capacity designed into the model during training.

Deep-dive How would you benchmark a speculative decoding configuration fairly?

Fix the target model, hardware, parallelism and sampling settings (including temperature, since acceptance depends on it), and use prompts from the real workload, stratified by task, because acceptance varies widely between copying-heavy and creative tasks. Report mean accepted length and acceptance rate alongside latency, and measure both single-request latency (TPOT at batch 1) and serving behaviour under an open-loop load generator at several arrival rates, comparing tokens/s per GPU and TTFT/TPOT percentiles with speculation on and off. Include draft overheads (separate model memory, its KV cache, any reduction in admitted batch), CPU and launch overheads, and long and short contexts, since long contexts keep verification memory-bound.
