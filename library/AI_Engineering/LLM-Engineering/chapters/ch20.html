Part VII · Faster Decoding and Longer Contexts — Chapter 21

Long-Context Inference

Contexts grew from 2,048 tokens in 2020 to 128k as a standard and a million at the frontier. At those lengths every assumption of short-context serving breaks: attention dominates prefill, the cache dominates decode and outgrows GPU memory, and the positional encoding meets positions it never saw in training. This chapter derives where the costs cross over, explains how RoPE is stretched to new lengths, how sparse attention cuts the cost of reading a long cache, and when retrieval is the better answer.

Why this chapter

Long context changed what language models are used for. A model that can read a whole codebase, a contract with its appendices, an hour of transcribed meeting or the full history of an agent's actions can answer questions no retrieval system would route correctly. Reasoning models (Chapter 26) generate tens of thousands of tokens of thought, so even short prompts produce long contexts. By 2025, 128k-token windows were the norm for open models such as Llama 3.1, and providers offered a million tokens or more.

For the inference engineer, long context is where the costs of earlier chapters change their shape. The attention term that we could ignore at 2k tokens grows quadratically and overtakes the linear layers. The KV cache that was a few hundred megabytes per sequence becomes tens of gigabytes, so the memory-limited batch collapses and the cache, not the weights, sets the time of each decode step. And the model itself must be made to work at lengths beyond those it was pretrained on, because pretraining on million-token sequences is prohibitively expensive. This chapter takes these problems in order: what long contexts cost and where the crossovers lie; how rotary position embeddings are extended (position interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, YaRN); how to tell whether a model really uses its long context; how sparse attention, from training-free page selection to DeepSeek's trained sparse attention, cuts the bytes each decode step must read; and how long context compares with retrieval.

21.1 What long context costs

Two crossovers organise everything. In prefill, a prompt of \(T\) tokens costs about \(2NT\) FLOPs in the linear layers and, with a causal mask, about \(2LdT^2\) in attention: each query attends on average to \(T/2\) earlier positions, with \(2d\) FLOPs for the score and \(2d\) for the weighted value per position and layer (§2.10). The attention share of prefill FLOPs is therefore

\[ \frac{LdT}{N+LdT},\qquad\text{equal to one half at}\quad T^{*}_{\text{FLOP}}=\frac{N}{Ld}. \]

For Llama 3 8B, \(T^{*}_{\text{FLOP}}=8.03\times10^{9}/(32\times4096)\approx61{,}000\) tokens; for Llama 3 70B, \(70.6\times10^{9}/(80\times8192)\approx108{,}000\). Below these lengths prefill is dominated by the dense matrix products and costs roughly linear time; above them it becomes quadratic. Notice that the ratio depends on the model's width-to-depth balance, not its size alone: wider, shallower models reach the crossover later.

In decode, each step reads the weights once and each sequence's cache once (§3.6). For one sequence at context \(T\), the cache bytes exceed the weight bytes when

\[ T\,s_{kv}\;>\;N\,b_w,\qquad T^{*}_{\text{byte}}=\frac{Nb_w}{s_{kv}}. \]

For Llama 3 8B in BF16, \(16.06\,\text{GB}/128\,\text{KiB}\approx122{,}500\) tokens; for Llama 3 70B, \(141\,\text{GB}/320\,\text{KiB}\approx431{,}000\). But in a batch, the weights are shared and the caches are not: with \(B\) sequences the cache bytes exceed the weights once \(BT>T^{*}_{\text{byte}}\). At 128k context, a single sequence of Llama 3 8B already reads more cache than weights, and the cache occupies 16 GB, so an 80 GB GPU can hold only three or four such sequences beside the weights. Long-context serving is cache-bound in both memory and time.

Figure 21.1 plots the attention share for Llama 3 8B. At the 2k–8k contexts of the earlier chapters it is a few per cent, which is why we could treat prefill as \(2NT\). At 128k it is two thirds, and at a million it is almost everything: a million-token prompt to an 8B model costs about \(2\times8\times10^{15}\) linear FLOPs plus \(2.7\times10^{17}\) attention FLOPs, around eight minutes on one H100 at 60% utilisation. This is why the context parallelism of §18.4 exists, why FlashAttention's efficiency matters most at long lengths (Chapter 9), and why long prefixes are such valuable targets for prefix caching (Chapter 10). In decode, the corresponding fact is that time per output token at 128k is dominated by reading 16 GB of cache per sequence, so the batch sizes that make short-context decode efficient are not available. Before addressing these costs, though, there is a more basic problem: a model pretrained on 8k-token sequences does not work at 128k at all.

21.2 Positions beyond training

Recall rotary position embedding from §2.5. The \(d_h\) coordinates of each query and key are grouped into \(d_h/2\) pairs, and pair \(r\) at position \(i\) is rotated by the angle \(i\theta_r\), with frequencies \(\theta_r=\Theta^{-2r/d_h}\) for \(r=0,\dots,d_h/2-1\). The dot product of a query at position \(i\) and a key at position \(j\) then depends on positions only through the angles \((i-j)\theta_r\). Each frequency has a wavelength \(\Lambda_r=2\pi/\theta_r\), the distance in tokens over which pair \(r\) completes one rotation. For Llama 3, with \(\Theta=500{,}000\) and \(d_h=128\), the wavelengths range from about 6 tokens for \(r=0\) to about 2.5 million tokens for \(r=63\), geometrically spaced.

Figure 21.2 places the wavelengths on a log scale against an 8k training length. The high-frequency pairs, with wavelengths of a few to a few hundred tokens, complete many full rotations within the training length, so every angle they can take has been seen in training at many relative distances; these pairs encode fine-grained, local order, and they are unaffected by longer contexts. The low-frequency pairs, with wavelengths longer than the training length, never complete a rotation in training. The angles \((i-j)\theta_r\) that the model saw for them lay in an interval \([0,T_{\text{train}}\theta_r)\) smaller than \(2\pi\); at relative distances beyond \(T_{\text{train}}\), these pairs produce angles outside that interval, inputs the attention heads have never been trained on. This is why naive extrapolation fails: perplexity explodes a little beyond the training length, not because the model cannot represent long-range relationships, but because its low-frequency position features go out of distribution.

The diagnosis suggests the cure: change the frequencies so that, at the new length, every pair's angles stay within the range seen in training, while disturbing the well-learned high-frequency pairs as little as possible. The methods of the next section differ precisely in how they distribute this change across frequencies.

21.3 Stretching RoPE: interpolation, NTK and YaRN

Let \(\mu=T_{\text{target}}/T_{\text{train}}\) be the extension factor, for example 16 to go from 8k to 128k. Position interpolation (PI; Chen, Wong, Chen and Tian, Meta, June 2023)[1] is the simplest remedy: divide every position by \(\mu\), equivalently divide every frequency, \(\theta_r'=\theta_r/\mu\). Positions up to \(T_{\text{target}}\) then map into \([0,T_{\text{train}})\), so no pair ever sees an angle outside its trained range. With about a thousand steps of fine-tuning on long sequences, PI extended LLaMA models from 2k to 32k tokens. Its weakness is that it compresses the high-frequency pairs too: two adjacent tokens are now separated by \(1/\mu\) of the angle they had in training, so the model's resolution of local order is degraded, and some short-context quality is lost.

NTK-aware scaling (proposed by a contributor known as bloc97 in June 2023, motivated by neural-tangent-kernel arguments about learning high-frequency functions) changes the base instead of the positions: \(\Theta'=\Theta\,\kappa\) with \(\kappa=\mu^{d_h/(d_h-2)}\). Then \(\theta_r'=\theta_r\,\kappa^{-2r/d_h}\): the highest frequency (\(r=0\)) is unchanged, the lowest (\(r=d_h/2-1\)) is divided by exactly \(\mu\), and the frequencies in between are scaled by a geometrically varying factor. High frequencies keep their local resolution; low frequencies are interpolated. It works without fine-tuning for moderate extensions and better with it, and a "dynamic" variant scales \(\Theta\) with the current sequence length, so short contexts are untouched. Raising \(\Theta\) itself became standard practice for long-context training: Llama 3's \(\Theta=500{,}000\), against Llama 2's 10,000, is an example.

YaRN (Peng, Quesnelle, Fan and Shippole, 2023)[2] makes the frequency-dependent treatment explicit. For each pair it counts the rotations completed over the training length, \(n_{\text{rot}}(r)=T_{\text{train}}/\Lambda_r\). Pairs with many rotations (\(n_{\text{rot}}>c_{\text{hi}}\), typically 32) are left alone; pairs with less than one rotation (\(n_{\text{rot}}<c_{\text{lo}}\), typically 1) are fully interpolated by \(\mu\); pairs in between are blended with a linear ramp,

\[ \theta_r'\;=\;\Big(\frac{1-g_r}{\mu}+g_r\Big)\theta_r,\qquad g_r=\operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\frac{n_{\text{rot}}(r)-c_{\text{lo}}}{c_{\text{hi}}-c_{\text{lo}}},\,0,\,1\Big). \]

YaRN adds a second ingredient. With more tokens to attend to, the softmax spreads its mass more thinly and its entropy rises, which blunts attention; YaRN compensates by sharpening the logits, multiplying queries and keys by \(0.1\ln\mu+1\) (so the logits by its square). With a few hundred steps of fine-tuning, YaRN extended Llama 2 to 128k tokens using about a tenth of the tokens and fewer steps than earlier methods, and it became the standard recipe; Qwen, DeepSeek and others ship YaRN configurations, and Llama 3.1's 128k context uses a closely related frequency-dependent scaling with low- and high-frequency thresholds.

Figure 21.3 compares the three schemes for an extension from 8k to 128k. PI is a flat line at 1/16: every pair, even the six-token-wavelength one, is slowed sixteen-fold. NTK starts at 1 for the highest frequency and declines smoothly to 1/16 at the lowest, so it interpolates mid-frequency pairs that YaRN would leave alone. YaRN's curve is flat at 1 for the first twenty pairs, whose wavelengths are under about 250 tokens and which complete more than 32 rotations in 8k tokens, falls steeply through the pairs whose wavelengths are comparable to the training length, and reaches 1/16 for every pair with a wavelength beyond 8k. The design principle is the diagnosis of §21.2: leave alone what was well learned, interpolate what was never seen, and blend the rest.

None of these methods removes the need for long training data. A model extended by YaRN can attend at 128k, but to use that range well it must be trained on documents in which long-range dependencies matter, which is why every serious long-context model includes a continued-pretraining or fine-tuning phase on long sequences, often with progressively increasing lengths. LongRoPE (2024) went further, searching per-dimension scaling factors to extend models to two million tokens. Positional extension makes long contexts possible; whether the model makes good use of them is a separate question, and it must be measured.

Drill 21.1 — Which pairs need interpolation?

For a model with \(\Theta=10{,}000\), \(d_h=128\) and \(T_{\text{train}}=4{,}096\) (Llama 2), find the pair index above which the wavelength exceeds the training length, and the index below which pairs complete more than 32 rotations. What fraction of pairs does YaRN leave untouched?

Solution. \(\Lambda_r=2\pi\,\Theta^{2r/d_h}=2\pi\times10^{4r/64}\). \(\Lambda_r>4096\) when \(10^{r/16}>652\), i.e. \(r>16\log_{10}652\approx45\). More than 32 rotations means \(\Lambda_r<128\), i.e. \(10^{r/16}<20.4\), \(r<21\). So pairs 0–20 (about a third) are kept exactly, pairs 46–63 (about 28%) are fully interpolated, and the 25 in between are ramped. Llama 3, with base 500,000 and an 8k training length, has 28 never-completed pairs (\(r\ge36\)), because a larger base stretches all wavelengths.

Worked example — which RoPE frequencies need rescaling

Setup. Llama 3 was pretrained at \(T_{\text{train}}=8192\) with \(\Theta=500{,}000\) and \(d_h=128\), so it has 64 frequency pairs with \(\theta_r=\Theta^{-2r/d_h}\) and wavelengths \(\Lambda_r=2\pi\Theta^{2r/d_h}\). Llama 3.1 extends the context to 128k with a scale factor of 8, keeping high-frequency pairs (\(\Lambda_r<T_{\text{train}}/4=2048\)), dividing the frequency of low-frequency pairs (\(\Lambda_r>T_{\text{train}}\)) by 8, and blending in between.

Low-frequency pairs. \(\Lambda_r>8192\) means \(\Theta^{2r/128}>8192/2\pi\approx1304\), i.e. \(2r/128>\ln1304/\ln500{,}000=7.17/13.12=0.547\), so \(r\ge35\). Pairs 35–63, 29 of them, never completed a full rotation during pretraining. Their angles at positions beyond 8k would be new to the model, so they are interpolated.

High-frequency pairs. \(\Lambda_r<2048\) means \(\Theta^{2r/128}<326\), i.e. \(2r/128<0.441\), so \(r\le28\). Pairs 0–28, also 29, rotated at least four times within the training length and saw every angle many times; they encode local order and are left alone.

Consequence. Only the six pairs \(r=29,\dots,34\) are blended. The model keeps its fine positional resolution exactly where it matters for nearby tokens and stretches only the slow dimensions, which is why a short continued-training phase suffices, as opposed to the full positional interpolation of §21.3, which compresses every pair and blurs local order.

21.4 Does the model use its context?

Advertised context lengths say how many tokens a model accepts, not how well it uses them. The first popular test, the "needle in a haystack" (Kamradt, 2023), hides a sentence such as a passphrase at a chosen depth in a long irrelevant document and asks for it back, sweeping length and depth. Most long-context models now pass it almost perfectly, which shows that attention can retrieve a distinctive fact from anywhere in the window. It is also a weak test, because finding one unusual sentence by lexical match is much easier than reasoning over many dispersed pieces of information.

Harder evaluations expose real limits. Liu and colleagues (2023)[3] found that in multi-document question answering, accuracy followed a U-shape over the position of the relevant document: high when it was near the beginning or end of the context, markedly lower in the middle, an effect they called lost in the middle. RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024)[4] combined multi-needle retrieval, variable tracking across the context, aggregation of frequent words and question answering with distractors, and defined a model's effective context length as the longest at which it stays above a quality threshold; for many models of 2024 the effective length was a fraction of the advertised one, sometimes a quarter. Benchmarks of long-document reasoning, such as multi-hop questions over books and code repositories, show similar gaps.

For deployment, two lessons follow. First, a long-context capability must be evaluated on the product's own task at the lengths it will see, not inferred from a needle test or a context-length specification. Second, how information is placed matters: putting the question after the documents, and the most relevant material near the start or end, measurably improves accuracy, and prompt structure interacts with prefix caching (§10.6), since stable documents placed first can be cached across requests. Quality limits and cost limits often point the same way, towards reading less of the context more carefully, which is the idea behind sparse attention.

21.5 Sparse attention

In a long context, most keys receive almost no attention from any given query: the softmax concentrates on a small number of relevant positions plus a few structural ones (the attention sinks of §8.7). If we knew in advance which keys matter, we could read only those, and decode at 128k would cost little more than at a few thousand. Sparse attention methods differ in how they decide which keys to read, and whether the model is trained to work with the selection.

The static patterns of Chapter 8, sliding windows and local/global hybrids, fix the selection by position and must be trained into the model; they bound the cache but cannot retrieve a distant fact that a window has dropped. Training-free, query-aware methods choose keys dynamically at decode time without changing the model. Quest (Tang et al., 2024)[5] keeps, for each page of the paged cache (Chapter 10), the elementwise minimum and maximum of its keys; for a query \(q\), the sum over coordinates of \(\max(q_cm_c,q_cM_c)\) is an upper bound on \(q\cdot k\) for every key in the page, so the pages with the largest bounds are the only ones that can contain high-scoring keys. Quest reads those pages fully and skips the rest, cutting the cache bytes read per step by an order of magnitude at little accuracy cost on many tasks. The limitation of training-free methods is that the model was never trained to attend sparsely, so when the selection misses a relevant key, nothing compensates.

Figure 21.4 illustrates the bound on eight pages.

Trained sparse attention builds selection into the model. DeepSeek's Native Sparse Attention (NSA; Yuan et al., 2025)[6] computes each query's attention through three branches: a compressed branch attending to block-level summaries of the whole context, a selected branch attending at full resolution to the top-scoring blocks identified from the compressed scores, and a sliding-window branch for local context, combined by learned gates. The block structure is chosen to match GPU memory access, so the sparsity translates into real speed, and because the model is trained this way from the start (or continued-pretrained), it learns to put the information it needs where the selection will find it. Moonshot's MoBA (2025) applies a mixture-of-experts-style gate over blocks of keys.

Figure 21.5 sketches the common structure. DeepSeek took it into production with DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) in DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp (September 2025)[7]. A small "lightning indexer", with its own low-dimensional FP8 keys, scores every previous token for each query, and the main MLA attention then attends only to the top \(k_s=2048\) tokens. The indexer still touches every token, so its cost grows linearly with context, but each touch is far cheaper than full attention, and the expensive part becomes constant in context length. DeepSeek reported that long-context costs fell enough to cut API prices substantially, with quality on par with the dense-attention predecessor.

Worked example — the bytes of DSA at 128k

Per decode token at context \(T=131{,}072\), DeepSeek-V3's dense MLA attention reads the whole latent cache, \(T\times68.6\) KiB ≈ 9.2 GB (across all 61 layers). With DSA, the indexer reads one small FP8 key per token per layer, 128 bytes, so \(T\times61\times128\) B ≈ 1.0 GB, and the main attention reads only \(2048\times68.6\) KiB ≈ 0.14 GB. Cache bytes per token fall from about 9.2 GB to about 1.2 GB, a factor of about 8, and the factor grows with context: at a million tokens, from about 72 GB to about 8 GB. The indexer, now the dominant term, is linear in \(T\) with a small constant, so the remaining cost is \(O(T)\) with a coefficient about nine times smaller than dense MLA's.

The example shows the economics of trained sparse attention: it does not make long contexts free, but it changes the constant in front of the dominant term by close to an order of magnitude, in both bytes (decode) and FLOPs (prefill, where selected attention replaces quadratic dense attention for each query). That is enough to change prices. And it composes with everything else in this chapter: positional extension makes the range usable, sparse attention makes it affordable, and memory management makes it fit.

21.6 Fitting the cache: offload, compression and distribution

A 128k context of Llama 3 70B occupies \(131{,}072\times320\) KiB ≈ 43 GB in BF16, half an H100; a million tokens occupy about 344 GB. There are three ways to make such caches fit, each with a characteristic cost. The first is compression: an FP8 or 4-bit cache (§8.6) halves or quarters the bytes, and architectural choices such as GQA and MLA (Chapter 8) reduce them far more, which is why long-context models are almost always GQA or MLA models. Eviction and selection methods (§8.7) discard or skip tokens the model is unlikely to need, approximately.

The second is offload: keep most of the cache in CPU memory, or in a tiered store (Chapter 13), and bring parts of it to the GPU when needed. The arithmetic is unforgiving for dense attention. Each decode step must read the whole cache; over PCIe Gen5 at about 64 GB/s, a 43 GB cache would take about 670 ms per token, a hundred times slower than reading it from HBM. Offload therefore pairs naturally with sparse attention: if only the selected pages are needed each step, only those must cross PCIe, and prefetching the pages the next step will probably need hides much of the latency. Offload is also useful in coarser forms, for example to hold the caches of paused conversations or agent sessions between turns, where the transfer cost is paid once per turn rather than per token.

The third is distribution: spread one sequence's cache across several GPUs, by tensor parallelism over KV heads (§18.2) or by context parallelism (§18.4), so that each GPU holds a slice. For prefill, context parallelism also divides the quadratic attention work. For decode, each step's attention becomes a split-KV computation across devices with a small reduction of partial softmax results per layer. The memory and the bandwidth of several GPUs are then applied to one long sequence, at the cost of a latency-bound collective in each layer. In practice, long-context services combine all three: GQA or MLA caches in FP8, distributed over a tensor-parallel group, with prefix caching for documents that recur and tiered storage for idle sessions.

Drill 21.2 — How many 128k sequences fit?

Llama 3 70B on eight H100s with TP = 8. How many concurrent sequences at 128k context fit with a BF16 cache, with an FP8 cache, and what is the decode step time at that batch (BF16 weights, FP8 cache, 5 µs all-reduces)?

Solution. Each GPU holds 17.6 GB of weights; with 90% memory utilisation and about 3 GB for activations, about 51 GB per GPU, 408 GB in total, is available for cache. A 128k sequence needs about 43 GB in BF16, so 9 sequences fit, or 19 with FP8 (21.5 GB each). At batch 19 with FP8, each GPU reads 17.6 GB of weights plus \(19\times21.5/8\approx51\) GB of cache per step, about 20.5 ms at 3.35 TB/s, plus 160 all-reduces of \(19\times8192\times2\) bytes, about 0.8 ms: TPOT ≈ 21 ms for 19 sequences, about 110 tokens/s per GPU, compared with about 1,500 at 2k context (Figure 18.3). Long context reduces throughput per GPU by an order of magnitude, which is the economic case for sparse attention and caching.

21.7 Long context or retrieval?

Before long context was cheap, the standard way to give a model access to a large corpus was retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): split documents into chunks, embed them, retrieve the few chunks most similar to the query, and put only those into a short prompt. Long context offers an alternative: put the whole document, or many documents, into the prompt and let attention find what matters. The choice is a trade-off between cost and recall, and it is worth making quantitatively.

The cost of long context is the prefill of the whole context, plus the decode steps reading its cache. For Llama 3 8B, a 128k prompt costs about \(2\times8\times10^{9}\times131{,}072\approx2.1\times10^{15}\) linear FLOPs plus \(2LdT^2\approx4.5\times10^{15}\) attention FLOPs, \(6.6\times10^{15}\) in all, about 11 seconds of one H100 at 60% utilisation; a RAG prompt of 4k tokens costs about \(6.8\times10^{13}\), a hundredth as much, plus the retrieval itself, which is cheap. Long context therefore costs one to two orders of magnitude more per query, unless the long prefix is shared across queries: with prefix caching, a document asked many questions is prefilled once, and each further question pays only for its own tokens and for decode steps that read the cache. Quality points the other way. Retrieval can miss: the relevant chunk may not be similar to the query, the answer may require combining many chunks, or chunking may cut context the answer depends on. Long context sees everything, subject to the effective-length limits of §21.4.

Figure 21.6 contrasts the two paths and the hybrid that connects them. Studies comparing the two found that long-context models generally answered better when they could afford to read everything, while RAG was far cheaper and nearly as good on many queries. Li and colleagues (2024)[8] proposed a hybrid, Self-Route, in which the model first answers from retrieved chunks and reports whether they suffice, and only the queries it judges unanswerable are routed to full long context; it retained most of long context's accuracy at a fraction of its cost. The general lesson is that retrieval and long context are complements: retrieval narrows what must be read, long context reads it well, and caching makes repeated reading cheap. Production systems increasingly combine all three.

Drill 21.3 — When does prefix caching make long context competitive?

A team asks \(Q\) questions about the same 100k-token document with Llama 3 8B. Compare the prefill FLOPs of long context without caching, long context with prefix caching, and RAG with 4k-token prompts. For what \(Q\) is cached long context within 2× of RAG on prefill cost?

Solution. One 100k prefill: \(2NT+2LdT^2\approx1.6\times10^{15}+2.6\times10^{15}=4.2\times10^{15}\) FLOPs. Without caching: \(4.2Q\times10^{15}\). With caching: \(4.2\times10^{15}\) once, plus a few hundred tokens per question (each attending to the cached 100k: about \(4Ld\times100\text{k}+2N\approx7\times10^{10}\) FLOPs per token, so about \(2\times10^{13}\) for a 300-token question). RAG: about \(6.8\times10^{13}\) per question. Cached long context costs \(4.2\times10^{15}+2Q\times10^{13}\); RAG costs \(6.8Q\times10^{13}\). Within 2×: \(4.2\times10^{15}+2\times10^{13}Q\le1.36\times10^{14}Q\), i.e. \(Q\ge36\). After a few dozen questions on the same document, long context with caching costs about the same as RAG in prefill, though each decode step still reads a 100k cache, about 13 GB, which RAG avoids.

21.8 Contexts that grow: agents and reasoning

The long contexts of this chapter have so far been long prompts: a document, a codebase, a transcript. A second kind of long context grows during a session. An agent that calls tools appends each tool result and each of its own actions to its context, turn after turn, so that after an hour of work its context may hold hundreds of thousands of tokens of history. A reasoning model (Chapter 26) appends thousands of tokens of thought before each answer. These workloads change the shape of the cost: the context is not prefilled once but extended many times, and the same prefix is re-read on every turn.

Two engineering consequences follow. The first is that session-level caching becomes essential. Between turns, the agent's cache should not be discarded and recomputed; it should be kept, on the GPU if the next turn is imminent, or offloaded to CPU memory or a tiered store if the agent is waiting for a slow tool, and the next turn should be routed to wherever the cache lives (§13.8). With caching, each turn pays only for its new tokens and for decode steps that read the accumulated cache; without it, each turn re-prefills the whole history, and the cost of a session grows quadratically with its number of turns. The second consequence is that decode steps late in a session read very large caches, so the batch sizes that make decode efficient are not available, exactly the regime of §21.1 in which speculative decoding (Chapter 20) and sparse attention pay most.

Agents also manage their contexts actively, which is a design choice with inference consequences. Compaction replaces old history with a summary written by the model, keeping the context within a budget at the cost of an extra generation and of losing detail; it also invalidates the cache from the point of the change, since everything after an edited prefix must be recomputed. Context editing removes stale tool results for the same reason, with the same cost. A well-designed agent therefore edits its context rarely and at the end where possible, keeps stable material (system prompt, tool definitions, task description) at the beginning so that it remains cached, and appends rather than rewrites. These are the prefix-caching rules of §10.6 applied to a context that changes over hours rather than seconds, and they matter as much for cost as any kernel optimisation.

Looking ahead

Every technique in this chapter works around a property of softmax attention: each new token must be compared with every earlier one, so the state carried forward, the KV cache, grows linearly with context, and the work grows with it. Sparse attention reduces the constant; it does not change the growth. A different line of research asks whether a model can carry a fixed-size state instead, updated at each token like a recurrent network, so that decode costs the same at a million tokens as at one. Chapter 22 develops state-space models, linear attention and their hybrids with attention, explains why they returned in 2023–25 after recurrent networks lost to Transformers, and analyses what they cost and what they give up.

Chapter summary

  • Crossovers: attention exceeds half of prefill FLOPs beyond \(T^{*}_{\text{FLOP}}=N/(Ld)\) (≈ 61k for Llama 3 8B, 108k for 70B); one sequence's cache bytes exceed weight bytes beyond \(T^{*}_{\text{byte}}=Nb_w/s_{kv}\) (≈ 122k for 8B). Long-context serving is cache-bound in memory and time.
  • RoPE pair \(r\) has wavelength \(\Lambda_r=2\pi\Theta^{2r/d_h}\). High-frequency pairs complete many rotations in training and generalise; low-frequency pairs with \(\Lambda_r>T_{\text{train}}\) see unseen angles beyond the training length, so naive extrapolation fails.
  • Position interpolation divides all frequencies by \(\mu\) (hurts local resolution); NTK-aware scaling multiplies the base by \(\mu^{d_h/(d_h-2)}\) (high frequencies kept, low interpolated); YaRN keeps pairs with \(>c_{\text{hi}}\) rotations, interpolates those with \(<c_{\text{lo}}\), ramps between, and sharpens logits by \((0.1\ln\mu+1)^2\). Long-context training data remains necessary.

Warm-up What are the main costs of long-context inference?

Prefill: linear layers cost \(2NT\) FLOPs, causal attention about \(2LdT^2\); attention dominates beyond \(T^*=N/(Ld)\) (≈ 61k for Llama 3 8B), so long prompts become quadratic (a 1M-token prompt to 8B is minutes on one GPU). Decode: each step reads each sequence's cache; one sequence's cache exceeds the weights beyond \(Nb_w/s_{kv}\) (≈ 122k for 8B BF16), and a 128k cache is 16 GB for 8B or 43 GB for 70B, so few sequences fit and per-GPU throughput falls by an order of magnitude. Model: RoPE must be extended beyond the training length and the model trained to use long context. Remedies: GQA/MLA and FP8 caches, context and tensor parallelism, prefix caching, sparse attention, and retrieval.

Warm-up Explain position interpolation, NTK-aware scaling and YaRN.

All extend RoPE to longer contexts by changing frequencies so that, at the new length, rotation angles stay within the range seen in training. Position interpolation divides all positions (frequencies) by the extension factor \(\mu\): safe, but it also compresses high-frequency pairs and hurts local resolution. NTK-aware scaling multiplies the base by \(\mu^{d_h/(d_h-2)}\): the highest frequency is unchanged, the lowest divided by \(\mu\), geometric in between. YaRN treats pairs by how many rotations they complete over the training length: keep those with many (\(>32\)), fully interpolate those with less than one, linearly ramp between, and sharpen attention logits by \((0.1\ln\mu+1)^2\) to counter entropy growth. All work best with some fine-tuning on long sequences; YaRN needs the least.

Core Derive the context length at which attention dominates prefill, and evaluate it for two models.

Linear layers: \(2N\) FLOPs per token. Causal attention: each query attends on average to \(T/2\) keys, with about \(2d\) FLOPs for scores and \(2d\) for the weighted sum per key per layer, so \(4Ld\times T/2=2LdT\) per token and \(2LdT^2\) for the prompt. Attention share \(=2LdT/(2N+2LdT)=LdT/(N+LdT)\), one half at \(T^*=N/(Ld)\). Llama 3 8B: \(8.03\times10^9/(32\times4096)\approx61\)k; Llama 3 70B: \(70.6\times10^9/(80\times8192)\approx108\)k. The crossover depends on the width-to-depth balance: \(N\approx12Ld^2\) for a standard block, so \(T^*\approx12d\) for dense models with standard FFN ratios, larger for wider models. (Embedding parameters and GQA shift it slightly.)

Core Why does RoPE break beyond the training length, in terms of frequencies?

RoPE rotates coordinate pair \(r\) by angle \(i\theta_r\), \(\theta_r=\Theta^{-2r/d_h}\), so scores depend on \((i-j)\theta_r\). Pair \(r\) has wavelength \(\Lambda_r=2\pi/\theta_r\). High-frequency pairs (short \(\Lambda_r\)) complete many rotations within the training length, so all their angles were seen at many distances, and they generalise to longer contexts. Low-frequency pairs with \(\Lambda_r>T_{\text{train}}\) never completed a rotation: their training angles covered only \([0,T_{\text{train}}\theta_r)\), a fraction of the circle. Beyond \(T_{\text{train}}\) they take angles never seen, so attention patterns that depend on them become unpredictable and perplexity explodes. Hence extension methods interpolate low frequencies into their seen range and preserve high frequencies.

Core How do Quest and DSA reduce the bytes read per decode step?

Quest (training-free): for each page of the paged KV cache, store elementwise min and max of its keys. For the current query, \(\sum_c\max(q_cm_c,q_cM_c)\) upper-bounds \(q\cdot k\) for every key in the page; read only the top-scoring pages fully and skip the rest, reading a small fraction of the cache with little loss on many tasks. DSA (trained, DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp): a lightweight indexer with small FP8 keys scores every previous token for each query, and the main MLA attention attends only to the top 2,048 tokens. At 128k, cache bytes per token fall from about 9.2 GB to about 1.2 GB (indexer ≈ 1.0 GB plus selected latents ≈ 0.14 GB); the remaining cost is linear with a small coefficient. Training with the selection lets the model compensate for what is not selected.

Core Explain lost-in-the-middle and effective context length, and how they affect product design.

Liu et al. (2023) found that in multi-document QA, accuracy is highest when the relevant document is near the start or end of the context and drops in the middle, a U-shape. RULER (2024) tests multi-needle retrieval, variable tracking, aggregation and QA with distractors, defining effective length as the longest context at which performance stays above a threshold; many models' effective lengths were far below advertised. Design implications: evaluate on the product's tasks at its real lengths; place the question after documents and the most relevant material near the ends; prefer retrieval or reranking to narrow context when possible; cache stable documents as prefixes; and do not assume a 1M-token window means 1M tokens of reliable reasoning.

Core Compare RAG and long context on cost and quality.

Cost: long context prefills the whole document (for 8B at 128k, about \(6.6\times10^{15}\) FLOPs, ~11 s of an H100) and every decode step reads a large cache; RAG prefills a few thousand tokens (about 1% of that) plus cheap retrieval. Quality: RAG can miss information that is relevant but dissimilar to the query, spread over many chunks, or split by chunking; long context sees everything, within effective-length limits. Caching changes the calculus: a document asked many questions is prefilled once, making long context competitive after a few dozen questions (Drill 21.3). Hybrids such as Self-Route answer from retrieval first and escalate to long context when the model reports missing information, keeping most of long context's accuracy at much lower cost.

Deep-dive Derive NTK-aware scaling’s base factor.

We want to multiply the base \(\Theta\) by \(\kappa\) so that the lowest-frequency pair (\(r=d_h/2-1\)) is slowed by exactly \(\mu\), as in position interpolation, while the highest (\(r=0\)) is unchanged. New frequency: \(\theta_r'=(\kappa\Theta)^{-2r/d_h}=\theta_r\kappa^{-2r/d_h}\). At \(r=0\), \(\theta_0'=\theta_0\) automatically. At \(r=d_h/2-1\), \(\kappa^{-(d_h-2)/d_h}=1/\mu\) requires \(\kappa=\mu^{d_h/(d_h-2)}\). Between, the scaling factor \(\kappa^{-2r/d_h}=\mu^{-2r/(d_h-2)}\) varies geometrically with \(r\). The name comes from the argument that networks learn high-frequency components poorly (spectral bias, studied via the neural tangent kernel), so high frequencies should not be compressed. Dynamic NTK sets \(\mu\) from the current sequence length, leaving short contexts untouched.

Deep-dive Design the attention and memory strategy for serving 1M-token contexts with a 70B-class GQA model.

Memory: a 1M-token BF16 cache is about 344 GB; FP8 halves it; distribute across a TP group (KV heads) and, for prefill, context parallelism across servers. Prefill: 1M tokens cost about \(1.4\times10^{17}\) linear plus \(1.3\times10^{18}\) attention FLOPs, so use ring attention across many GPUs with load-balanced chunks and FlashAttention kernels, and prefix-cache stable documents in a tiered KV store so repeated queries skip prefill. Decode: dense attention would read hundreds of GB per token; use sparse attention (trained, like NSA or DSA, if the model supports it; otherwise query-aware page selection such as Quest) so only selected pages are read, enabling CPU offload of cold pages with prefetch. Positional extension via YaRN-style scaling with long-context fine-tuning. Evaluate with RULER-style tests at the target length. Consider retrieval to narrow context for most queries and reserve full 1M context for those that need it.

Deep-dive Why do trained sparse attention methods outperform training-free ones at the same sparsity?

Training-free methods (Quest, eviction, top-k on dense models) choose keys using scores or bounds from a model trained with dense attention; when the selection misses keys that the dense model would have used, the output degrades and nothing compensates. Trained methods (NSA, MoBA, DSA) include the selection in training (or continued pretraining), so gradients shape representations to make relevant information findable by the selector, and the model learns to rely on what will be selected (for example, through compressed summaries and local windows). They also design selection to align with hardware (block granularity matching memory access), so sparsity yields real speed. The cost is training compute and model-specific kernels.

Deep-dive How does long context interact with speculative decoding, disaggregation and prefix caching?

Speculative decoding: long contexts make decode steps cache-dominated; verification reads each cache once regardless of draft length, so speculation stays nearly free even at large batch (MagicDec), making it especially valuable for long-context serving. Disaggregation: long prompts make prefill dominant and compute-bound, raising the prefill-to-decode pool ratio (Figure 13.5); KV transfer is large but GQA/MLA keep \(\chi\) small; context parallelism fits the prefill pool. Prefix caching: long, stable prefixes (documents, codebases, agent histories) are the highest-value cache entries, since each hit saves quadratic prefill; tiered KV stores (Mooncake, Dynamo KVBM) keep them across requests, and KV-aware routing sends queries to where their prefix lives.

Deep-dive What limits KV-cache offload to CPU for long contexts, and when is it useful?

Per-token dense attention must read the whole cache each step; over PCIe Gen5 (~64 GB/s) moving a 43 GB cache takes ~670 ms, far slower than HBM (3.35 TB/s). So offload is useful only when (a) attention is sparse, so only selected pages must cross PCIe each step, ideally prefetched based on predicted selection; (b) caches are idle between turns (paused conversations, agent sessions), where moving them once per turn is cheap relative to recomputation; (c) as a tier in a prefix cache, where loading beats recomputing long prefixes (§10.8); or (d) for local inference where memory is the binding constraint and speed is secondary. Grace-Hopper-style systems with fast CPU–GPU links (NVLink-C2C) shift the trade-off.

Design A legal-tech product needs answers grounded in 300-page contracts (~150k tokens). Design the serving approach.

Each contract is queried many times by the same team, so prefill once and cache: store the contract's KV as a prefix in a tiered cache (HBM, CPU, SSD) with KV-aware routing. Use a long-context model with GQA/MLA, YaRN-style extension and verified effective length at 150k on legal tasks; use FP8 cache. For each question, place the contract first (cached), the question last. For cost, add retrieval: answer from top chunks first and escalate to full-context reading when needed (Self-Route), or use retrieval to reorder sections so relevant clauses are near the end. Decode with speculative decoding (long-context friendly). Evaluate on lawyer-annotated questions (multi-clause reasoning, cross-references), not needle tests; monitor citations for grounding. Capacity: few concurrent 150k sequences fit per GPU group, so plan TP groups and consider sparse attention models if available.

Design How would you evaluate whether a model’s 128k context is fit for a product?

Build tests at the product's actual lengths (and beyond), with information at varied depths: single and multiple needles; multi-hop questions combining facts from distant parts; aggregation (counting, summarising across the document); tracking entities or variables through the text; distractors similar to the answer; and the product's real queries on real documents with expert-graded answers. Measure accuracy versus length and depth (to detect lost-in-the-middle), compare with a RAG baseline and with shorter contexts, and with the model's behaviour at its native training length. Include latency and cost per query at those lengths, and test prompt structures (question placement). Define the effective length as the longest at which quality meets the product threshold, and enforce it in routing.

Core Why are long-context models almost always GQA or MLA models?

The cache grows linearly with context: \(s_{kv}=2Ln_{kv}d_hb_{kv}\) per token. With multi-head attention (\(n_{kv}=n_h\)), a 70B-class model would need about 2.5 MiB per token, 320 GB at 128k for one sequence, which neither fits nor can be read per step at reasonable speed. GQA reduces \(n_{kv}\) (Llama 3: 8 KV heads, 320 KiB per token), and MLA compresses to a latent (DeepSeek-V3: about 69 KiB). These reduce memory, decode bytes, KV transfer for disaggregation and prefix-cache storage by large factors, which is what makes long contexts serviceable at all. Sparse attention then reduces the bytes read per step further.

Core What is the effect of a longer RoPE base \(\Theta\) on long-context behaviour?

Wavelengths \(\Lambda_r=2\pi\Theta^{2r/d_h}\) scale with \(\Theta\) raised to a power, so a larger base stretches all but the highest-frequency pairs, slowing rotations: more pairs have wavelengths beyond any given length, and relative positions over long distances map to smaller angles. Training with a large base (Llama 3 uses 500,000) makes long-distance angles less extreme and eases later extension (NTK-aware scaling is equivalent to raising the base at inference). Too large a base reduces the angular resolution of mid-range distances. Long-context training recipes combine a larger base with progressively longer training sequences and YaRN-style scaling for the final extension.

Design An agent product's sessions grow to 200k tokens over dozens of tool calls. How do you keep inference cost under control?

Keep each session's KV cache between turns instead of re-prefilling the history: hold it in HBM while the next turn is imminent, offload it to CPU memory or a tiered KV store while the agent waits on slow tools, and route each turn to the worker holding its prefix (KV-aware routing). Each turn then pays only for new tokens plus decode steps over the accumulated cache, instead of a cost that grows quadratically with the number of turns. Structure the context for caching: stable system prompt and tool definitions first, task description next, appended history last; avoid editing early parts, since any edit invalidates everything after it. Compact (summarise) rarely and in large steps, accepting a one-time recompute. Use FP8 caches and a GQA/MLA model, speculative decoding for the long-context decode steps, and sparse attention if available. Monitor tokens prefilled per turn and cache hit rates per session.
