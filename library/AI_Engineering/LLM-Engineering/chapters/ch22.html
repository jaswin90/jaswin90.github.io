Part VIII · Post-Training and Reasoning — Chapter 26

Reasoning Models and Test-Time Compute

Since 2024 the most capable models think before they answer, generating thousands of tokens of reasoning that the user may never see. Quality now scales with inference compute as well as training compute, and inference has become the larger bill. This chapter traces the path from chain-of-thought prompting to o1 and DeepSeek-R1, quantifies the two ways of spending test-time compute, sampling more and thinking longer, and works out what reasoning does to the cost, latency and architecture of serving.

Why this chapter

Throughout this book, a request has had a few thousand tokens of prompt and a few hundred tokens of output, and the art of serving it has been to process the prompt quickly and stream the output steadily. Reasoning models change the proportions. Asked a hard mathematics or coding question, OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) or DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) may generate ten or twenty thousand tokens of "thinking", exploring approaches, checking intermediate results, backtracking, before writing a short answer. The output is now the dominant part of the request, and it is generated one token at a time in the memory-bound decode regime that Chapters 3 and 6 analysed.

The reason providers accept this cost is that it works. OpenAI reported that o1's accuracy on competition mathematics rose smoothly with the logarithm of the compute spent thinking at test time, and DeepSeek showed that reinforcement learning on verifiable problems taught a model to think longer, by itself, as it got better. For the first time since the scaling laws of Chapter 1, there was a second axis along which quality scales: not only how much compute goes into training a model, but how much goes into each answer. That has direct consequences for inference engineers. Test-time compute is paid on every request, forever; the economics of a product now depend on how many tokens its model thinks; and the serving system must handle very long, unpredictable outputs, parallel samples and, during training itself, enormous volumes of rollout generation.

The three chapters before this one built the training side of the story: reinforcement learning and PPO (Chapter 23), learning from preferences (Chapter 24), and learning from verifiable rewards with GRPO (Chapter 25), the method that produced o1-style reasoning in the open. This chapter takes the models those methods produce and studies them from the side of inference, where their long outputs are paid for on every request.

The chapter has two halves. The first is about the quantity being scaled: how chain of thought became trained reasoning, what "test-time compute" means, and the mathematics of its two forms, parallel sampling with selection and sequential thinking. The second is about the system: what thinking costs in tokens, latency and memory, how serving must adapt, and why the inference engine has become a central component of training itself.

26.1 From chain of thought to reasoning models

In 2022, Wei and colleagues showed that prompting a large model with worked examples that spelled out intermediate steps, a chain of thought, sharply improved its accuracy on arithmetic and logic problems[1]; Kojima and colleagues found that simply appending "Let's think step by step" did much of the same. The explanation is computational as much as linguistic. A Transformer performs a fixed amount of computation per generated token, a forward pass of fixed depth; a problem that needs more serial steps than one pass provides cannot be solved in one token, but can be solved if the model writes intermediate results and conditions on them. Generated tokens are the model's scratch memory and its way to buy more sequential computation.

A second 2022 idea, self-consistency (Wang et al.)[2], sampled many chains of thought for the same question and took the majority answer, improving accuracy further by spending compute in parallel rather than in sequence. In 2023, Lightman and colleagues at OpenAI trained process reward models that scored each step of a solution and used them to select among samples, finding step-level supervision more effective than scoring only the final answer[3]. All of this worked with models that had not been trained to reason; it elicited latent capability through prompting and selection.

The step change came from training. OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) was trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to produce long chains of thought, and its announcement showed two plots that defined the new era: accuracy on the AIME mathematics competition rising with the logarithm of train-time reinforcement-learning compute, and rising again with the logarithm of test-time thinking compute. In January 2025, DeepSeek-R1 provided an open counterpart with a detailed recipe[4]. Its precursor, R1-Zero, was trained from the base model by reinforcement learning alone, with rewards only for correct final answers and correct format on verifiable problems, and no human demonstrations of reasoning. As training proceeded, the model's responses grew longer on their own, from hundreds to thousands of tokens, and behaviours such as re-checking work and trying alternative approaches emerged; its AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 71.0%, and the full R1 pipeline reached 79.8%. Within months, most major model families had reasoning variants, often with a user- or system-controlled "reasoning effort".

26.2 Test-time compute as a scaling axis

"Test-time compute" means the computation spent answering one query, beyond the minimum needed to emit the answer. There are three basic ways to spend it, and they have different costs and different system implications.

Figure 26.1 lays out the three. Sequential scaling generates a longer chain of reasoning; its cost and its latency both grow with the number of tokens. Parallel scaling draws \(n_s\) independent samples and selects an answer by majority vote, by a reward model or by a verifier such as unit tests; its cost grows with \(n_s\), but its latency is that of one sample, and the samples share the prompt's KV cache. Search expands partial solutions step by step, keeping the most promising according to a process reward model, a beam search over reasoning steps rather than tokens; it can be the most compute-efficient when a good step-level verifier exists, but it is the hardest to batch efficiently. Trained reasoning models such as o1 and R1 internalise much of the search into a single long chain, generating alternatives and checks as text, which is why sequential thinking dominates their compute; parallel sampling composes with it.

How should a fixed budget be divided? Snell and colleagues (2024)[5] studied "compute-optimal" test-time scaling and found that the best strategy depends on problem difficulty: for easy and medium problems, revising answers sequentially was most efficient, while for hard problems parallel sampling with a verifier helped more, and with the right allocation a smaller model with more test-time compute could match a model fourteen times larger on problems where the small model had a reasonable base success rate. The general lesson is that test-time compute and model size are partly substitutable, with a rate of exchange that depends on the task, and that the choice is an engineering decision about cost and latency. To make it, we need the mathematics of each form.

26.3 Parallel sampling: coverage and voting

Suppose one sample solves a problem with probability \(p_s\), independently across samples. The probability that at least one of \(n_s\) samples is correct, the coverage or pass@\(n_s\), is

\[ \text{pass@}n_s\;=\;1-(1-p_s)^{n_s}. \]

Coverage matters when correct answers can be recognised: code that passes unit tests, a proof that a checker accepts, a numerical answer that can be verified. With a perfect verifier, accuracy equals coverage, and even rare successes become useful: a problem solved once in fifty samples is solved with probability 0.87 by a hundred samples. In practice we estimate pass@\(n_s\) from \(n\ge n_s\) samples of which \(c\) are correct; the naive estimate \(1-(1-c/n)^{n_s}\) is biased, and the standard unbiased estimator, introduced with OpenAI's Codex evaluation, is

\[ \widehat{\text{pass@}n_s}\;=\;1-\binom{n-c}{n_s}\Big/\binom{n}{n_s}, \]

the probability that a random subset of \(n_s\) of the \(n\) samples contains at least one correct one.

Figure 26.2 shows coverage for single problems of three difficulties and for a benchmark mixing difficulties. For one problem the curve is a saturating exponential, flat once \(n_s\) is a few multiples of \(1/p_s\). Averaged over a benchmark, however, coverage keeps growing roughly in proportion to \(\log n_s\) over two orders of magnitude, because ever-harder problems start to be solved; Brown and colleagues (2024)[6] observed exactly such log-linear, power-law-like scaling of coverage with samples, up to thousands of samples, on coding and mathematics. The catch is selection. Without a verifier, the system must decide which sample to return, and its accuracy is bounded by coverage but usually far below it.

The simplest selector is majority voting, the self-consistency method. For a question with a binary answer and per-sample accuracy \(p_s\), the majority of an odd number \(n_s\) of independent samples is correct with probability

\[ P_{\text{maj}}(n_s)\;=\;\sum_{i>n_s/2}\binom{n_s}{i}p_s^{\,i}(1-p_s)^{n_s-i}, \]

Condorcet's jury theorem from 1785. If \(p_s>1/2\), this tends to 1 as \(n_s\) grows; if \(p_s<1/2\), it tends to 0.

Figure 26.3 shows the theorem at work. Voting is a powerful amplifier when the model is more often right than wrong: from 65% per sample to over 96% with 33 votes. It cannot rescue a model that is usually wrong, and it saturates far below coverage on hard problems, where correct samples are rare. Real problems are kinder than the binary case in one respect: with numerical or free-form answers, wrong answers are usually spread over many values, so the correct answer can win a plurality even when it is produced by fewer than half of the samples. And real samples are not independent, since they come from the same model with the same biases, so the gains flatten earlier than the theorem predicts. Better selectors, such as outcome reward models, process reward models and, for code, execution against tests, close more of the gap between voting and coverage, which is why verification has become as important as generation in reasoning systems.

Worked example — what does a vote cost?

A reasoning model averages 6,000 thinking tokens and a 300-token answer per sample on a problem set, and is served on a deployment with a 20 ms TPOT at the batch size used. One sample: 6,300 decode steps, about 126 s of latency. Majority of 16: the 16 samples share the prompt (prefill once, copy-on-write KV blocks for the prompt; §10.7) and run concurrently in the batch, so latency is still about 126 s, plus vote time, but output tokens, the dominant cost, are 16 times larger: about 100,000 per problem. If output tokens are priced at a few dollars per million, a single problem costs tens of cents instead of a cent or two. Parallel scaling is cheap in latency and expensive in money; sequential scaling is the reverse.

26.4 Sequential thinking and its control

A reasoning model's main lever is the length of its chain of thought, and that length is learned, not set. During R1's reinforcement learning, the average response length rose steadily with training steps as the model discovered that longer reasoning earned more reward on hard problems. The resulting models think for different lengths on different problems, a few hundred tokens for simple questions and tens of thousands for hard ones, and their length distributions are heavy-tailed. They also overthink: Chen and colleagues (2024)[7] found reasoning models spending hundreds or thousands of tokens on "2 + 3 = ?", re-verifying answers they had already found. Since every token costs decode time, controlling thinking length is as much an inference concern as a modelling one.

Several controls have emerged. Reasoning effort settings, exposed by several APIs as low, medium and high, condition the model, through training or the system prompt, to think less or more. Thinking budgets cap the number of reasoning tokens; when the cap is reached, the engine forces the end of the thinking section and the model must answer. The s1 study (Muennighoff et al., 2025)[8] introduced "budget forcing" in both directions: truncating thinking at a maximum, and, to make a model think longer, suppressing its end-of-thinking token and appending "Wait", which often led it to re-examine and correct its answer, giving a simple, controllable test-time scaling curve. Training can also reward brevity, for example by penalising length beyond what correctness requires, and distillation from a reasoning model into a smaller one (§15.7) transfers much of its ability at a fraction of the per-token cost.

From the engine's point of view, these controls are features of the sampler and the stop-condition logic (§4.9): a budget is a per-request limit on tokens inside a delimited region, budget forcing is a logits modification at a chosen position, and reasoning effort is part of the prompt or of request metadata. Their effect on cost is first-order, because they cut the dominant term, the number of thinking tokens. A product that halves average thinking length at the same accuracy halves its inference bill.

26.5 The cost of thinking

A reasoning request is almost all decode. With \(n_{\text{in}}\) prompt tokens, \(n_{\text{think}}\) thinking tokens and \(n_{\text{out}}\) answer tokens, prefill costs about \(2Nn_{\text{in}}\) FLOPs once, and generation \(2N(n_{\text{think}}+n_{\text{out}})\) FLOPs spread over as many memory-bound decode steps. Its latency is dominated by \((n_{\text{think}}+n_{\text{out}})\times\text{TPOT}\), and its cache grows throughout: at the end of the request the sequence holds \(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{think}}+n_{\text{out}}\) tokens of KV. Long outputs therefore combine the cost structure of long context (Chapter 21) with that of decode, and they move the bottleneck of the whole system towards decode.

This changes the comparison between models. Consider answering a hard question either with a large model that answers directly or with a smaller reasoning model that thinks first. The FLOPs may be similar, but the latencies are not, because latency counts sequential steps, not FLOPs.

Worked example — think longer or use a bigger model?

Option A: Llama 3 70B answers in 500 tokens. Generation FLOPs \(\approx2\times70.6\times10^{9}\times500\approx7\times10^{13}\). On eight H100s with TP = 8 at batch 64, TPOT ≈ 8.3 ms (Figure 18.3), so latency ≈ 4 s. Option B: an 8B reasoning model thinks for 5,000 tokens and answers in 300. FLOPs \(\approx2\times8\times10^{9}\times5300\approx8.5\times10^{13}\), about the same. On one H100 per replica at batch 64, with prompts of about 1k tokens and an average live context of about 3.7k over the generation, each step reads 16 GB of weights and about 31 GB of cache, so TPOT ≈ 14 ms and latency ≈ \(5300\times14\) ms ≈ 74 s, eighteen times longer. Cost: A occupies 8 GPUs for about 4.2 s per batch of 64 answers, about 0.52 GPU-seconds per answer; B occupies one GPU for 74 s per 64 answers, about 1.16 GPU-seconds per answer. The reasoning model costs about twice as much per answer and takes eighteen times as long; if it answers hard questions that the 70B model cannot, it is still the right choice. Speed and price are no longer the same trade-off.

The memory profile of a reasoning request is also unusual. Its cache starts small and grows to many gigabytes over a minute or more: for Llama 3 8B, 20,000 tokens of thinking end with about 2.6 GB of cache per sequence. A batch of such requests grows its memory footprint continuously, and a scheduler that admitted requests on the basis of their prompt sizes alone would find itself out of memory halfway through, forced to preempt (§10.5), which for long outputs means recomputing or swapping tens of thousands of tokens.

Figure 26.4 sketches the two behaviours. Engines handle growth in three ways: reserving headroom by admitting fewer requests than the prompt sizes would allow, estimating output lengths from the request (reasoning effort, task type, the model's own history) and admitting accordingly, and preempting gracefully when growth exceeds the plan, preferring to swap rather than recompute long contexts. Output-length prediction, a research topic for scheduling in Chapter 11, becomes a practical necessity.

26.6 Serving reasoning models

The changes to the serving system follow from the cost structure. The first is the balance between phases. With prompts of a few thousand tokens and outputs of ten thousand, decode dominates; in a disaggregated deployment the pool ratio of §13.5 swings towards decode, and in a colocated one, prefill interference matters less while decode memory matters more. The second is latency targets. A user waiting a minute for an answer cares about total time and about seeing progress, so many products stream a summary of the reasoning, or the reasoning itself, while TPOT targets can be looser than for chat, which allows larger batches. The third is memory planning, discussed above: the KV pool must accommodate the final, not the initial, size of each request.

Several techniques from earlier chapters become more valuable. Speculative decoding (Chapter 20) attacks exactly the decode-dominated latency, and its gains persist at large batch because the long, growing contexts keep verification memory-bound (§20.6); reasoning text, with its repeated phrases and re-checks, also tends to be predictable. Prefix sharing makes parallel sampling cheap in prefill and memory: the \(n_s\) samples of a vote share the prompt's KV blocks by reference counting and copy-on-write (§10.7), so the marginal cost of a sample is its own generated tokens. KV quantisation and cache-efficient attention (Chapter 8) matter because contexts grow long during generation. And scheduling must cope with heavy-tailed output lengths: a few very long requests can occupy memory for minutes, so fairness and preemption policies (§11.7) decide whether short requests behind them wait.

Finally, reasoning changes what a "request" is. Agents interleave thinking with tool calls, so a single task may involve dozens of reasoning segments separated by tool results, each extending a context that should stay cached (§21.8). Products expose thinking in different ways, hiding it entirely, summarising it, or streaming it, which affects what the engine must return and when. And because thinking tokens are billed but often unseen, usage accounting must separate them, which the OpenAI-compatible API does with a reasoning-token count in its usage record.

Drill 26.1 — Memory planning for reasoning requests

A Llama 3 8B reasoning model runs on one H100 with about 55 GB for KV (BF16, 128 KiB per token). Prompts average 1,000 tokens and outputs 12,000 tokens. How many concurrent requests can be admitted if each must be guaranteed its full final length, and how many if admission uses prompt size only? What happens in the second case?

Solution. Final length 13,000 tokens × 128 KiB ≈ 1.7 GB per request, so about 32 requests can be guaranteed. By prompt size, 1,000 tokens × 128 KiB ≈ 131 MB, so about 420 could be admitted at first; but that already fills the pool with prompts, and as soon as they generate, each needs a new 16 MB block every 128 tokens, so the pool overflows almost immediately, long before any request finishes. The engine must then preempt most of them, recomputing or swapping their caches repeatedly, and throughput collapses. A practical engine admits somewhere in between, with an estimate of output length and some headroom, and uses an FP8 cache to double capacity.

26.7 Inference inside training: RL rollouts

Reasoning models are made by reinforcement learning (Chapters 23 and 25), and reinforcement learning for language models is mostly inference. In each training step, the current policy generates responses, called rollouts, to a batch of prompts; the responses are scored by a reward function, such as a checker for mathematical answers, unit tests for code, or a learned reward model; and the policy is updated to make high-reward responses more likely. The generation step produces long reasoning chains for thousands of prompts, often several per prompt, and dominates the wall-clock time of training.

GRPO, the algorithm behind R1 (§25.3), makes the inference structure explicit. Recall that for each prompt it samples a group of \(n_s\) responses, scores them \(\mathcal R_1,\dots,\mathcal R_{n_s}\), and uses as each response's advantage its reward standardised within the group,

\[ \hat A_i\;=\;\frac{\mathcal R_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathcal R_{1:n_s})}{\operatorname{std}(\mathcal R_{1:n_s})}, \]

so that no separate value network is needed; the policy-gradient update then raises the log-probability of tokens in above-average responses and lowers it in below-average ones, with a clipped ratio and a penalty keeping the policy near a reference. Group sampling is parallel test-time scaling used as a training signal, and it maps directly onto an inference engine's parallel sampling with a shared prompt. §25.7 estimated a full RLVR step; here we look at the two inference problems it raises most sharply, stragglers and weight synchronisation.

Figure 26.6 shows the loop. It poses inference problems of its own. Throughput: rollout generation must be as efficient as production serving, so RL frameworks embed production engines such as vLLM and SGLang rather than generating with training code. Weight synchronisation: after each update the engine needs the new weights, hundreds of gigabytes for a large model, moved from training to inference GPUs, or switched in place when the same GPUs alternate between roles, quickly enough not to dominate the step. Stragglers: rollout lengths are heavy-tailed, so in a synchronous design the whole batch waits for the longest response while most GPUs idle; asynchronous designs let the trainer consume finished rollouts while others continue, at the cost of training on slightly stale, off-policy samples, which the update rule must tolerate. Numerics: the log-probabilities computed by the inference engine and by the trainer must agree closely, since the update compares them, which connects to the batch-invariance and determinism issues of §4.10 and §12.9; mismatches between inference and training kernels have caused instabilities in practice.

The result is that the inference engine, once the last stage of a model's life, now sits inside its training loop. The same kernels, schedulers and caches that serve users generate the experience from which the next model learns, and improvements in inference efficiency translate directly into cheaper and faster training of reasoning models.

Drill 26.2 — The straggler problem

A synchronous RL step generates 4,096 rollouts whose lengths are roughly exponential with mean 4,000 tokens, all started together on a pool with TPOT 25 ms. Estimate how long the step's generation phase takes, and the average GPU utilisation during it, if the batch fits in memory and idle slots are not refilled.

Solution. The longest of 4,096 exponential lengths with mean 4,000 is about \(4000\times(\ln4096+0.58)\approx35{,}600\) tokens, so the phase lasts about \(35{,}600\times25\) ms ≈ 890 s. The average rollout takes \(4{,}000\times25\) ms = 100 s, so on average each slot is busy for about 100 of 890 s, about 11% utilisation (the batch's decode steps also get cheaper as it empties, but the slots are idle). This is why RL systems cap response lengths, refill freed slots with new prompts (continuous batching across RL steps), or go asynchronous, accepting slightly off-policy data.

Worked example — how long does weight synchronisation take?

Setup. An RL run trains Llama 3 70B on one cluster and generates rollouts on 16 separate 8×H100 servers. After each policy update, every rollout server needs the new 141 GB of BF16 weights. Each server has eight 400 Gb/s network adapters, about 400 GB/s in total.

Per server. If each rollout GPU receives exactly the shard it needs for its tensor-parallel layout, 17.6 GB, the transfer takes \(141/400\approx0.35\) s per server, provided the senders can supply it. Sending to all 16 servers from one copy of the weights at 400 GB/s would take 16 times as long, 5.6 s, so systems use a broadcast tree or let rollout servers forward to each other, keeping the total close to the 0.35 s plus a few hops of latency.

Against the step. Drill 26.2 found a synchronous generation phase of about 900 s. Even a naive 5.6 s synchronisation is under 1% of that. Weight sync matters only when steps are short (small models, short responses, asynchronous pipelines that update often) or when the layouts differ, because resharding from the trainer\'s layout to the engine\'s tensor-parallel layout can cost more than the transfer.

Consequence. For reasoning RL the inference engine\'s throughput, not the network, sets the pace. Doubling rollout throughput halves most of the step, whereas doubling the network speed saves less than a second.

26.8 Measuring reasoning: cost per solved problem

Reasoning makes the usual way of comparing models misleading. A benchmark table that reports accuracy alone hides the fact that one model may spend ten times the tokens of another to achieve it, and that a vote over sixteen samples may cost sixteen times as much as a single answer. The quantity that matters for a product is accuracy as a function of cost, and the natural single number is the cost per solved problem: the expected inference cost of a query divided by the probability that it is answered correctly. Reported results should therefore state the sampling budget explicitly, pass@1 (one sample), maj@\(n_s\) (majority of \(n_s\)) or pass@\(n_s\) with a verifier, together with the average number of thinking and output tokens.

Plotting accuracy against tokens per query gives an accuracy–cost frontier for each model, in the same spirit as the latency–throughput frontiers of Chapters 6 and 18. Different models and settings trace different curves: a small model at high reasoning effort, a large model at low effort, a distilled model with a vote, each occupies some region, and for a given task the dominant options are those on the upper-left envelope. Two lessons recur. First, the frontier is task-dependent: for routine questions, non-reasoning or low-effort modes dominate, because extra thinking buys little; for hard problems, the extra tokens are where the accuracy comes from. Second, the price of tokens is not the only cost. Latency matters to users, and the long contexts produced by thinking raise the per-token cost of serving through memory pressure, which is why providers price output tokens several times higher than input tokens and why a reasoning model's effective cost per token is higher than its list price suggests.

Routing turns these lessons into systems. A lightweight classifier, or the model itself, estimates a query's difficulty and chooses a mode: answer directly, think briefly, think at length, or sample several times and verify. Because most traffic in typical products is easy, routing can cut average tokens per query severalfold while preserving accuracy on the hard tail, and it is one of the main levers that Chapter 27 will develop for serving at scale.

Drill 26.3 — Cost per solved problem

On a benchmark, mode A (no thinking) answers in 400 tokens with 55% accuracy; mode B (thinking) uses 6,000 tokens with 80% accuracy; mode C (B with a 5-sample majority vote) reaches 86%. With output tokens costing the same in each mode, compare cost per solved problem. If a router could send the 50% of queries that mode A already solves to A and the rest to B, what would the blended accuracy and cost be, assuming the router is perfect and B solves 60% of the hard half?

Solution. Cost per solved problem: A, \(400/0.55\approx730\) tokens; B, \(6000/0.80=7{,}500\); C, \(30{,}000/0.86\approx34{,}900\). A is cheapest per solved problem but fails on 45% of queries; C buys six points over B at more than four times the cost per solve. With routing: half the queries go to A (400 tokens, solved), half to B (6,000 tokens, 60% solved), so accuracy is \(0.5+0.5\times0.6=80\%\), matching B, at an average of 3,200 tokens per query, about half of B's cost. Routing by difficulty moves the system onto a better point of the accuracy–cost frontier.

Looking ahead

Reasoning models made inference the dominant cost of frontier AI and made its efficiency a business concern at every scale. The next question is how to run inference as a service across a fleet: how to route requests among many replicas and models, scale capacity with demand that varies by the hour, serve many fine-tuned variants of one base model on the same GPUs, and account for what each request costs. Chapter 27 takes up serving at scale: routing and load balancing, autoscaling, multi-LoRA serving, multi-tenancy and the economics of a production LLM service.

Chapter summary

  • Chain of thought gives a fixed-depth model more sequential computation by writing intermediate steps; self-consistency samples many chains and votes; process reward models score steps. o1 (2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (2025) trained long reasoning with RL; R1-Zero's responses lengthened and self-checking emerged from outcome rewards alone (AIME 15.6% → 71.0%).
  • Test-time compute: sequential (think longer; latency grows), parallel (sample and select; latency of one sample, cost × \(n_s\)), search (verifier-guided). Its best allocation depends on difficulty; it partly substitutes for model size.
  • Coverage pass@\(n_s=1-(1-p_s)^{n_s}\), unbiased estimator \(1-\binom{n-c}{n_s}/\binom{n}{n_s}\); over benchmarks coverage grows roughly linearly in \(\log n_s\). Majority voting (Condorcet) amplifies \(p_s>1/2\) towards 1 and \(p_s<1/2\) towards 0; verifiers close the gap to coverage.
  • Thinking length is learned and heavy-tailed; models overthink. Controls: reasoning effort, thinking budgets, budget forcing ("Wait"), length penalties, distillation.
  • Cost: reasoning requests are decode-dominated; latency ≈ (thinking + answer tokens) × TPOT; FLOPs may match a larger model's while latency is many times longer. Caches grow during generation (e.g. 2.6 GB for 20k tokens on 8B).
  • Serving: decode-heavy pools, admission with expected output length and headroom, graceful preemption, speculative decoding, prefix sharing for parallel samples, KV quantisation, streaming of reasoning, reasoning-token accounting.
  • RL rollouts (GRPO: group-standardised advantages) make inference the dominant cost of training; challenges are throughput, weight sync, stragglers (heavy-tailed lengths) and inference–training numerical agreement.

Warm-up What is test-time compute, and why did it become important in 2024–25?

Test-time compute is computation spent per query beyond the minimum to emit an answer: thinking longer (long chains of thought), sampling several answers and selecting (voting, reward models, verifiers), or searching over partial solutions. It became important when models trained with reinforcement learning to reason (OpenAI o1, September 2024; DeepSeek-R1, January 2025) showed accuracy rising smoothly with the logarithm of thinking compute, a second scaling axis besides training compute. It shifts cost from one-off training to every request, makes outputs long (thousands to tens of thousands of tokens), and makes inference efficiency a primary driver of product economics.

Warm-up Explain self-consistency and why it works.

Sample several independent chains of thought at non-zero temperature for the same question and return the most common final answer. If a single sample is correct with probability above one half (or the correct answer is the plurality among many possible answers), the majority of many samples is correct with higher probability, by Condorcet's jury theorem: for binary answers, \(P_{\text{maj}}(n_s)=\sum_{i>n_s/2}\binom{n_s}{i}p_s^i(1-p_s)^{n_s-i}\to1\). Gains flatten because samples are correlated, and voting cannot help if the model is usually wrong. It costs \(n_s\) times the output tokens but not \(n_s\) times the latency, since samples run in parallel and share the prompt's KV.

Core Derive pass@k coverage and its unbiased estimator.

With independent samples each correct with probability \(p_s\), the probability all \(n_s\) are wrong is \((1-p_s)^{n_s}\), so pass@\(n_s=1-(1-p_s)^{n_s}\). To estimate it for a problem from \(n\ge n_s\) samples with \(c\) correct, plugging \(\hat p=c/n\) into the formula is biased (Jensen). Instead compute the probability that a uniformly random subset of \(n_s\) of the \(n\) samples contains at least one correct: \(1-\binom{n-c}{n_s}/\binom{n}{n_s}\), which is unbiased for pass@\(n_s\); average over problems. Over benchmarks with heterogeneous difficulty, coverage grows roughly log-linearly with \(n_s\), since problems with tiny \(p_s\) start to be solved.

Core How do thinking length and parallel sampling differ in cost and latency?

Thinking longer: one sequence generates more tokens; FLOPs and output tokens grow linearly, and latency grows linearly too, since tokens are produced sequentially at TPOT each; the cache grows, reducing batch capacity. Parallel sampling: \(n_s\) sequences share the prompt; output tokens and FLOPs grow \(n_s\)-fold, but latency stays that of one sample (they decode concurrently in the batch), and prompt KV is shared by reference. Example: 6,300 tokens per sample at 20 ms TPOT is ~126 s either way for one sample; a 16-sample vote keeps ~126 s but costs 16× the output tokens; thinking 16× longer would take ~34 minutes. The best mix depends on task difficulty, verifier availability and latency targets.

Core Why does a reasoning model’s memory footprint make scheduling harder?

Its KV cache grows throughout generation: a request with a 1k prompt and 12k output ends with 13k tokens of cache (≈ 1.7 GB for Llama 3 8B in BF16). Admitting by prompt size lets the batch's memory grow until the pool overflows, forcing preemption of requests that have generated thousands of tokens, which is costly (recompute or swap). Output lengths are heavy-tailed and unknown. Engines therefore reserve headroom, estimate output length (from reasoning effort, task type, history) for admission, use FP8 caches to raise capacity, prefer swapping over recomputation for long contexts, and choose preemption victims carefully. Fairness also matters: a few very long requests can occupy memory for minutes.

Core What controls exist for reasoning length, and where are they implemented?

Reasoning effort (low/medium/high) conditions the model through training or prompt; thinking budgets cap reasoning tokens and force the end of the thinking section when reached; budget forcing (s1) suppresses the end-of-thinking token and appends 'Wait' to extend reasoning, or truncates it; length penalties in RL training discourage unnecessary length; distillation transfers reasoning to smaller models with lower per-token cost. In the engine these are sampler and stop-logic features: per-request counters inside delimited regions, logits modifications at chosen positions, and request metadata. Since thinking tokens dominate cost, these controls are first-order levers on the inference bill.

Core Explain GRPO and why it suits LLM reinforcement learning.

For each prompt, sample a group of \(n_s\) responses from the current policy, score them with a reward (verifier, tests, reward model), and compute each response's advantage as its reward standardised within the group, \(\hat A_i=(\mathcal R_i-\text{mean})/\text{std}\). The policy gradient increases log-probabilities of tokens in above-average responses and decreases those in below-average ones, with PPO-style clipping and a KL penalty to a reference policy. It needs no learned value network (saving memory and avoiding value estimation over long sequences), and its group sampling maps directly onto efficient parallel sampling with shared prompts in an inference engine. Used for DeepSeekMath and DeepSeek-R1.

Deep-dive Compare a small reasoning model with a large direct-answer model on cost and latency.

FLOPs per answer are about \(2N\times\) generated tokens. A 70B model answering in 500 tokens uses ~\(7\times10^{13}\) FLOPs; an 8B model thinking 5,000 tokens and answering in 300 uses ~\(8.5\times10^{13}\), similar. But latency counts sequential steps: 500 steps at ~8 ms (70B, TP = 8) ≈ 4 s versus 5,300 steps at ~14 ms (8B, one GPU, batch 64, growing cache) ≈ 74 s. GPU cost per answer: ~0.5 GPU-s versus ~1.2 GPU-s, because the reasoning model's growing caches limit batching and decode is memory-bound. So the reasoning model is slower and somewhat more expensive, but may answer harder questions; the trade-off is quality per dollar and per second, and depends on the task mix. Speculative decoding and thinking budgets narrow the latency gap.

Deep-dive What makes RL rollout generation hard to run efficiently, and how do systems address it?

Volume: thousands of prompts × several samples × thousands of tokens per step, so generation dominates; systems embed production engines (vLLM, SGLang) with continuous batching and prefix sharing. Stragglers: heavy-tailed lengths mean a synchronous step waits for the longest rollout (for 4,096 exponential lengths with mean 4k, the max is ~9× the mean, ~11% utilisation); remedies are length caps, refilling freed slots with new prompts, partial rollouts carried over, and asynchronous training on slightly stale data with off-policy corrections. Weight sync: hundreds of GB of new weights per step must reach rollout GPUs quickly (NCCL broadcast, in-place updates when GPUs alternate roles, resharding between training and inference layouts). Numerics: inference and training log-probs must agree for importance ratios; mismatched kernels or batch-dependent numerics cause instability, motivating batch-invariant kernels and recomputation of log-probs by the trainer.

Deep-dive When should a system use a verifier or reward model instead of majority voting?

Voting needs no extra model and works when the correct answer is the most common one, but it is bounded by the model's typical behaviour and needs discrete, comparable answers. A verifier (unit tests, proof checker, symbolic evaluation) is best when available: it turns coverage into accuracy, so even rare correct samples are found, which makes large \(n_s\) worthwhile. Outcome reward models generalise to unverifiable tasks but can be gamed and are imperfect, so best-of-\(n_s\) with a learned RM can degrade at large \(n_s\) (reward hacking). Process reward models score steps and enable search and early pruning, saving compute on bad partial solutions. In practice: verifiers for code and maths, RMs or voting for open-ended tasks, and search when a good PRM exists and latency allows.

Deep-dive How do reasoning workloads change disaggregated serving and capacity planning?

Outputs (thinking + answer) far exceed prompts, so the decode pool must be much larger than prefill (pool ratio of §13.5 falls well below 1), and decode memory is the binding constraint because caches grow to the full output length. Capacity planning must use output-length distributions (heavy-tailed) rather than means, reserve KV headroom, and model preemption. TPOT targets can be looser than for chat (users wait for a complete answer, perhaps watching a reasoning summary), allowing larger batches, but long contexts during decode limit batch anyway. Speculative decoding and FP8 caches are high-value. KV transfer cost per request is small relative to the long decode, so disaggregation's transfer overhead is easily amortised. Routing can separate reasoning and non-reasoning traffic onto pools tuned for each.

Deep-dive Why do reasoning models overthink, and how can inference systems mitigate it?

RL with outcome rewards rewards correctness, not efficiency; once longer reasoning helps on hard problems, the model learns to reason long generally, including re-verifying easy answers. Lengths are heavy-tailed and loosely tied to difficulty. Mitigations: training-side length penalties or rewards for concise correct answers; reasoning-effort conditioning; thinking budgets enforced by the engine; early exit when the answer stabilises (detect repeated conclusions); routing easy queries to non-reasoning modes or smaller models via a classifier; and distilled models with calibrated lengths. Monitoring tokens per request by task type identifies where budgets can be cut without accuracy loss, the most direct cost lever for reasoning products.

Design Design the serving system for a maths-tutoring product built on a reasoning model.

Workload: short prompts, long reasoning (thousands of tokens), interactive users who want progress. Model: a distilled reasoning model sized for cost, with reasoning-effort control; route simple questions to low effort. Serving: decode-heavy deployment (colocated with chunked prefill or disaggregated with a large decode pool), FP8 weights and cache, speculative decoding (EAGLE or MTP) for latency, streaming a reasoning summary or progress indicator, thinking budgets per tier. Memory: admission by estimated final length with headroom; graceful preemption by swapping. Quality: optional verification of final numeric answers (symbolic checker) with a second sample on failure; self-consistency only for hard problems flagged by low confidence. Metrics: time to answer, tokens per request by effort level, accuracy on a held-out set, cost per solved problem.

Design A team wants to run RL training for a 32B reasoning model on 256 GPUs. How would you allocate GPUs between rollouts and training?

Estimate per step: prompts × samples × mean rollout length gives generation tokens; with the engine's throughput per GPU (decode-bound, long contexts), compute rollout GPU-seconds; the trainer's cost is forward and backward over the same tokens (~3× a forward), plus reference and old-policy log-probs. Typically rollouts dominate, so allocate the majority of GPUs to generation (for example 192 generation, 64 training) or time-share all GPUs, alternating roles with in-place weight updates. Reduce stragglers with length caps and slot refilling; consider asynchronous pipelines (training on step t while generating step t+1) with off-policy correction. Use fast weight sync (NCCL broadcast from trainer to inference shards, resharding TP layouts). Measure GPU utilisation of each pool and rebalance; ensure inference and trainer numerics match.

Core What are the main differences between serving a chat model and a reasoning model?

Output length: hundreds of tokens versus thousands to tens of thousands, so reasoning is decode-dominated in cost and latency. Memory: reasoning caches grow to the final length; admission and preemption must plan for it. Latency expectations: users wait longer but want progress (streamed reasoning or summaries); TPOT targets can be looser. Variance: heavy-tailed lengths complicate scheduling and fairness. Features: reasoning budgets and effort levels, reasoning-token accounting, hidden or summarised thoughts, parallel sampling and voting. Optimisations that matter more: speculative decoding, FP8 KV, cache-efficient attention, large decode pools; prefill optimisations matter less unless prompts are long.

Core Why does the inference engine matter for training reasoning models?

RL for reasoning generates rollouts with the current policy each step, and generation of long chains for thousands of prompts dominates training time. So the engine's throughput (continuous batching, paged KV, prefix sharing for group samples, CUDA graphs, quantisation where acceptable, speculative decoding) directly sets the speed and cost of training. Additional requirements beyond serving: fast weight updates every step, deterministic or batch-invariant numerics so that inference log-probs match the trainer's, support for logprob outputs, handling of stragglers, and integration with training frameworks. Improvements in inference engines thus translate into cheaper and faster RL, which is why RL frameworks embed vLLM and SGLang.

Design How would you report and compare reasoning models fairly for a product decision?

Use the product's own task distribution, stratified by difficulty, and report for each model and setting: accuracy under an explicit sampling budget (pass@1, maj@\(n_s\), or pass@\(n_s\) with the verifier the product will actually use), mean and tail thinking and output tokens, latency to final answer at the deployment's TPOT, and cost per solved problem (expected cost divided by accuracy). Plot accuracy against tokens (or dollars) per query to see the frontier, including low- and high-effort modes and routed combinations. Measure under realistic serving conditions, since long reasoning contexts reduce batch sizes and raise the true per-token cost. Check robustness (variance across seeds, sensitivity to prompt format) and failure modes such as overthinking on easy items. Choose the configuration on the upper-left envelope that meets the product's latency and accuracy targets, and revisit as models and prices change.

Core Why are output tokens priced higher than input tokens, and why is this especially relevant for reasoning models?

Input tokens are processed in prefill, a compute-bound, highly parallel pass in which thousands of tokens share each weight read, so their cost per token is low (and lower still with prefix caching). Output tokens are produced one per sequence per decode step, a memory-bound operation in which the weights and the sequence's whole KV cache are read for each token; even with batching, the GPU time per output token is several times that per input token, and each output token also lengthens the cache that every later step must read. Providers therefore price output tokens several times higher. Reasoning models generate thousands to tens of thousands of output tokens per request, and their growing caches limit batch sizes, so their cost is dominated by exactly the expensive kind of token. Thinking budgets, routing by difficulty, distillation and speculative decoding attack this dominant term.
