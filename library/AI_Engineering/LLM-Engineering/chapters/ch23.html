Part IX · Production — Chapter 27

Serving at Scale: Routing, Autoscaling, Multi-LoRA and Cost

A production LLM service is not one engine but a fleet: many replicas of several models behind a gateway, scaled with demand, shared by many tenants and many fine-tuned variants, and accountable for what each token costs. This chapter develops the fleet-level questions an inference engineer must answer: how to route requests, how to scale capacity when starting a replica takes minutes, how to serve thousands of LoRA adapters on shared GPUs, how to isolate tenants, and how to compute and reduce the cost of a million tokens.

Why this chapter

Every earlier chapter optimised something inside a single replica: a kernel, a cache, a scheduler, a parallel layout. Real services run hundreds or thousands of replicas, and a new set of problems appears at that scale. Requests must be spread across replicas so that none is overloaded while others idle, and so that requests land where their prefix caches already are. Demand follows the clock, peaking in working hours and falling at night, and capacity must follow it without the minutes-long cold starts of large models breaking latency targets. Customers fine-tune models, and serving each fine-tune on its own GPUs would be ruinously expensive. Tenants with different priorities and contracts share the same hardware. And at the end of every month someone asks what each million tokens cost, and whether it could cost less.

These are systems problems with quantitative answers, and many of them reuse ideas from earlier chapters at a larger scale: queueing theory (§11.8) for routing and capacity, the roofline and batching for cost, paging (Chapter 10) for adapters, and cache-aware routing (§13.8) for prefixes. Some come from the wider theory of distributed systems, such as the "power of two choices" in load balancing and token-bucket rate limiting. We take them in the order a request meets them: the fleet's architecture, routing, scaling, adapters, tenancy, model choice, cost and reliability.

Learning objectives
  1. Describe the architecture of an LLM serving fleet: gateway, router, replicas, autoscaler, model registry and observability.
  2. Analyse load-balancing policies with queueing results, including the power of two choices, and combine load with cache affinity.
  3. Design autoscaling for LLMs, accounting for cold-start time, the right scaling signals and headroom.
  4. Explain LoRA adapters and how S-LoRA and Punica serve thousands of them on shared GPUs, and compute their memory and compute overheads.
  5. Implement multi-tenant isolation with rate limits, priorities and fair scheduling, and model cascades and routers across models.
  6. Compute the cost per million tokens of a deployment and identify the levers that reduce it; design for reliability.

Uses: Chapters 3, 6, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 18. Symbols: notation page.

27.1 From one engine to a fleet

Figure 27.1 shows the components of a typical serving fleet. Requests arrive at an API gateway, which authenticates the caller, enforces rate limits and quotas, records usage for billing, and attaches metadata such as the tenant, the requested model or adapter, and the priority. A router chooses a replica for each request, using load, cache affinity and the replica's capabilities. Replicas are the engines of Chapter 12, each on one GPU or a parallel group, possibly organised into disaggregated prefill and decode pools (Chapter 13). An autoscaler adds and removes replicas as demand changes, drawing weights from a model registry that stores model and adapter versions. And an observability layer collects the metrics, traces and logs from every component (§12.8, Chapter 28) that drive the router, the autoscaler and the people operating the system.

Two properties of LLM traffic shape every component. Requests are heterogeneous: one may be a 100-token chat turn and the next a 100,000-token document or a 20,000-token reasoning chain, so request counts are a poor measure of load, and tokens, separately for prefill and decode, are the right unit. And requests are long-lived: a streaming response holds resources on its replica for seconds to minutes, so placement decisions cannot be revised cheaply once made. With those in mind, the first decision is where to send each request.

27.2 Routing and load balancing

The simplest routers send each request to a random replica, or to replicas in turn (round-robin). Both ignore the replicas' state, and with heterogeneous requests they regularly send work to replicas that are already busy. At the other extreme, join the shortest queue (JSQ) sends each request to the least-loaded replica, which balances best but requires up-to-date load information from every replica for every decision, a coordination cost that grows with the fleet. Between them lies a celebrated result. Mitzenmacher (1996) showed that sampling just two replicas at random and choosing the less loaded of the two, the power of two choices, captures most of the benefit of JSQ. In the classical "supermarket" queueing model with \(n_r\) servers at utilisation \(u\), the fraction of servers with at least \(i\) queued jobs decays as \(u^i\) under random assignment but as

\[ u^{(n_c^{\,i}-1)/(n_c-1)} \]

when the router samples \(n_c\ge2\) servers: doubly exponentially in \(i\). Long queues become vanishingly rare with only two samples per decision.

Figure 27.2 confirms the theory with a small simulation of sixteen replicas. At moderate utilisation the policies differ by a factor of two in tail latency; at 90% utilisation, random routing's 99th percentile is fourteen times JSQ's, round-robin's three and a half times, and the power of two choices only one and a half. For LLM serving the lesson carries over with two adjustments. First, the load measure must be in tokens, not requests: outstanding prefill tokens, active decode sequences and KV-cache usage are far better predictors of a replica's delay than its queue length. Second, load is not the only consideration, because a request whose prefix is cached on one replica is much cheaper there (§10.6, §13.8). The routers of production systems therefore combine the two: among a small set of candidate replicas, chosen by cache affinity or at random, pick the one with the best score of cached prefix against load, as in the KV-aware routing of §13.8.

A related technique is consistent hashing, which maps each session or prefix to a replica by hashing, so that requests sharing a conversation or document land on the same replica and hit its cache, while adding or removing a replica moves only a small fraction of keys. Plain consistent hashing can overload a replica that happens to own popular keys; the "bounded loads" variant caps each replica at a small multiple of the average and spills the excess to the next replica on the ring, trading a little cache affinity for balance. Session affinity, prefix-hash routing and load-aware selection are the three ingredients of almost every LLM router, weighted differently by product: chat with long system prompts favours affinity, bursty batch jobs favour balance.

Worked example — what cache-affinity routing saves

Setup. A chat product serves Llama 3 8B on 8 replicas. A typical turn arrives with 4,000 tokens of conversation history and 200 new tokens. Each replica caches the prefixes it has seen (§10.6).

Without affinity. If the load balancer chooses replicas without regard to content, a conversation\'s next turn lands on the replica holding its history with probability \(1/8\). The other 7 times in 8, the whole 4,200-token prompt is prefilled: about \(2\times7.5\times10^9\times4200\approx6.3\times10^{13}\) FLOPs, 127 ms at 50% MFU on an H100.

With affinity. Routing each conversation to the replica that holds its prefix (by hashing the conversation or by matching prefixes) prefills only the 200 new tokens: about 6 ms. TTFT falls by about 120 ms and prefill GPU time per turn by 95%, which on a chat workload frees a large share of the fleet\'s compute for decode.

The cost. Affinity fights load balancing: a popular conversation or tenant pins its load to one replica, and a replica that fails or is drained loses its cache. Production routers therefore combine the two: prefer the replica with the longest cached prefix unless its queue is more than some threshold longer than the least-loaded one. That trade-off between cache hits and queueing is the routing analogue of the batching–latency trade-off of Chapter 11.

27.3 Autoscaling

Demand for an interactive LLM service follows human schedules: a daily cycle with a peak several times the trough, weekly patterns, launches and incidents that bring sudden spikes. Provisioning for the peak at all hours wastes most of the fleet most of the time; scaling with demand saves money, provided capacity arrives before it is needed. The difficulty is that a new replica of a large model is slow to start. It must be scheduled onto a free GPU, possibly pulling a container image of many gigabytes; it must load its weights, 16 GB for an 8B model and 141 GB for a 70B one in BF16, from local disk at several GB/s or from network storage at one or two; it must initialise the engine, allocate the KV pool, and capture CUDA graphs or compile kernels. End to end, a cold start ranges from tens of seconds for a small model with weights on local NVMe to several minutes for a large one fetched over the network.

Figure 27.3 shows the consequence. A reactive autoscaler that adds a replica when load crosses a threshold delivers it one cold start later; during a steep ramp, demand exceeds capacity for that interval, queues grow, and TTFT targets are missed. The requirement is simple to state: capacity at time \(t\) must cover the demand at time \(t+\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the cold-start time. Three practices follow. Scale on the right signals. GPU utilisation, as reported by nvidia-smi, is a poor signal (§12.8); queue length, outstanding tokens, KV-cache usage and TTFT or TPOT against targets are good ones, because they measure the quantities users experience. Keep headroom. Run at a target utilisation below the knee of the queueing curve (§11.8), so that a replica's worth of growth can be absorbed while new capacity starts; Figure 27.2 shows how quickly tails grow above 80–90%. Predict. Daily patterns are regular enough to forecast, and scaling ahead of the forecast, with reactive scaling as a backstop for surprises, removes most deficits.

The other lever is to shrink \(\tau\). Weights can be pre-staged on local disks of idle machines, loaded with parallel reads, or streamed directly into GPU memory; engines can snapshot an initialised process, including compiled kernels and captured CUDA graphs, and restore it; a pool of "warm" replicas, started but idle, can absorb spikes in seconds at the cost of their GPUs. For many small models or adapters, a single replica can host several and swap between them (§27.4). Scale to zero, popular in serverless platforms, is attractive for rarely used models but pays a full cold start on the first request after idling, which is acceptable for batch work and rarely for interactive use.

Worked example — headroom for a morning ramp

A chat service on Llama 3 70B (TP = 8 replicas, each handling about 15,000 output tokens/s at its target latency) sees demand rise from 300,000 to 900,000 output tokens/s over two hours each morning, roughly linearly, so 5,000 tokens/s per minute. A cold start takes 4 minutes. During those 4 minutes demand rises by 20,000 tokens/s, more than one replica's capacity. A reactive policy that waits until replicas are 90% loaded will therefore fall behind throughout the ramp. Either keep two replicas (30,000 tokens/s) of spare capacity at every point of the ramp, or start replicas from a forecast at least 4 minutes ahead of need, or cut the cold start to under a minute with local weights and snapshots, in which case one replica of headroom suffices. At the peak, 60 replicas (480 GPUs) are needed; at the overnight trough, 20.

27.4 Serving many fine-tunes: multi-LoRA

Many products are built on fine-tuned variants of a few base models, one per customer, task or language, and most fine-tunes are made with low-rank adaptation (LoRA; Hu et al., 2021)[1]. LoRA freezes the base weights and learns, for selected weight matrices \(W\in\mathbb R^{d_{\text{in}}\times d_{\text{out}}}\), a low-rank update \(\Delta W=W_{\downarrow}W_{\uparrow}\) with \(W_{\downarrow}\in\mathbb R^{d_{\text{in}}\times r_a}\) and \(W_{\uparrow}\in\mathbb R^{r_a\times d_{\text{out}}}\), for a small rank \(r_a\) (8 to 64 is typical). The adapted layer computes \(xW+(xW_{\downarrow})W_{\uparrow}\). An adapter is tiny: for Llama 3 8B with rank 16 on all attention and MLP projections of all 32 layers, it has \(r_a\sum(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}})\approx42\) million parameters, about 84 MB in BF16, half a per cent of the base model.

Serving each adapter on its own replica would waste almost all of the GPU: the base weights, identical for every adapter, would be duplicated thousands of times. The alternative is to load the base model once and serve requests for many adapters in the same batch, each request using its own adapter. Two systems of 2023 made this efficient. Punica (Chen et al.)[2] introduced a batched kernel, SGMV (segmented gather matrix–vector multiplication), that computes \((xW_{\downarrow}^{(i)})W_{\uparrow}^{(i)}\) for a batch in which consecutive segments of rows use different adapters \(i\), so that one kernel launch serves all adapters in the batch while the base GEMM runs once for everyone. S-LoRA (Sheng et al.)[3] added memory management: adapters live in CPU memory and are paged into GPU memory on demand, in a unified paging pool shared with the KV cache, since both consist of many small tensors of varying lifetimes; it served thousands of adapters on a single GPU with throughput close to serving the base model alone.

Figure 27.4 shows the computation. The arithmetic of its overheads is straightforward. In FLOPs, an adapter adds \(2r_a(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}})\) per token per adapted matrix, about 84 MFLOPs per token for the rank-16 example, half a per cent of the base model's 16 GFLOPs. In bytes, which matter more in memory-bound decode, each distinct adapter in the batch must be read once per step: with \(k\) distinct adapters, \(k\times84\) MB on top of the base model's 16 GB. A batch spanning 32 adapters reads 2.7 GB of adapters per step, adding about 17% to the bytes and hence to TPOT; a batch of requests for the same adapter adds 0.5%. Routers therefore group requests by adapter where load allows, a form of affinity, and very popular fine-tunes may be merged into their own copy of the weights (\(W+\Delta W\)) and served as separate models, which removes the overhead entirely for them.

Worked example — memory for a thousand adapters

A platform hosts 1,000 rank-16 adapters of Llama 3 8B (84 MB each, 84 GB in total) on H100s. They cannot all live in GPU memory beside a useful KV pool, so most stay in host memory, and the GPU keeps a working set. If at any moment about 50 adapters have active requests on a replica, they need 4.2 GB of GPU memory, leaving about 50 GB for KV cache. Loading an adapter that is not resident takes about \(84\,\text{MB}/25\,\text{GB/s}\approx3\) ms over PCIe, far less than a prefill, so on-demand paging adds little latency if it is overlapped with the request's own prefill. The economics: one replica serves all 1,000 customers at nearly the cost of serving the base model, instead of 1,000 replicas.

27.5 Tenants, priorities and fairness

A shared fleet must protect its users from one another. Without controls, one tenant's burst of long-context requests can fill every replica's KV pool and starve everyone else's latency, the "noisy neighbour" problem. Three mechanisms provide isolation, at different timescales.

Rate limits at the gateway bound how much each tenant can submit. The standard mechanism is the token bucket: a bucket of capacity \(b\) fills at rate \(r\) and each request removes as many tokens as it will consume (input tokens, or an estimate of total tokens); a request that finds the bucket too empty is rejected or delayed. Over any interval of length \(T\), a tenant can consume at most \(b+rT\), so the bucket permits bursts up to \(b\) while enforcing a long-run rate \(r\). LLM providers typically limit both requests per minute and tokens per minute, with separate limits for input and output, because the two consume different resources.

Priorities and tiers decide who waits when capacity is short. Interactive traffic usually takes precedence over batch jobs, which can be queued for minutes or hours and run in troughs at a discount; paid tiers may take precedence over free ones. Within an engine, priority affects admission order and preemption choice (§11.7): a high-priority request can preempt a low-priority one's KV blocks. Across the fleet, priority affects routing, with dedicated or reserved capacity for the highest tiers. Batch APIs, which accept a file of requests and promise completion within a day at half price, exist precisely to move flexible work into the troughs of Figure 27.3.

Fair scheduling divides capacity among tenants who are all within their limits but together exceed it. The Virtual Token Counter of Sheng et al. (§11.7) adapts fair queueing to LLMs by charging each tenant for the tokens it has been served, weighted for input and output, and serving the tenant with the smallest counter next, which guarantees each backlogged tenant a fair share of throughput regardless of request sizes. Fairness at the fleet level additionally requires that routing does not concentrate one tenant's traffic on a few replicas, which interacts with cache affinity: a tenant with a long shared system prompt benefits from concentration, a tenant with a burst harms others by it.

Drill 27.1 — Sizing a token bucket

A tenant's contract allows 2 million tokens per minute with bursts of up to 30 seconds' worth. What bucket rate and capacity implement it? A request arrives needing 600,000 tokens when the bucket holds 400,000; how long must it wait?

Solution. Rate \(r=2\times10^{6}/60\approx33{,}300\) tokens/s. Capacity \(b=30r=10^{6}\) tokens. The request needs 200,000 more tokens than the bucket holds; at 33,300 per second that takes about 6 s, after which it is admitted and the bucket is emptied. Requests larger than \(b\) can never be admitted, so the limit also caps the largest single request; providers either size \(b\) above the maximum context or count only input tokens against the bucket at admission and output tokens as they are generated.

27.6 Choosing among models: cascades and routers

A fleet usually serves several models of different sizes and prices, and not every request needs the largest. A cascade sends each request first to a small, cheap model and escalates to a larger one only when the small model's answer is judged inadequate, by its own confidence, by a verifier, or by a learned scorer. FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, 2023)[4] showed that cascades over commercial APIs could match the best single model's accuracy at a fraction of the cost. A router makes the choice up front: a classifier predicts, from the query alone, which model is the cheapest that will answer well. RouteLLM (Ong et al., 2024)[5] trained such routers on human preference data and reported cost reductions of more than half at a small loss in quality relative to always using the strongest model.

Figure 27.5 contrasts the two. A cascade's expected cost per query is the small model's cost plus the escalation rate times the large model's cost, and its latency rises for escalated queries, which pay for both. A router pays only for the model it chooses, but a wrong prediction either wastes money (sending an easy query to the large model) or quality (sending a hard one to the small model). For reasoning models (Chapter 26), the same logic chooses among reasoning efforts rather than models. The inference-engineering consequence is that a fleet's traffic mix across models is a design variable, and the capacity plan, autoscaling and cost model must be computed per model.

27.7 What a million tokens costs

The cost of serving a token is the cost of the GPU time it consumes. For a replica of \(n_g\) GPUs priced at \(c_{\text{gpu}}\) dollars per GPU-hour, producing \(\Phi\) tokens per second at its operating point, the cost per million tokens is

\[ \text{cost per }10^6\text{ tokens}\;=\;\frac{n_g\,c_{\text{gpu}}}{3600\,\Phi}\times10^{6}\;\Big/\;u, \]

where \(u\) is the average utilisation of the replica: a replica that is busy half the time costs twice as much per token as one that is always busy. Every lever of this book appears in the formula: \(\Phi\) is set by the roofline, the batch size, quantisation, attention variant and speculative decoding; \(n_g\) by parallelism; \(u\) by routing, autoscaling and traffic shape; and \(c_{\text{gpu}}\) by the hardware generation and the purchasing arrangement.

Figure 27.6 evaluates the formula for Llama 3 8B on one H100. At batch 1 a million output tokens cost about $3.30; at batch 64, about 11 cents; beyond that the cache term flattens the curve. A deployment that is idle half the time pays twice as much at every point. This is why the economics of LLM serving are dominated by batch size and utilisation, and why the techniques that raise them, continuous batching, paged caches, quantisation, routing and autoscaling, matter more to cost than any single kernel. Input tokens cost far less than output tokens, because prefill processes thousands of tokens per weight read; with prefix caching, repeated input tokens cost less still, which is why providers discount cached input.

Worked example — self-host or use an API?

A team needs 2 billion output tokens and 8 billion input tokens per month from a 70B-class model, and can buy H100 time at $2.50 per GPU-hour. Self-hosting: a TP = 8 FP8 replica at 15 ms TPOT delivers about 35,000 output tokens/s (Figure 18.3 and §18.5, option C), plus prefill. Two billion output tokens per month is about 770 tokens/s on average, a small fraction of one replica, but traffic peaks at perhaps four times the average and the team needs redundancy, so it runs two replicas, 16 GPUs, around the clock: about \(16\times2.5\times730\approx\)$29,000 per month, or about $14.60 per million output tokens at this volume, because utilisation is under 10%. API: at illustrative prices of $0.60 per million input and $2.00 per million output tokens, the bill is about $4,800 + $4,000 = $8,800. The API wins by a factor of three at this volume. Self-hosting breaks even only when traffic fills the replicas: with the same 16 GPUs fully used, about 180 billion output tokens per month, the cost would fall to about 16 cents per million. Utilisation decides.

27.8 Reliability

A fleet of thousands of GPUs fails continuously: GPUs develop memory errors, NVLink or network links flap, hosts crash, engine processes run out of memory on unexpected inputs, and new software versions have bugs. Reliability engineering for LLM serving applies the standard practices of distributed systems, with a few LLM-specific twists. Health checks must test the whole path, a short generation with known output rather than a TCP ping, because a replica can accept connections while producing garbage. Failure domains matter: a tensor-parallel replica of eight GPUs fails if any one of them does, so large replicas are less available than small ones, and a deployment should spread replicas across hosts, racks and zones.

When a replica fails mid-stream, its in-flight requests lose their KV caches. The gateway can retry them elsewhere, but a retry repeats the prefill and, unless the partial output is kept and appended to the prompt, the user sees the response restart; good systems resume from the tokens already streamed. Timeouts and hedging handle slow rather than failed replicas: sending a duplicate of a request that has not started streaming within a deadline to a second replica cuts tail latency at the cost of some duplicated work, a classical technique that is cheap for short prefills and expensive for long ones. Graceful degradation keeps the service up under overload: shedding low-priority traffic first, lowering reasoning effort or maximum output length, routing to smaller models, and rejecting early with a clear error rather than queueing until timeouts (the early rejection of Mooncake, §13.7).

Software changes are the most common cause of incidents, so rollouts are gradual: a new engine version or model is deployed to a small fraction of replicas (a canary), compared on latency, error rates and output quality against the old version (Chapter 28), and expanded only if healthy, with automatic rollback otherwise. Draining a replica before upgrading it, by stopping new admissions and letting in-flight requests finish, avoids interrupting streams; for long reasoning requests that may take minutes, the drain must be correspondingly patient. These practices are unglamorous, but in production they determine what users experience at least as much as the kernels of Part II.

Drill 27.2 — The availability of a tensor-parallel replica

Each GPU in a fleet is unavailable 0.5% of the time, independently. What is the availability of a TP = 1 replica, a TP = 8 replica, and a service that needs at least 9 of 10 TP = 8 replicas to meet its load?

Solution. TP = 1: 99.5%. TP = 8: all eight GPUs must be up, \(0.995^8\approx96.1\%\). For the service: each replica is up with \(p=0.961\); the probability that at least 9 of 10 are up is \(p^{10}+10p^9(1-p)\approx0.672+0.273=0.945\). Even with a spare replica the service meets its capacity only 94.5% of the time, so it needs more spares, smaller replicas, or faster replacement of failed GPUs. Large parallel groups multiply the failure rate of their members.

27.9 Putting it together: a capacity plan

The pieces of this chapter combine into a procedure that every large deployment follows in some form, and it is worth carrying through once. Start from the demand model: requests per second over the day and week, and for each class of request (chat, document question answering, reasoning, batch) its distributions of input tokens, output tokens and prefix reuse. Convert demand into work: prefill tokens per second after cache hits, and decode tokens per second, per model. Choose a replica configuration for each model from the frontiers of Chapter 18, including precision, parallelism and whether to disaggregate, and measure (do not assume) each replica's sustainable throughput at the latency targets, for prefill and decode separately. Divide work by per-replica throughput at a target utilisation chosen from the latency-versus-load curve, add spares for failures and the headroom that one cold start requires, and the result is the replica count over time.

The second half of the plan is the set of policies that make the arithmetic true in operation. The router must keep replicas evenly loaded and caches warm, or the effective utilisation will be lower and the cache-hit rate assumed in the work estimate will not materialise. The autoscaler must follow the demand model with enough lead time. Rate limits must keep any one tenant within the assumptions. And the cost model closes the loop: GPU-hours per day times price, divided by tokens served, gives cost per million tokens by model and tenant, which can be compared with prices and with alternatives such as a smaller model, a cascade or an API.

Worked example — a capacity plan for a chat product

Demand: peak 2,000 requests/s, trough 600; each request averages 1,500 input tokens (1,000 of them a cached system prompt and history) and 350 output tokens. Work at peak: prefill of \(2000\times500=10^{6}\) uncached tokens/s and decode of \(7\times10^{5}\) tokens/s. Replica: Llama 3 8B in FP8 on one H100, measured at about 9,000 decode tokens/s at batch 128 within a 25 ms TPOT target, with chunked prefill absorbing about 15,000 prefill tokens/s on the same GPU. Prefill needs about 67 replica-equivalents and decode about 78, so decode binds: \(7\times10^{5}/9000\approx78\) replicas at full use. Target utilisation 75% at peak: 104 replicas. Spares and headroom: cold start 60 s, during which demand can rise by about 2%, plus one rack of 8 GPUs as failure spare: about 115 GPUs at peak, about 40 at the trough. Cost: averaging about 75 GPUs over the day at $2.50/hour is about $4,500 per day for about \(1300\times350\times86{,}400\approx3.9\times10^{10}\) output tokens (at an average of 1,300 requests/s), about 12 cents per million output tokens. The plan is only as good as its measured inputs; Chapter 28 is about obtaining them.

Drill 27.3 — What if the cache-hit rate falls?

In the worked example, a product change puts a timestamp at the top of the system prompt, so the 1,000 cached tokens per request are no longer reused. How do the prefill work, the binding resource and the number of peak replicas change?

Solution. Uncached prefill rises from 500 to 1,500 tokens per request, so prefill work triples to \(3\times10^{6}\) tokens/s at peak. At about 15,000 prefill tokens/s per replica (while also decoding), prefill now needs about 200 replica-equivalents against decode's 78: prefill binds. At 75% target utilisation that is about 267 replicas instead of 104, two and a half times the fleet, for a one-line change to a prompt template. Alternatively the team could disaggregate prefill onto a dedicated pool, but the cheaper fix is to move the timestamp to the end of the prompt. Prefix-cache hit rate belongs in every capacity plan and on every dashboard.

Looking ahead

Every decision in this chapter, which router, how much headroom, whether to merge an adapter, when to self-host, depends on measurements: throughput at a latency target, tail latency under load, cost per token, quality relative to a reference. Measuring these quantities correctly is harder than it looks; benchmarks are easy to run and easy to run wrongly, and production systems drift in ways that only good observability reveals. Chapter 28 takes up benchmarking, observability and evaluation: load-generation methodology and its pitfalls, standard benchmarks such as MLPerf Inference, tracing and metrics for live systems, and how to detect quality regressions when the model, the engine or the hardware changes.

Chapter summary

  • A fleet comprises a gateway (auth, rate limits, metering), router, replicas (possibly disaggregated pools), autoscaler, model registry and observability. LLM traffic is heterogeneous (measure load in tokens) and long-lived.
  • Routing: random and round-robin ignore state; join-the-shortest-queue is best but costly; the power of two choices makes queues of length \(i\) decay as \(u^{(n_c^i-1)/(n_c-1)}\) and nearly matches JSQ (P99 at 90% load: 94 vs 10 vs 6.6 service times). LLM routers combine token-based load with prefix and session affinity, often via consistent hashing with bounded loads.
  • Autoscaling: capacity must lead demand by the cold-start time (tens of seconds to minutes: image, weights, engine, graphs); scale on queue depth, outstanding tokens, KV usage and latency against SLO, not GPU utilisation; keep headroom, forecast, and shrink cold starts with local weights, snapshots and warm pools.
  • Multi-LoRA: \(\Delta W=W_{\downarrow}W_{\uparrow}\) of rank \(r_a\); a rank-16 adapter of Llama 3 8B is ≈ 84 MB. Punica's SGMV batches many adapters with one base GEMM; S-LoRA pages adapters and KV in a unified pool. Overhead grows with distinct adapters per batch (≈ 17% bytes for 32 adapters); group by adapter, merge popular ones.
  • Tenancy: token-bucket limits (\(\le b+rT\) over any interval), priority tiers and batch APIs, fair schedulers such as VTC.
  • Cascades (FrugalGPT) and routers (RouteLLM) send most queries to cheap models; the traffic mix across models is a design variable.
  • Cost per million tokens \(=n_gc_{\text{gpu}}\times10^6/(3600\,\Phi\,u)\): batching moves it thirty-fold, utilisation multiplies it; self-hosting beats APIs only at high utilisation.
  • Capacity plan: demand model → prefill and decode work after cache hits → measured per-replica throughput → replicas at target utilisation plus spares and cold-start headroom → cost per million tokens; prefix-cache hit rate and output length are the most sensitive inputs.
  • Reliability: end-to-end health checks, failure domains (TP groups multiply failures), resume-from-stream retries, hedging, graceful degradation, canaries, drains and rollback.

Warm-up Describe the architecture of a production LLM serving fleet.

Clients reach an API gateway that authenticates, applies rate limits and quotas, meters usage and attaches tenant, model, adapter and priority. A router selects a replica for each request using token-based load (outstanding prefill tokens, active decodes, KV usage) and cache/session affinity. Replicas are inference engines (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM) on single GPUs or parallel groups, possibly split into prefill and decode pools, possibly hosting many LoRA adapters. An autoscaler adjusts replica counts from demand forecasts and live signals, pulling weights from a model registry. Observability (metrics, traces, logs, quality monitors) feeds routing, scaling, alerting and rollouts. Supporting pieces: KV/prefix stores, batch queues, canary deployment and rollback machinery.

Warm-up Explain the power of two choices and why it matters for LLM routing.

Instead of querying all replicas (join the shortest queue, expensive at scale) or choosing randomly (poor tails), the router samples two replicas at random and sends the request to the less loaded. In the supermarket model, the fraction of servers with at least \(i\) jobs falls from \(u^i\) (random) to \(u^{2^i-1}\), doubly exponentially, so long queues nearly vanish; in our simulation at 90% utilisation the P99 was 94 service times for random, 10 for two choices and 6.6 for JSQ. For LLMs, use tokens (outstanding prefill, decode slots, KV usage) as the load measure, and combine with cache affinity: choose candidates by prefix hash or session, then pick the least loaded among them.

Core How would you design autoscaling for a 70B chat service?

Measure the cold start end to end (scheduling, image pull, weight load, engine init, CUDA graphs) and shrink it: weights on local NVMe or pre-staged, parallel loading, process snapshots, warm idle replicas. Scale on signals that reflect user experience: queue depth and outstanding tokens per replica, KV usage, TTFT and TPOT percentiles against SLO, plus request rates by token class, not GPU utilisation. Keep headroom so that demand growth during one cold start is absorbed (in the example, ~2 replicas during the morning ramp). Use forecasts from daily and weekly patterns to scale ahead of demand, with reactive scaling as a backstop and hysteresis to avoid oscillation. Scale down by draining replicas. Route batch traffic into troughs. Validate with replayed traffic.

Core How do S-LoRA and Punica serve many adapters efficiently?

Both keep one copy of the base model and serve requests for different adapters in the same batch. The base GEMM \(xW\) runs once for all tokens; each token's adapter update \((xW_{\downarrow}^{(i)})W_{\uparrow}^{(i)}\) is computed by Punica's SGMV kernel, which handles a batch whose rows are grouped into segments using different adapters in one launch. S-LoRA stores adapters in CPU memory and pages them into a unified GPU pool shared with the KV cache, with custom kernels for non-contiguous adapter memory and scheduling that limits the number of distinct adapters per batch; it served thousands of adapters on a GPU. Overheads: small FLOPs (rank-16 adds ~0.5% for an 8B model) but extra bytes per distinct adapter per step (~84 MB each), so grouping by adapter and merging very popular adapters help.

Core Compute the cost per million output tokens for a deployment and name the main levers.

Cost per \(10^6\) tokens = \(n_gc_{\text{gpu}}\times10^6/(3600\,\Phi\,u)\). Example: Llama 3 8B on one H100 at $2.50/hour, batch 64, 2k context: roofline \(\Phi\approx6{,}400\) tokens/s, so about $0.11 per million at full utilisation and $0.22 at 50%. Levers: throughput \(\Phi\) (batching, continuous batching, paged KV, quantisation, GQA/MLA, speculative decoding, kernels), GPUs per replica \(n_g\) (use the smallest parallel degree meeting latency), utilisation \(u\) (routing, autoscaling, batch traffic in troughs, multi-model consolidation, multi-LoRA), GPU price (generation, reservations, spot for batch), and input-side savings (prefix caching, shorter prompts). Output tokens dominate cost; reasoning tokens even more so.

Core How would you protect tenants from each other on a shared fleet?

Gateway: token-bucket limits per tenant on requests and on input and output tokens (rate \(r\), burst \(b\); at most \(b+rT\) over any window), maximum context and output lengths, quotas. Scheduling: priority tiers (interactive over batch, paid over free) for admission and preemption in engines; fair queueing across tenants within a tier, such as VTC, charging tokens served. Capacity: reserved replicas or pools for high tiers, batch APIs for flexible work, early rejection under overload instead of unbounded queueing. Routing: avoid concentrating one tenant's burst on a few replicas, balancing against prefix affinity. Monitoring per tenant: latency, rejections and usage, to detect and contain noisy neighbours.

Core What are cascades and model routers, and how do they reduce cost?

Both exploit the fact that most queries are easy. A cascade sends every query to a cheap model first and escalates to a larger one only if the answer fails a check (confidence, verifier, scorer); expected cost = small cost + escalation rate × large cost, with extra latency on escalations (FrugalGPT). A router predicts, from the query alone, which model (or reasoning effort) suffices and sends it there directly (RouteLLM), paying one model per query but risking misroutes. Both need calibrated signals and evaluation on the real traffic mix; both make the traffic split across models a capacity-planning input. Typical gains are large cost reductions at small quality loss relative to always using the strongest model.

Deep-dive Why does tail latency explode at high utilisation, and how much headroom should a fleet keep?

Queueing theory (§11.8): waiting time grows like \(u/(1-u)\) for simple queues, and tails grow faster, so near saturation small load increases cause large delays; random routing makes it worse because some replicas are far above average load. Better routing (two choices, JSQ) flattens the curve (P99 at 90% load: 10 versus 94 service times), and LLM batching changes the service model (processor sharing within a batch) but not the qualitative behaviour: KV pools fill and admission queues grow. Headroom should be chosen from the SLO: measure P99 TTFT/TPOT versus load for the actual routing and engine, find the utilisation at which the SLO is just met, then keep enough spare capacity to absorb demand growth during one cold start plus failure of the largest replica. For many services this means average utilisation of 60–80% at peak.

Deep-dive Compute the memory and bandwidth overhead of serving many LoRA adapters in decode.

A rank-\(r_a\) adapter on a matrix \(d_{\text{in}}\times d_{\text{out}}\) adds \(r_a(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}})\) parameters. For Llama 3 8B, rank 16 on q, k, v, o, gate, up, down: per layer \(16\times(8192+5120+5120+8192+18432\times3)\approx1.31\)M, ×32 ≈ 42M parameters, 84 MB in BF16. FLOPs per token ≈ 84M, 0.5% of the base 16 GFLOPs. Bytes per decode step: base 16 GB plus 84 MB per distinct adapter in the batch: 32 distinct adapters add 2.7 GB (~17%), raising TPOT proportionally. Memory: a working set of resident adapters (e.g. 50 × 84 MB = 4.2 GB) plus CPU-resident cold adapters paged in over PCIe (~3 ms each). Policies: cap distinct adapters per batch, group by adapter in routing, prefetch on request arrival, merge heavy adapters into dedicated replicas.

Deep-dive When is self-hosting cheaper than an API, quantitatively?

Self-hosting cost per month ≈ GPUs × price × hours, fixed regardless of use; cost per token = that divided by tokens served, so it depends on utilisation. A 16-GPU FP8 70B deployment at $2.50/GPU-hour costs ~$29k/month; it can produce ~180B output tokens per month fully utilised (~16 cents per million), but at 2B tokens per month it costs ~$14.60 per million, far above typical API prices. Break-even volume = monthly fixed cost / API price per token, adjusted for redundancy (at least two replicas), peak-to-average ratio, engineering and operations effort, and input-token costs. Self-hosting also brings control (data, models, latency, fine-tunes, custom decoding) that can justify it below break-even; APIs bring elasticity and no idle cost.

Deep-dive How should a fleet handle replica failures during streaming responses?

Detect quickly with end-to-end health checks and stream-level timeouts. For in-flight requests, the KV cache on the failed replica is lost; the gateway should retry on another replica with the original prompt plus the tokens already streamed appended, so generation resumes rather than restarts (with the same sampling state where reproducibility matters), and suppress duplicate tokens to the client. For long contexts, if KV was mirrored to a tiered store, fetch it instead of recomputing. Rate-limit recovery so that a burst of retries does not overload the survivors, prioritising resumed requests. Spread replicas across failure domains; size spares for the loss of the largest replica; remember that TP groups fail if any GPU fails. Record incidents per GPU to evict flaky hardware.

Design Design the serving platform for a company offering fine-tuned 8B models to 5,000 customers.

Base model: one 8B family, FP8, on H100 replicas with TP = 1. Adapters: LoRA (rank 8–32) per customer, stored in a registry and in host memory; serve with S-LoRA/Punica-style multi-LoRA engines (unified paging, SGMV kernels). Routing: consistent hashing of customer to a subset of replicas (bounded loads) so each replica's resident adapter set stays small and its prefix caches stay warm; spill to others under load. Autoscaling on outstanding tokens and adapter load; warm replicas for spikes. Cost accounting per customer from tokens and adapter-switching overheads. Monitoring: per-adapter latency, distinct adapters per batch, adapter page-in rates, quality checks per adapter version; canaries for base-model and engine updates.

Design Your service’s GPU bill doubled while traffic rose 20%. How do you investigate?

Decompose cost per token = \(n_gc_{\text{gpu}}/(3600\,\Phi\,u)\). Check utilisation \(u\): did autoscaling headroom or minimum replica counts grow, is traffic spikier, did a routing change reduce batching or cache hits? Check throughput \(\Phi\): did the token mix change (longer outputs, reasoning modes, longer contexts reducing batch), did a model or engine upgrade lose quantisation or CUDA graphs, did speculative decoding get disabled, did prefix-cache hit rates fall (system prompt edits, timestamps), did multi-LoRA batches become more fragmented? Check \(n_g\): did parallel degrees increase? Check prices: on-demand replacing reserved capacity. Use per-replica metrics (tokens/s, batch size, KV usage, MBU), cost per tenant and per model, and compare before/after over the same traffic replay. Fix the largest factor first.

Design Design a multi-model routing system that keeps quality while halving cost.

Models: a small fast model, a mid model, a large or reasoning model. Collect labelled data: for a sample of real queries, outputs from each model judged by humans or a strong judge, giving per-query 'cheapest adequate model' labels. Train a lightweight router (embedding + classifier) to predict it, calibrated to a target quality; optionally add a cascade check (verifier or self-confidence) on the small model's answers for borderline queries. Evaluate offline the accuracy–cost frontier against always-large; choose a threshold meeting quality with ~50% cost. Deploy with shadow mode, then an A/B test on user metrics. Core What makes cold starts of LLM replicas slow, and how can they be shortened?

Steps: scheduling onto a GPU node (possibly provisioning a VM), pulling container images (often many GB), loading weights (16 GB for 8B, 141 GB for 70B BF16) from local disk (several GB/s) or network storage (1–2 GB/s), initialising the engine (allocating KV pools, NCCL groups for TP), compiling kernels and capturing CUDA graphs, and warming caches. Together: tens of seconds to several minutes. Shorten with pre-pulled images, weights on local NVMe or in host page cache, parallel and streaming weight loads directly to GPU, smaller formats (FP8 halves bytes), process or GPU-memory snapshots restoring an initialised engine, warm standby replicas, and keeping models resident while swapping adapters instead of models.

Core Explain the role of headroom, forecasts and draining in scaling down safely.

Scaling down too aggressively leaves no spare capacity for the next increase, which will take a full cold start to meet; so the autoscaler keeps headroom based on expected growth during one cold start and the loss of a replica. Forecasts of daily patterns indicate when demand will fall, allowing gradual scale-down. Removing a replica must not cut active streams: the replica is drained, i.e. removed from the router's candidate set, allowed to finish in-flight requests (which may take minutes for long reasoning outputs), and only then shut down; its prefix caches are lost, so routing affinity must be updated. Hysteresis and minimum dwell times prevent oscillation between scaling up and down.

Design Walk through a capacity plan for an LLM product from demand to GPUs.

Model demand per request class over the day: arrival rates, input and output token distributions, prefix reuse. Convert to work: uncached prefill tokens/s and decode tokens/s per model. Choose and measure a replica configuration (precision, parallelism, colocated or disaggregated) at the latency targets, obtaining sustainable prefill and decode throughput per replica. Replicas needed = max over resources of work divided by per-replica throughput, divided by a target utilisation from the latency-versus-load curve (often 70–80% at peak), plus spares for failures and headroom for growth during one cold start. Then make it true in operation: routing that keeps load even and caches warm, autoscaling on forecasts with reactive backup, rate limits that bound tenants. Close the loop with cost per million tokens by model and tenant, and track the sensitive inputs, prefix-cache hit rate and output length, on dashboards, because a prompt-template change can multiply prefill work.
