Part IX · Production — Chapter 29

On-Device and Edge Inference

Not all inference happens in data centres. Laptops, phones, cars and embedded systems run language models for privacy, latency, offline use and cost, with one user, a few gigabytes of memory and a power budget measured in watts. This chapter applies the book's arithmetic to that regime: what bandwidth allows a single user, how llama.cpp, GGUF and MLX squeeze models onto consumer hardware, how quantisation, flash offloading and sparsity stretch the memory, and what power and heat do to sustained performance.

Why this chapter

In March 2023, days after Meta's LLaMA weights became available to researchers, Georgi Gerganov released llama.cpp, a dependency-free C++ implementation that ran a 7-billion-parameter model on a MacBook's CPU, quantised to 4 bits. Within weeks people were running language models on laptops, Raspberry Pis and phones, and a community grew around local inference. By 2025 every major operating-system vendor shipped on-device models: Apple's Apple Intelligence foundation model of about three billion parameters, Google's Gemini Nano and Gemma variants for Android, Microsoft's Phi models on Copilot+ PCs. Local applications such as Ollama and LM Studio made downloading and running an open model as easy as installing an app.

The reasons for this shift are practical rather than fashionable. Local inference keeps data on the device, which matters for personal, medical and corporate information. It removes network round trips, which matters for keyboards, voice assistants and anything interactive. It works offline. And its marginal cost is zero to the provider, since the user's hardware and electricity pay for it. The constraints are equally practical. There is a single user, so the batch is one and the economies of batching that dominate Parts IV to VIII disappear. Memory is small and shared with everything else the device is doing. Bandwidth is a fraction of a data-centre GPU's. Power is limited by a battery and heat by a fanless case. Every technique in this chapter is a response to one of these constraints, and most are applications of ideas developed earlier in the book, pushed to their extremes.

Learning objectives
  1. Compute the decode-speed ceiling and prefill speed of a model on a given device from its bandwidth and compute.
  2. Compare the memory systems of phones, laptops with unified memory and discrete consumer GPUs, and the role of NPUs.
  3. Describe llama.cpp, the GGUF format and k-quant block quantisation, and compute bits per weight.
  4. Describe Apple MLX and other on-device runtimes and what they optimise.
  5. Plan a model, precision and context to fit a device's memory budget, including KV cache.
  6. Explain flash offloading, activation sparsity and expert offloading for models larger than memory, and analyse power, thermal and battery limits.

29.1 The arithmetic of a single user

On a personal device, decode almost always runs at batch 1. The arithmetic intensity of a batch-1 decode step is about \(2/b_w\) FLOPs per weight byte (§6.3): 1 FLOP per byte in BF16, 4 at 4-bit. Every consumer device, from a phone to a gaming GPU, has a ridge point of tens to hundreds of FLOPs per byte, so batch-1 decode is deeply memory-bound everywhere, and its speed is set by memory bandwidth alone:

\[ \text{tokens per second}\;\le\;\frac{\beta}{N\,b_w+t\,s_{kv}}, \]

the ratio of bandwidth to the bytes read per token, weights plus cache. For short contexts the cache term is small and the ceiling is simply \(\beta/(Nb_w)\). An 8B model in 4-bit quantisation (about 4.5 bits per weight with scales) occupies about 4.5 GB; on a laptop with 120 GB/s of memory bandwidth its ceiling is about 27 tokens per second, on a high-end laptop with 546 GB/s about 120, and on a phone with 60 GB/s about 13.

Figure 29.1 plots the ceiling for four classes of device, showing only models that fit each device's memory. Two regularities stand out. Speed scales inversely with model size, so every doubling of parameters halves tokens per second, whatever the device; and memory capacity, not compute, decides which models are possible at all. A phone runs 1–3B models comfortably and an 8B model at the edge of usability; a base laptop runs up to about 14B; a laptop with 128 GB of fast unified memory runs a 70B model at about reading speed; a 24 GB gaming GPU runs a 32B model quickly but cannot hold a 70B one. The comparison with the data centre is instructive: an H100 reads 3.35 TB/s, so its batch-1 ceiling for the same 8B model is about 740 tokens per second, but it earns its keep by serving hundreds of users at once, which a personal device never needs to do.

Prefill is the exception to memory-boundedness. A prompt of hundreds or thousands of tokens is a large matrix product, compute-bound, and its speed is the device's achieved FLOP/s divided by \(2N\). Consumer GPUs and laptop GPUs deliver tens of TFLOP/s in 16-bit arithmetic and phones a few, so a 3B model on a phone at an effective 2 TFLOP/s prefills about 310 tokens per second: a 2,000-token prompt, such as a long email thread, takes six or seven seconds before the first token. On devices, TTFT for long prompts is often the more noticeable delay, which is why mobile runtimes put so much effort into running prefill on the NPU or GPU and why prompt caching (§10.6) matters as much on a phone as in a data centre.

Worked example — an 8B assistant on a laptop

Llama 3 8B at 4.5 bits per weight (4.5 GB) on a laptop with 120 GB/s unified memory and a GPU delivering about 10 TFLOP/s in FP16. Decode ceiling: \(120/4.5\approx27\) tokens/s; at 70% efficiency about 19, comfortably above reading speed. Cache: 128 KiB per token in FP16, so a 4k context adds 0.5 GB and reduces the ceiling by about 10%; an 8-bit cache halves that. Prefill: \(2\times8\times10^{9}\) FLOPs per token at 10 TFLOP/s is about 625 tokens/s, so a 1,500-token prompt takes about 2.4 s. Memory: 4.5 GB of weights plus 0.5 GB of cache plus runtime overhead, about 6 GB, which fits beside other applications on a 16 GB machine.

29.2 The hardware

Consumer devices fall into three architectural families, which differ mainly in their memory. Phones have a system-on-chip in which CPU cores, a GPU and a neural processing unit (NPU) share LPDDR memory, typically 8–16 GB in 2025 flagships with 50–80 GB/s of bandwidth; the operating system reserves much of it and limits how much any app may use. Laptops with unified memory, most prominently Apple's M-series, share one pool of LPDDR between CPU and GPU with far more bandwidth: about 100–120 GB/s in base chips, up to about 546 GB/s in the M4 Max and about 819 GB/s in the M3 Ultra, with capacities up to 128 GB in laptops and 512 GB in desktops. PCs with discrete GPUs separate a fast but small GPU memory (24 GB at about 1 TB/s on an RTX 4090, 32 GB at about 1.8 TB/s on an RTX 5090) from a larger, slower system memory connected over PCIe.

Figure 29.2 sets the three side by side. Unified memory explains Apple Silicon's popularity for local LLMs: a 128 GB laptop can hold a 70B model in 4 bits and read it at over 500 GB/s, which no discrete consumer GPU can match in capacity. A discrete GPU is faster when the model fits in its memory, but a model that spills into system memory must cross PCIe at a few tens of GB/s and slows dramatically, so on PCs the choice of model is dictated by the GPU's memory size.

NPUs deserve a careful word. Phone and laptop vendors advertise NPU throughput in TOPS, trillions of low-precision operations per second, typically 35–50 TOPS in INT8 for 2024–25 flagship chips. These numbers matter for prefill and for vision models, which are compute-bound, but they do not raise the decode ceiling, which is set by the memory the NPU shares with everything else. NPUs also impose constraints that GPUs do not: they favour static shapes and particular quantisation formats, their compilers support a limited set of operators, and dynamic workloads such as variable-length attention with a growing cache map onto them awkwardly. In practice many on-device runtimes run prefill on the NPU or GPU and decode on whichever unit has the most efficient access to memory, and the most important specification of an AI-capable phone is its memory bandwidth and capacity, not its TOPS.

29.3 llama.cpp and GGUF

llama.cpp[1] began as a single file of C++ that implemented the LLaMA forward pass on the CPU with hand-written SIMD kernels and 4-bit weights. It grew into the most widely used local inference engine, built on the ggml tensor library, with back ends for x86 and ARM CPUs, Apple Metal, CUDA, Vulkan, SYCL and others, and support for hundreds of model architectures. Its design priorities differ from those of data-centre engines: minimal dependencies, a single portable binary, low memory overhead, memory-mapped model files that load instantly and are shared between processes, and good performance at batch 1 on whatever hardware the user has. It also includes a server with an OpenAI-compatible API and parallel "slots" for a few concurrent users, which is how applications such as Ollama and LM Studio expose it.

In August 2023 the project introduced GGUF, a single-file format for models. A GGUF file contains a header, a key–value metadata section describing the architecture, hyperparameters and tokenizer (vocabulary, merges, special tokens, chat template), a table of tensor descriptors (name, shape, quantisation type, offset), and the aligned tensor data itself. Everything needed to run the model is in one file, which can be memory-mapped so that the operating system pages weights in on demand and shares them between processes.

Figure 29.3 shows the layout. The format's quantisation types encode the block scaling of §14.2. The early types were simple: Q4_0 stores blocks of 32 weights as 4-bit integers with one FP16 scale, \(4+16/32=4.5\) bits per weight; Q8_0 the same with 8-bit integers. The k-quants, added in mid-2023, use two-level blocking: a super-block of 256 weights is divided into 16 blocks of 16 (or 8 of 32), each block has its own low-precision scale (and minimum, for asymmetric types), and the super-block has full-precision scales for those scales. This captures local variation in magnitude, as small groups do, while spending few bits on scales. Q4_K, for example, uses 4-bit weights with 6-bit block scales and minimums and FP16 super-block scales, about 4.5 bits per weight; Q2_K to Q6_K span roughly 2.6 to 6.6 bits.

Two refinements matter for quality. Mixed-precision presets such as Q4_K_M keep the most sensitive tensors, typically parts of the attention and the FFN down-projection, at a higher type, giving an average of about 4.8 bits per weight with noticeably better quality than uniform 4-bit. And an importance matrix (imatrix), computed by running calibration text through the model and recording the squared activations reaching each weight, weights the quantisation error by how much each weight matters, the same activation-aware idea as AWQ (§14.7). With these, 4- to 5-bit GGUF models are close to their 16-bit originals on most tasks, and 3-bit models usable for many.

Worked example — bits per weight of a k-quant

Consider a 4-bit k-quant super-block of 256 weights divided into 8 blocks of 32, each block with a 6-bit scale and a 6-bit minimum, and the super-block with an FP16 scale for the scales and an FP16 scale for the minimums. Bits: weights \(256\times4=1024\); block scales and minimums \(8\times(6+6)=96\); super-block scales \(2\times16=32\). Total 1,152 bits for 256 weights, exactly 4.5 bits per weight. A plain Q4_0 block (32 weights with one FP16 scale) also costs 4.5 bits, but has no minimum and only one level of scaling; the k-quant spends the same bits more cleverly, with asymmetric ranges and finer scales, and its error is correspondingly lower.

29.4 MLX and other on-device runtimes

Apple's MLX (December 2023)[2] is an array framework designed for Apple Silicon's unified memory. Arrays live in memory shared by CPU and GPU, so operations can run on either without copies; computation is lazy, building a graph that is evaluated when results are needed, which allows fusion; and its API resembles NumPy and PyTorch. The companion MLX-LM package runs and fine-tunes Hugging Face models with quantised weights, and on high-bandwidth Macs it approaches the bandwidth ceiling of Figure 29.1. MLX made the Mac a common platform for local experimentation with large models, including 4-bit 70B models on 128 GB laptops and quantised versions of very large MoE models on Mac Studios with 512 GB of memory.

Other runtimes target other niches. ExecuTorch, from the PyTorch project, compiles models ahead of time for mobile and embedded deployment, with delegates that hand subgraphs to vendor NPUs (Qualcomm, MediaTek, Apple Core ML) and a small runtime suitable for apps. MLC-LLM uses the TVM compiler to generate kernels for many back ends, including WebGPU, which lets models run inside a web browser. Vendor stacks, such as Qualcomm's AI Engine and Apple's Core ML, provide the most efficient path to their own NPUs but require models converted to their formats. And Ollama and LM Studio wrap llama.cpp (and MLX) in model management, a local API and a user interface.

All of these share the concerns of the data-centre engines, kernels, quantisation, caching, scheduling, but weighted differently. Start-up time and memory overhead matter more, because a phone app may load a model on demand and must coexist with others. Portability matters more, because the hardware is diverse. Batch sizes are one or a few, so continuous batching is irrelevant but prompt caching is not. And energy, discussed in §29.7, becomes a first-class metric.

29.5 Fitting the model: memory budgets

On a device, the model, its cache, the runtime and everything else the user is running must fit in memory together, and the operating system enforces limits: a phone app may be allowed only a few gigabytes even on a 12 GB phone, and a laptop user expects their browser and editor to keep working. Planning therefore starts from a memory budget.

Figure 29.5 lines up weight memory against three typical limits. On phones, the practical sizes are 1–4B parameters at 4 bits, which is why vendors' on-device models cluster there, and why their training emphasises distillation from larger models (§15.7): a well-distilled 3B model is far more useful than a crudely quantised 8B one that barely fits. Apple described its 2024 on-device model as about three billion parameters compressed with a mixed 2-bit and 4-bit scheme averaging under 4 bits per weight, with low-rank adapters, swapped in for different tasks, recovering the quality lost to compression, a phone-scale version of the multi-LoRA serving of §27.4.

The KV cache must fit too, and on devices it is often the factor that limits context length. Llama 3.2 3B, with 28 layers and 8 key–value heads of dimension 128, stores 112 KiB per token in 16-bit precision: a 4k context needs about 460 MB and a 32k context 3.7 GB, more than the model itself. On-device runtimes therefore quantise the cache to 8 or 4 bits (§8.6), cap the context at what the product needs, and reuse cached prefixes such as system prompts across turns. Runtime overhead, scratch buffers for activations and the operating system's own needs add the rest; a useful rule of thumb is to keep the model's weights under about half of the memory the application can actually obtain.

Drill 29.1 — Plan a phone deployment

An app may use about 4 GB on a phone with 60 GB/s of bandwidth. Choose between Llama 3.2 3B at 4.5 bits and Llama 3 8B at 3 bits, for 4k-token conversations. Estimate memory and decode speed for each, and say which you would pick.

Solution. 3B at 4.5 bits: weights about 1.8 GB (3.2B parameters), cache at 4k with an 8-bit cache about 230 MB, total with overhead about 2.5 GB; decode ceiling \(60/(1.8+0.23)\approx30\) tokens/s, perhaps 20 in practice. 8B at 3 bits: weights about 3.0 GB, cache (128 KiB per token at 16-bit, 64 KiB at 8-bit) about 270 MB at 4k, total about 3.7 GB, at the limit; decode ceiling \(60/3.3\approx18\) tokens/s, perhaps 12 in practice. The 3B model leaves headroom, runs faster and, at 4.5 bits, loses little to quantisation; the 8B model at 3 bits risks memory pressure and is noticeably degraded by 3-bit quantisation. Unless the task needs the 8B model's knowledge, choose the 3B model, ideally a version distilled for the app's task.

29.6 Larger than memory: flash, sparsity and experts

When a model does not fit in DRAM, the next tier is flash storage, which on phones and laptops reads at a few GB/s, tens to hundreds of times slower than DRAM. Streaming all weights from flash for every token would take seconds per token. Apple's "LLM in a flash" (Alizadeh et al., 2023)[3] showed how to run models up to twice the size of available DRAM by reading from flash only what each token needs. It relies on the activation sparsity of FFN layers (§15.8): with a predictor, only the FFN neurons likely to be active for the current token are loaded. Two further ideas make flash reads efficient: windowing keeps the neurons used by the last few tokens in DRAM, since consecutive tokens activate overlapping sets, so only the difference is loaded; and row–column bundling stores the up-projection row and down-projection column of each neuron contiguously, so that one larger read fetches both, matching flash's preference for large sequential reads. The paper reported speed-ups of several times on CPU and over twenty times on GPU compared with naive loading.

Figure 29.6 summarises the scheme. Its effectiveness depends on activation sparsity, which, as §15.8 showed, is high for ReLU-style models and lower for SwiGLU models unless they are trained or thresholded for it, and on batch size, which on a device is one, exactly the regime where contextual sparsity pays. PowerInfer-2 (2024) applied related ideas to smartphones, splitting "hot" and "cold" neurons between DRAM and flash and pipelining flash reads with computation. Mixture-of-experts models fit the same pattern even more naturally: at batch 1 each token uses only \(k_r\) experts per layer (§19.4), so the attention and shared weights can stay in fast memory while experts are fetched on demand from slower memory or flash, with caching of recently used experts. This is how quantised MoE models with tens or hundreds of billions of total parameters run, slowly but usefully, on workstations and high-memory laptops, and why models such as gpt-oss-20b, released with 4-bit MoE weights, were designed to fit in about 16 GB.

Worked example — an MoE model larger than memory

Setup. A laptop has 16 GB of unified memory at 120 GB/s, of which about 10 GB can be given to a model, and an SSD that reads at about 5 GB/s. The model is a 30B-parameter MoE with 3B active parameters per token, quantised to 4.5 bits per weight: 17 GB in total, 1.7 GB read per token.

If everything fitted. Decode would read 1.7 GB per token from DRAM, a ceiling of \(120/1.7\approx70\) tokens per second, far faster than a dense 8B model (§29.1). That is the attraction of MoE on devices.

With experts streamed from flash. Attention, embeddings and the most popular experts stay in DRAM; the rest are read from the SSD when routed to. If a fraction \(m\) of the active expert bytes miss the DRAM cache, each token costs \(1.7/120\) s plus \(1.7m/5\) s. With \(m=0.05\) that is 14 + 17 ms, about 32 tokens per second; with \(m=0.2\), 14 + 68 ms, 12 tokens per second; with \(m=0.5\), under 6.

Consequence. The SSD is 24 times slower than DRAM, so the hit rate must exceed about 96% before flash stops dominating. That is why on-device MoE work concentrates on predicting and caching experts (exploiting the fact that consecutive tokens tend to reuse experts) and on reading in large contiguous chunks, as §29.6 described for dense models.

29.7 Power, heat and battery

On a mains-powered GPU, energy is a cost; on a phone, it is a hard limit twice over. The battery holds a fixed amount of energy, typically 15–20 watt-hours, and the case can dissipate only a few watts continuously without becoming uncomfortable or throttling. Both limits translate into token budgets. If decoding a 3B model draws about 5 W at 20 tokens per second, each token costs 0.25 J; a 15 Wh battery holds 54 kJ, enough for about 200,000 tokens if the model did nothing else, a fraction of a day's heavy use. Energy per token falls with smaller and more aggressively quantised models, since fewer bytes are moved, and with efficient processors: NPUs usually deliver more operations per joule than GPUs, which deliver more than CPUs, which is the strongest argument for NPU offload even in memory-bound decode.

Heat imposes a second, time-dependent limit. A phone or fanless laptop can run at peak power for seconds to a minute, after which its temperature rises and the system lowers clocks to stay within its thermal envelope. Benchmarks that run for ten seconds therefore overstate sustained performance; a fair on-device benchmark runs long enough to reach thermal steady state and reports both burst and sustained tokens per second. For memory-bound decode the effect is softer than for compute-bound work, since lower clocks hurt bandwidth-limited work less, but prefill, which is compute-bound, slows noticeably under throttling, lengthening TTFT for long prompts.

Drill 29.2 — Tokens per battery

A phone with a 16 Wh battery runs a 3B model whose decode draws 4 W at 25 tokens/s and whose prefill draws 8 W at 300 tokens/s. A typical interaction has a 1,500-token prompt and a 300-token reply. How many interactions does a full battery support, ignoring everything else?

Solution. Prefill: 1,500 tokens at 300 tokens/s takes 5 s at 8 W, 40 J. Decode: 300 tokens at 25 tokens/s takes 12 s at 4 W, 48 J. Each interaction costs about 88 J. The battery holds \(16\times3600=57{,}600\) J, so about 650 interactions, or a few hours of continuous use. Prefill is nearly half of the energy; caching the system prompt and conversation prefix between turns would cut it substantially, and an NPU running prefill more efficiently would help further.

29.8 Small models, speculation and hybrid inference

The data-centre techniques that do not depend on batching transfer to devices, and some become more valuable. Speculative decoding (Chapter 20) is ideal at batch 1, where the target model leaves almost all compute idle: a tiny draft model, or n-gram lookup on the user's own text, proposes tokens that the main model verifies, raising tokens per second at the same bandwidth. llama.cpp and MLX both support draft models, and speculative gains on devices of 1.5–2.5× are common for predictable text. Prompt caching saves prefill time and energy across turns of a conversation. Distilled small models (§15.7) make the most of the parameters a device can hold.

The last idea is not to choose between device and cloud at all. Hybrid inference runs what it can locally, for privacy, latency and cost, and sends to a server what the local model cannot handle well: a long document, a hard reasoning question, a request for knowledge the small model lacks. The routing decision is the cascade of §27.6 with a twist, since the "small model" is free and private and the "large model" costs money and sends data off the device; Apple's design of on-device models backed by server models running on its own hardware is an example. Designing such a system means deciding which requests may leave the device, how to detect that the local answer is inadequate, and how to keep the experience seamless when the network is slow or absent.

29.9 Beyond phones and laptops: browsers and small servers

Two further edge settings complete the picture. The first is the web browser. WebGPU, a browser API that exposes the device's GPU to web pages, lets a model run inside a tab with no installation: MLC's WebLLM and similar projects compile models to WebGPU shaders and download quantised weights into the browser's storage. The arithmetic is that of the underlying device, bandwidth over bytes per token, with extra costs: the first visit downloads gigabytes of weights, shader compilation adds start-up time, and browsers limit how much memory a page may allocate. In return, a web application can offer private, offline-capable inference to any visitor with a capable GPU, which suits small models for tasks such as summarising a page or drafting text in a form.

The second is the small shared server: an office workstation, a home server or an industrial edge box that serves a handful of users or devices on a local network. Here the batch is no longer one, and the data-centre ideas return in miniature. llama.cpp's server divides its context into a few parallel slots, each serving one conversation, and batches their decode steps together; with \(B\) active slots, the weights are read once for all of them, so total throughput rises almost linearly with \(B\) while each user's speed falls only slightly, exactly the batching argument of §6.6. The KV cache, divided among the slots, becomes the limit, so the same planning applies: memory for weights, memory per slot for its context, and a cap on concurrent users. For a team of a dozen people, one high-memory workstation running a 30–70B model with a few slots can replace a cloud subscription, at the cost of operating it.

Drill 29.3 — A workstation serving a small team

A workstation with 128 GB of unified memory at 546 GB/s runs a 32B model at 4.5 bits (18 GB) with 8-bit KV caches of 128 KiB per token. The team wants 8 parallel slots with 16k contexts. Does it fit, and what are the per-user and total decode speeds when all slots are active at 8k average context?

Solution. Cache per slot at 16k: \(16{,}384\times128\) KiB ≈ 2.1 GB, so 8 slots need about 17 GB, plus 18 GB of weights and some overhead: about 40 GB, well within 128 GB. With all 8 slots at 8k average context, each step reads 18 GB of weights plus \(8\times8192\times128\) KiB ≈ 8.6 GB of cache, about 26.6 GB, so a step takes about \(26.6/546\approx49\) ms at the ceiling. Each user sees about 20 tokens/s and the team gets about 160 tokens/s in total, against about 30 tokens/s for a single user alone (\(546/18\)). Batching even a few users multiplies total throughput while keeping each above reading speed.

29.10 Shipping models to devices

Deployment to devices raises engineering problems that data centres do not have. Models must be distributed to millions of devices, so their size matters for download time and storage, which favours shipping one base model with small task adapters, as Apple does, and updating adapters rather than whole models. Devices are heterogeneous: different chips, memory sizes and operating-system versions, so a deployment ships several variants (precision, size) and selects one per device, with fallbacks when memory is short. Models must coexist with other applications: the operating system may evict a model's memory when the user switches apps, so runtimes must reload quickly, which memory-mapped formats help with. And evaluation must happen on real devices, since quantisation kernels, NPU compilers and thermal behaviour differ from the development machine: a model that is accurate and fast on a workstation can be slow or subtly different on a phone's NPU. The measurement discipline of Chapter 28, paired quality checks against the reference and sustained-performance benchmarks, applies with device-specific additions such as memory-pressure and battery tests.

Looking ahead

This chapter completes the technical tour of the book. We have followed a token from the transformer's arithmetic, through the GPU's memory hierarchy and kernels, through caches, schedulers, engines and distributed systems, through compression and new architectures, to fleets in data centres and to a single phone in a pocket. The final chapter turns that material to a specific use: the ML-systems design interview, in which a candidate must reason aloud about exactly these trade-offs under time pressure. Chapter 30 presents a framework for such interviews, worked case studies with model answers, and the rubrics by which they are judged.

Chapter summary

  • llama.cpp/ggml: portable, minimal, memory-mapped, batch-1-optimised, many back ends, OpenAI-compatible server (Ollama, LM Studio). GGUF: self-describing single file with tokenizer and chat template. k-quants: super-blocks of 256 with per-block scales (Q4_K ≈ 4.5 bpw); mixed presets (Q4_K_M ≈ 4.8) and importance matrices preserve quality.
  • MLX exploits unified memory with lazy, fused arrays; ExecuTorch, MLC-LLM (WebGPU), Core ML and vendor NPU stacks serve other niches.
  • Memory budgets: phone apps get a few GB, so 1–4B models at 4 bits, distilled (Apple's ≈ 3B model at under 4 bits with adapters). The KV cache often limits context (Llama 3.2 3B: 112 KiB/token); quantise it and cap context.
  • Larger than memory: LLM in a flash (activation-sparsity prediction, windowing, row–column bundling), PowerInfer-2, MoE expert offloading at batch 1.
  • Power and heat: energy per token (≈ 0.25 J for a 3B model on a phone) bounds tokens per battery; thermal throttling separates burst from sustained performance; NPUs are most efficient per joule.
  • Browsers (WebGPU) and small shared servers extend the edge: the latter batch a few users in parallel slots, multiplying total throughput at a small cost per user, with KV memory per slot as the limit. Shipping to devices requires variants, adapters and on-device validation.

Warm-up Why does local LLM inference run at batch 1, and what does that imply for performance?

A personal device serves one user, so there is usually one sequence decoding at a time. Batch-1 decode reads all weights (and the cache) for each token while doing about 2 FLOPs per weight, an intensity of ~\(2/b_w\) FLOPs/byte, far below any device's ridge point, so speed is set by memory bandwidth: tokens/s ≤ \(\beta/(Nb_w+ts_{kv})\). Implications: memory bandwidth and capacity matter more than TOPS; quantisation (fewer bytes per weight) directly raises speed; batching tricks are irrelevant but speculative decoding (using idle compute) and prompt caching help; and prefill, being compute-bound, sets TTFT for long prompts.

Warm-up What is GGUF and why was it introduced?

GGUF is llama.cpp's single-file model format (August 2023, replacing GGML formats). It contains a header, key–value metadata (architecture, hyperparameters, RoPE settings, tokenizer vocabulary and merges, special tokens, chat template, quantisation info), a tensor table (names, shapes, quantisation types, offsets) and aligned tensor data. It is self-describing (one file carries everything needed to run the model, so tools need no separate config or tokenizer files), extensible (new metadata keys without breaking readers), supports per-tensor quantisation types, and is memory-mappable, so loading is near-instant and weights are paged in on demand and shared across processes.

Core Estimate decode speed and TTFT for a 3B model on a phone.

Weights: 3.2B parameters at 4.5 bits ≈ 1.8 GB; cache 112 KiB/token at 16-bit (56 KiB at 8-bit), so ~0.23 GB at 4k with an 8-bit cache. Bandwidth ~60 GB/s: decode ceiling ≈ 60/2.0 ≈ 30 tokens/s, perhaps 20 in practice. Prefill: 2 × 3.2e9 ≈ 6.4 GFLOPs per token; at an effective 2 TFLOP/s on the GPU/NPU ≈ 310 tokens/s, so a 1,500-token prompt takes ~5 s before the first token; caching the system prompt and earlier turns removes most of that on subsequent turns. Energy: at ~4 W decode ≈ 0.16–0.2 J per token. Sustained performance may be lower after thermal throttling.

Core Explain k-quant block quantisation in llama.cpp.

Weights are grouped into super-blocks of 256, each divided into smaller blocks (16 of 16 or 8 of 32). Each small block stores its weights as low-bit integers and has its own low-precision scale (and, in asymmetric types, a minimum), e.g. 6-bit codes; the super-block stores FP16 'scale of scales' (and of minimums) that turn those codes into real scales. Reconstruction: weight ≈ d·s_b·q − m·m_b. This captures local variation in magnitude like small groups do while spending few bits on scales: Q4_K costs ~4.5 bits per weight (e.g. 1,024 + 96 + 32 bits per 256 weights), with lower error than Q4_0 at the same size. Types span Q2_K to Q6_K (~2.6–6.6 bpw); mixed presets (Q4_K_M) keep sensitive tensors at higher precision; an importance matrix weights errors by activation statistics.

Core Compare unified-memory laptops and discrete GPUs for local LLMs.

Unified memory (Apple M-series): CPU and GPU share one LPDDR pool with large capacity (up to 128 GB in laptops, 512 GB in desktops) and high bandwidth (120–819 GB/s), so large models (70B at 4–8 bits, quantised MoE models) fit and decode at useful speeds, with no copies between processors. Discrete GPUs (RTX 4090/5090): very high bandwidth (1–1.8 TB/s) and strong compute, so models that fit in 24–32 GB decode and prefill faster, but anything larger spills to system memory over PCIe (tens of GB/s) and slows dramatically. Choice: discrete GPU for fast small-to-medium models (≤ ~32B quantised), unified memory for large models and long contexts; multi-GPU PCs extend capacity with communication costs.

Core How does LLM in a flash run models larger than DRAM?

It keeps always-needed parts (attention, embeddings) in DRAM and stores FFN weights in flash, loading only what each token needs. A sparsity predictor estimates which FFN neurons will be active for the current token (exploiting ReLU-style activation sparsity). Windowing keeps the neurons used by the last few tokens in DRAM, since consecutive tokens activate overlapping sets, so only new neurons are read. Row–column bundling stores each neuron's up-projection row and down-projection column contiguously, turning two small reads into one larger, flash-friendly read. Together these allowed models up to twice DRAM size with several-fold (CPU) to over 20× (GPU) speed-ups versus naive loading. It works at batch 1, where contextual sparsity is high.

Core What limits does a phone’s battery and thermal envelope impose?

Battery: a 15–20 Wh battery holds 54–72 kJ; at ~0.2–0.25 J per decoded token for a 3B model (plus prefill energy, often comparable per interaction), that is a few hundred thousand tokens or several hundred interactions per charge if the model did nothing else, so energy per token is a product constraint. Thermal: sustained power is limited to a few watts; after seconds to a minute at peak, the device throttles clocks, reducing prefill speed especially (compute-bound) and decode less (bandwidth-bound). Mitigations: smaller, more quantised models; NPU execution (best energy per operation); prompt caching; speculative decoding (fewer target-model passes per token); and benchmarking at thermal steady state.

Deep-dive Why do NPU TOPS not translate into faster token generation?

Decode at batch 1 performs ~2 FLOPs per weight read, so its speed is bandwidth-limited: tokens/s ≈ bandwidth / bytes per token. The NPU shares the SoC's LPDDR (50–80 GB/s on phones) with the CPU and GPU, so it reads weights no faster than they do; tens of TOPS of compute sit idle. NPUs help compute-bound phases (prefill, vision encoders, batch processing) and are more energy-efficient per operation, so using them for decode can still save battery even at the same speed. Practical issues compound this: NPUs prefer static shapes and specific quantisation formats, and dynamic attention over a growing KV cache maps poorly to them, so many runtimes run decode on the GPU or CPU and prefill on the NPU.

Deep-dive How should an on-device model’s precision, size and context be chosen?

Start from the memory the app can actually use (e.g. ~4 GB on a phone), reserve headroom for the runtime and OS, and budget weights + KV cache. Prefer a smaller, distilled model at moderate precision (4–5 bits) over a larger model at very low precision, since quality falls steeply below ~3–4 bits and memory pressure causes failures. Size the KV cache from the needed context with an 8-bit or 4-bit cache, and cap context; use prompt caching across turns. Check speed: tokens/s ≈ bandwidth / (weights + cache bytes) against the product's needs (≥ ~10 tokens/s for chat), TTFT from prefill compute. Measure quality on the product's tasks after quantisation (paired evaluation), and energy per interaction. Consider task adapters (LoRA) to recover quality, as Apple did.

Deep-dive How can very large MoE models run on a high-memory workstation?

At batch 1 each token uses only \(k_r\) experts per layer (8 of 256 for DeepSeek-V3), i.e. the active parameters (~37B) rather than the total (671B). Quantise experts aggressively (2–4 bits) to shrink the total to a few hundred GB, keep attention, shared experts and embeddings resident in fast memory, and place experts in unified memory (e.g. 512 GB Mac Studio) or in system RAM/flash with on-demand loading and an LRU cache of recently used experts (locality across tokens helps). Decode speed ≈ bandwidth / bytes of active parameters per token (~20 GB at 4 bits for V3, so ~40 tokens/s at 800 GB/s ideal, less with expert misses). Prefill is slow (compute-bound on all routed experts). This enables useful local use of frontier-scale MoE models at reading speed.

Deep-dive When is speculative decoding most valuable on a device, and what limits it?

At batch 1 the target model's decode leaves nearly all compute idle, so verifying several drafted tokens costs about one forward pass: speedup ≈ \(\mathbb E[n_{\text{acc}}]/(1+\gamma c_d)\). It is most valuable for predictable text (code, structured output, edits) and with very cheap drafts: a tiny draft model sharing the tokenizer, n-gram/prompt lookup on the user's own documents, or MTP/EAGLE heads if the model has them. Limits: the draft model must fit in memory too (and its bytes per token count against bandwidth, raising \(c_d\)); acceptance falls at high temperature; and thermal limits can reduce the spare compute. Typical device gains are 1.5–2.5×.

Design Design an on-device writing assistant for phones.

Model: a 2–3B model distilled from a larger one for rewriting, summarising and replying, with task-specific LoRA adapters; 4-bit weights (k-quant or vendor format) with sensitive tensors at higher precision; 8-bit KV cache; context capped at 4–8k. Runtime: ExecuTorch/Core ML or llama.cpp/MLX with NPU or GPU prefill and efficient decode; prompt caching of system prompts and conversation prefixes; speculative decoding with prompt lookup (rewrites copy from input) or a tiny draft. Hybrid: escalate long documents or hard requests to a server model with user consent. Design A team wants to run a 70B model locally for internal code assistance. Recommend hardware and configuration.

At 4.5 bits a 70B model is ~40 GB, plus cache (320 KiB/token at 16-bit; 8-bit halves it; 16k context ≈ 2.6 GB at 8-bit). Options: a 128 GB unified-memory laptop or desktop (546–819 GB/s) runs it at ~10–20 tokens/s at batch 1, adequate for chat-style use, with plenty of room for long contexts; or two to four 24–32 GB GPUs with layer splitting or tensor parallelism over PCIe, faster per token but more complex. Use Q4_K_M or similar with an importance matrix, prompt caching for repository context, speculative decoding with a small same-family draft model (code is predictable), and an OpenAI-compatible local server (llama.cpp, Ollama, MLX) for IDE integration. Validate code-task quality versus the full-precision model; consider a distilled 14–32B coding model if speed matters more.

Core What is MLX and why does unified memory matter for it?

MLX is Apple's array framework for Apple Silicon: arrays live in unified memory accessible by CPU and GPU without copies; operations are lazily evaluated, building graphs that can be fused and executed when needed; the API mirrors NumPy/PyTorch, with automatic differentiation for fine-tuning. Unified memory removes the host–device transfer that dominates discrete-GPU workflows and lets models as large as the system memory (up to 128–512 GB) run on the GPU at the chip's full bandwidth. MLX-LM provides quantised inference and LoRA fine-tuning for Hugging Face models, reaching a good fraction of the bandwidth ceiling.

Core Why does the KV cache often limit context length on devices?

The cache grows linearly with context: \(2Ln_{kv}d_hb_{kv}\) bytes per token, e.g. 112 KiB for Llama 3.2 3B at 16-bit, so 32k tokens need ~3.7 GB, more than the 4-bit weights (~1.8 GB) and beyond a phone app's budget. It also adds bytes read per decode step, lowering tokens/s at long contexts. Remedies: quantise the cache (8-bit or 4-bit), cap context at what the product needs, use models with efficient attention (GQA, sliding windows, MLA), evict or summarise old context, and reuse cached prefixes across turns rather than recomputing.

Core How does a small multi-user edge server (e.g. llama.cpp with parallel slots) change the performance picture compared with a single user?

With \(B\) active conversations batched into each decode step, the weights are read once per step for all of them, so total throughput rises nearly linearly with \(B\) while each user's speed falls only by the added cache bytes per step: step time ≈ (weights + \(B\times\) context × \(s_{kv}\)) / bandwidth. For a 32B 4.5-bit model (18 GB) on a 546 GB/s workstation, one user gets ~30 tokens/s; eight users at 8k context get ~20 tokens/s each, ~160 in total. The limits become KV memory per slot (context length × bytes per token × number of slots) and, for long prompts, prefill compute, which is shared among users and can stall others' decoding unless chunked. The planning is the data-centre planning of Chapters 6 and 10 in miniature: weights plus per-slot cache must fit, and the number of slots trades per-user speed against total throughput.

Design What are the trade-offs of running a model in the browser with WebGPU?

Advantages: no installation, works on any device with a capable GPU and a modern browser, data stays on the user's machine, offline use after the first load, and zero serving cost to the provider. Costs: the first visit must download the quantised weights (hundreds of MB to several GB), so only small models are practical and caching in browser storage matters; WebGPU shader compilation adds start-up latency; browsers cap memory per page and per GPU buffer, limiting model and context size; performance is a fraction of native runtimes because kernels are generic and some hardware features (specific tensor-core paths, low-bit formats) are not exposed; and capability varies widely across users' devices, so the application needs fallbacks (a smaller model, or a server). Good fits: summarising or rewriting page content, form assistance, private document tools with 1–3B models.

Deep-dive What engineering problems arise when shipping models to millions of devices?

Distribution: model downloads consume bandwidth and storage, so ship one base model with small task adapters and update adapters rather than whole models; use delta updates. Heterogeneity: chips, NPUs, memory sizes and OS versions differ, so build several variants (sizes, precisions, NPU-compiled versions) and select per device, with fallbacks under memory pressure. Coexistence: the OS can evict an app's memory, so models must reload quickly (memory-mapped formats) and the app must handle eviction gracefully. Validation: quantisation kernels, NPU compilers and thermal behaviour differ per device, so run quality (paired against a reference) and sustained-performance, memory-pressure and battery tests on real devices in CI. Privacy and safety: on-device outputs cannot be filtered server-side, so safety measures must be built into the model or local pipeline. Telemetry is limited by privacy, so quality monitoring relies on opt-in signals and offline evaluation.
