Part IX · Production — Chapter 30

The ML-Systems Design Interview

The final chapter turns the book into a method for one of its intended uses: the system-design interview for ML-systems, inference and AI-infrastructure roles. It explains what interviewers assess, gives a framework for structuring an answer, collects the estimates every candidate should be able to make in their head, and works five case studies end to end, from a chat API to a trillion-parameter MoE deployment, with the follow-up questions interviewers ask and the rubric they use.

Why this chapter

A system-design interview for an inference role is a compressed version of the work this book describes. In forty-five to sixty minutes, a candidate is given an open problem, "design the serving system for a chat assistant used by ten million people", "how would you serve DeepSeek-V3 at the lowest cost per token?", "our long-document feature is too slow; fix it", and must clarify what is being asked, estimate the resources, propose an architecture, defend its trade-offs and adapt as the interviewer changes the constraints. There is rarely one right answer. What distinguishes strong candidates is the ability to reason quantitatively from first principles, to know which of the many techniques in this book matter for the problem at hand, and to explain their reasoning clearly enough that the interviewer can follow it.

The previous twenty-five chapters supply the knowledge; this chapter is about using it under time pressure. We begin with what interviewers assess and a six-step framework that keeps an answer organised. We then collect the back-of-envelope estimates, model memory, KV cache, step times, prefill times, GPU counts and costs, that should come as fluently as arithmetic. The heart of the chapter is five worked case studies, each solved in the order a candidate would speak, with the numbers, the decisions and the follow-ups. We close with the common failure modes and how answers are graded across seniority levels.

Learning objectives
  1. Explain what ML-systems design interviews assess and how answers are graded.
  2. Apply a six-step framework: requirements, estimation, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs, operations.
  3. Perform the standard back-of-envelope estimates for memory, latency, throughput, GPUs and cost from memory.
  4. Work through design problems for chat serving, long-document QA, large-MoE serving, low-latency code completion and offline batch inference.
  5. Anticipate follow-up questions and avoid the common failure modes.

Uses: the whole book, especially Chapters 3, 6, 10–13, 14, 18–21 and 26–28.

30.1 What interviewers assess

Inference and ML-systems design interviews at AI labs and large technology companies vary in format, but they assess a consistent set of abilities. Problem framing: does the candidate ask about the requirements that change the design (traffic, latency targets, model quality needs, cost constraints) rather than assuming them? Quantitative reasoning: can they estimate memory, bandwidth, compute and cost, quickly and roughly right, and use the estimates to make decisions? Breadth: do they know the space of techniques, batching, caching, quantisation, parallelism, disaggregation, speculation, and when each applies? Depth: can they go deep on the bottleneck when asked, for example explaining why decode is memory-bound and what that implies for batching and quantisation? Trade-offs: do they compare alternatives and state what they give up? Operations: do they think about failures, monitoring, rollout and cost over time? And communication: is the answer organised, are assumptions stated, and does the candidate adapt to hints?

A typical grading rubric for an inference system-design interview.

The weighting shifts with seniority. Entry-level candidates are expected to get the fundamentals right and reason soundly with guidance; senior candidates to drive the conversation, find the dominant constraint quickly and know production realities; staff-level candidates to reason about the whole system over time, including organisational and economic factors such as build-versus-buy, hardware roadmaps and how the design evolves as models change. In every case, numbers are the currency: an argument that "FP8 would help" is weak; an argument that "FP8 halves the bytes per decode step, so at our 20 ms target the batch per GPU roughly doubles and the fleet shrinks from about 750 to about 450 GPUs" is strong.

30.2 A framework for the answer

A good answer has a shape that the interviewer can follow, and the six steps in Figure 30.1 provide one. They are not a script to be recited but a checklist that prevents the common failures: designing before understanding the problem, never quantifying, or never discussing what could go wrong.

Requirements come first, because they determine everything else. For an inference system the essential questions are: which model, or what quality bar; how much traffic, at peak and on average, and in what shape (input and output lengths, prefix sharing, multi-turn sessions); what latency targets (TTFT and TPOT, or end-to-end for non-streaming), at which percentile; what cost target; and what constraints (hardware available, data residency, on-device). A candidate who asks "what are the typical prompt and output lengths?" has already shown that they know these drive the design. If the interviewer leaves numbers open, choose reasonable ones and state them.

Estimation turns requirements into resources, using the toolkit of §30.3. Architecture draws the request path, gateway, router, engines, caches, autoscaler, and names the engine-level choices: precision, parallelism, batching, chunked prefill, prefix caching, disaggregation. The deep dive picks the dominant bottleneck that the estimates revealed and explains it from first principles; this is where the depth of Parts I to III pays. Trade-offs compare at least one alternative for each major decision. Operations covers how the system is observed, how it fails, how it is changed and what it costs. Throughout, the candidate should think aloud, writing numbers down, so that the interviewer can follow and redirect.

Hardware numbers worth memorising are few. An H100 SXM has about 989 TFLOP/s of dense BF16 (1,979 in FP8), 3.35 TB/s of HBM bandwidth and 80 GB of memory; an H200 the same compute with 4.8 TB/s and 141 GB; a B200 roughly 2.25 PFLOP/s BF16 dense, 8 TB/s and 192 GB. NVLink on Hopper gives 450 GB/s per GPU per direction; the network about 50 GB/s per GPU; PCIe Gen5 about 64 GB/s. Achievable efficiencies are 60–80% of peak FLOP/s in large GEMMs and 70–90% of peak bandwidth in good decode kernels. With these, any estimate in this chapter can be made with a pencil, and in an interview it should be, aloud.

Worked example — the two-minute estimate

"How many H100s to serve Llama 3 70B to 1,000 requests per second, 500 output tokens each, with a 20 ms TPOT?" Decode work: \(1000\times500=5\times10^{5}\) tokens/s. Per-GPU throughput: FP8 weights, TP = 4, batch about 256 at 2k context gives roughly 14–15 ms per step and about 4,000 tokens/s per GPU (Chapter 18). GPUs: \(5\times10^{5}/4000\approx125\) at full use, about 180 at 70% utilisation, so roughly 45 four-GPU replicas, plus prefill capacity (absorbed by chunked prefill if prompts are short) and spares: "on the order of 200 H100s". Sanity check: 180 GPUs × $2.50/h ≈ $450/hour for \(1.8\times10^{9}\) tokens per hour, about 25 cents per million output tokens, a plausible number. The whole estimate takes two minutes and anchors the rest of the discussion.

Worked example — is $0.10 per million tokens for a 70B model plausible?

The claim. A provider offers Llama 3 70B at $0.10 per million output tokens. We check it against one 8×H100 server at $2.50 per GPU-hour, $20 per hour, using the estimation toolkit above: bytes per step, then tokens per step.

BF16, short contexts, large batch. At 1k context and batch 256, the cache is \(256\times1024\times320\) KiB ≈ 86 GB; with the 141 GB of weights, a step reads 227 GB in 10.6 ms at 80% of 26.8 TB/s. Compute is \(256\times141\) GFLOP ≈ 36 TFLOP, 9.1 ms at 50% MFU, just below the memory time. Throughput is \(256/0.0106\approx24{,}000\) tokens per second, \(24{,}000\times3600\approx87\)M tokens per hour, so the cost is \(20/87\approx\$0.23\) per million. That is not enough.

FP8 weights and cache. Both byte counts halve: 113 GB per step, 5.3 ms. Compute doubles in speed, so 36 TFLOP take 4.6 ms, still under the memory time. Throughput rises to about 48,000 tokens per second and the cost falls to about $0.12 per million.

Verdict. $0.10 is plausible only with FP8, short contexts, large and constantly full batches, cheaper GPU-hours than $2.50 or newer hardware, and little margin. Any of long contexts, low utilisation or a TPOT target below 10 ms breaks it. This is the shape of a good interview answer: a two-line computation that names exactly which assumptions the price depends on.

30.4 Case study: a chat assistant for ten million users

Prompt. "Design the serving system for a chat assistant with ten million daily active users."

Requirements. Ask, or assume and state: about 20 messages per user per day, so \(2\times10^{8}\) requests per day, an average of about 2,300 per second and a peak of perhaps three times that, 7,000 per second. Each request carries a system prompt and conversation history of about 1,000 tokens, of which about 600 are a shared or cached prefix, and produces about 300 output tokens. Targets: P99 TTFT under 1 s and P99 TPOT under 25 ms (comfortable streaming). Quality requires a 70B-class model. Cost should be well under a cent per request.

Estimates. Peak decode work is \(7000\times300=2.1\times10^{6}\) tokens/s. Peak uncached prefill is \(7000\times400=2.8\times10^{6}\) tokens/s, about \(2.8\times10^{6}\times1.4\times10^{11}\approx4\times10^{17}\) FLOP/s, which at about 1,200 effective FP8 TFLOP/s per GPU is about 330 GPUs' worth of compute, largely absorbable into decode steps' idle compute with chunked prefill. For decode, FP8 with TP = 4 at a 25 ms TPOT target allows large batches (about 512 per replica at 2k context), roughly 6,000 tokens/s per GPU. The chart shows the consequence for fleet size.

Figure 30.2 is the kind of plot to sketch in words during the interview: the configuration decisions are worth a factor of four in GPUs. At the stated peak, decode needs about 500 H100s with FP8 and TP = 4 at the 25 ms target, plus prefill capacity and spares: a peak fleet of roughly 700–800 GPUs, scaled down to perhaps 250 at night.

Architecture. A gateway with authentication, per-user rate limits and usage metering; a router that hashes conversation identifiers for session affinity, so that each turn's history is a prefix-cache hit on the replica that served the previous turn, with power-of-two load balancing among candidate replicas; FP8 TP = 4 replicas of a vLLM- or SGLang-style engine with paged KV, prefix caching, chunked prefill (token budget tuned against P99 TPOT) and an FP8 KV cache; a tiered KV store in host memory for idle conversations, so that a user returning after a few minutes still hits their cache; an autoscaler driven by outstanding tokens and KV usage with daily forecasts; and observability with TTFT and TPOT SLOs.

Figure 30.3 is the diagram to draw at this point.

Deep dive. The interviewer will usually probe decode: why is it memory-bound, why does FP8 help so much, what limits batch size? Answer with the roofline: each step reads the weights once and each sequence's cache; at 2k context and batch 512 the cache is the larger term, so the batch is limited by the TPOT target and by memory; FP8 halves both terms. Then prefix caching: with 600 of 1,000 input tokens cached, prefill work falls by 60%, which is why session affinity in the router matters as much as any kernel.

Trade-offs. Disaggregation would isolate TPOT from prefill bursts but costs transfer and complexity; with short prompts and a 25 ms target, colocated chunked prefill is probably sufficient (§13.6), revisited if long prompts grow. A smaller distilled model for easy queries with a router (§27.6) could halve cost. Speculative decoding would cut TPOT but competes with batching at these batch sizes and short contexts (§20.6), so it is better reserved for latency-sensitive tiers.

Operations. SLO burn-rate alerts; dashboards for prefix-hit rate, KV usage, preemptions and tokens per GPU; canary rollouts with paired quality evaluation; graceful degradation under overload (shorter max outputs, routing to a smaller model). Cost: average fleet of about 450 GPUs at $2.50/hour is about $27,000 per day for \(6\times10^{10}\) output tokens, about 45 cents per million output tokens, around 0.015 cents per request.

30.5 Case study: question answering over long documents

Prompt. "Users upload documents of up to 200k tokens and ask questions about them. TTFT is too slow. Design the system."

Requirements. Clarify: how many questions per document (many, typically, in a session); acceptable TTFT (say 5 s for the first question, 1 s for follow-ups); model (70B-class with 128k+ context); concurrency (hundreds of active documents). The key observation, to be made aloud, is that the cost is prefill of the document, and that the document is reused across questions.

Estimates. Prefill of a 200k-token document on a 70B model costs \(2Nn+2Ldn^2\approx2.8\times10^{16}+5.2\times10^{16}=8\times10^{16}\) FLOPs, attention dominating (the crossover for 70B is about 108k tokens). On one GPU at 600 TFLOP/s that is over two minutes; on eight GPUs with tensor parallelism, about 17 s; across 32 GPUs with context parallelism, about 5 s. The KV cache of the document is \(200{,}000\times320\) KiB ≈ 65 GB in BF16, 33 GB in FP8.

Architecture. Disaggregate (Chapter 13): a prefill pool sized for long prompts, with context parallelism (ring attention, load-balanced chunks) across a few servers for the first question; a decode pool that receives the cache over RDMA. Store each document's KV cache in a tiered cache (HBM, host DRAM, local SSD), keyed by document hash, with KV-aware routing that sends follow-up questions to workers that hold or can cheaply fetch the cache; place the question after the document so the document is a reusable prefix. With FP8 caches, a host with 2 TB of DRAM holds about 60 cached 200k-token documents.

Deep dive. The interviewer may probe the load-versus-recompute decision (§10.8): fetching a 33 GB cache from host memory over PCIe at 64 GB/s takes about 0.5 s, from a remote node over a single 400 Gb/s link about 0.7 s, or under 0.1 s when eight GPUs each pull their share in parallel, all far below recomputing 17–130 s of prefill. Or the decode side: each decode step reads 33–65 GB of cache per sequence, so batch per decode replica is small, TPOT is cache-bound, and speculative decoding is especially effective because long contexts keep verification memory-bound (§20.6).

Trade-offs. Retrieval (RAG) would answer many questions with 4k-token prompts at a hundredth of the cost but miss cross-document reasoning; a hybrid (Self-Route, §21.7) answers from retrieval first and escalates. Sparse attention models (DSA, NSA) would cut both prefill attention FLOPs and decode cache reads by close to an order of magnitude if available. Operations. Track cache-hit rate per document and per session, prefill pool queueing, transfer latency; evaluate long-context quality with RULER-style tests on real documents; set eviction policies by document recency and user tier.

30.6 Case study: serving a 671B MoE at low cost

Prompt. "We want to serve a DeepSeek-V3-class model (671B total, 37B active, MLA) at the lowest cost per token, for an API with high traffic."

Key insight to state early. At batch 1 the model behaves like a 37B model, but at moderate batch every step reads nearly all 671 GB of weights while each of the 256 experts sees only \(Bk_r/n_{\text{exp}}=B/32\) tokens (§19.4). Low cost requires each expert to see hundreds of tokens per step, so the aggregate batch across the expert-parallel group must be in the thousands to tens of thousands. That dictates wide expert parallelism.

Design. FP8 weights and computation, as the model was trained. Disaggregated prefill and decode (§19.7). Decode: expert parallelism across hundreds of GPUs (for example 256–320), one or two routed experts per GPU, data-parallel MLA attention so every GPU contributes its requests' tokens to every expert, all-to-all dispatch and combine with node-limited routing, FP8 dispatch and low-latency kernels (DeepEP), two-batch overlap to hide communication, and an expert-placement balancer with redundant hot experts. Prefill: smaller EP units (about 32 GPUs) with large batches and the same overlap. Multi-token-prediction modules for speculative decoding. Prefix caching with a disk tier for repeated prompts.

Estimates. Per decode GPU, expert weights are about 2.6 GB (one expert per layer over 58 layers in FP8) plus attention and shared weights, leaving most memory for MLA caches at about 70 KB per token, so a few hundred sequences per GPU at multi-thousand-token contexts; with 320 GPUs the aggregate batch per step is tens of thousands of tokens and each expert sees hundreds, near the ridge. Per-token communication is about 172 KB per MoE layer (§19.5), which at a few hundred sequences per GPU per step is comparable to compute and must be overlapped. DeepSeek's published figure, about 14,800 output tokens per second per eight-GPU node, gives about 30–40 cents per million output tokens at $2–2.50 per GPU-hour, which is the target to beat.

Follow-ups. "What if we only have 16 GPUs?" Then the model is memory-bound on all experts at every practical batch (§19.8): use FP8 or 4-bit experts on two 8-GPU servers with EP within each and pipeline or EP across, accept higher cost per token, or choose a smaller model. "How do you handle a hot expert?" Replicate it and rebalance placement from load statistics. "Why not tensor parallelism for experts?" Experts are small (\(7168\times2048\)); TP would make GEMMs thin and add all-reduces; EP keeps them whole.

30.7 Case study: code completion under 100 ms

Prompt. "Design inline code completion for an IDE: suggestions must appear within about 100 ms of the user pausing."

Requirements. The latency budget is end to end: network round trip, queueing, prefill of the context (the file around the cursor, perhaps 2–8k tokens), and generation of a short completion (10–50 tokens). Completions are frequently cancelled as the user keeps typing, so wasted work matters. Quality needs a capable but not frontier-size code model.

Design. Figure 30.5 allocates the budget. A small code model (1–7B), FP8 or 4-bit, served from regions close to users to keep the round trip near 20 ms. Prefix caching is decisive: consecutive keystrokes change only the end of the context, so the router sends each user's requests to the same replica (session affinity) and only the new tokens are prefilled, typically a few dozen. Decoding uses speculative decoding with prompt lookup (code completions frequently copy identifiers and patterns from the file) or a tiny draft model, at small batch sizes chosen for TPOT rather than throughput. The client debounces keystrokes, cancels in-flight requests when the user types, and the server aborts their decoding immediately to free slots. A small on-device model could handle the simplest completions offline, escalating to the server for longer ones (§29.8).

Estimates. A 7B model in FP8 on an H100 reads about 7 GB per step, about 2.1 ms at batch 1 and perhaps 3–4 ms at batch 16 with short contexts; 20 tokens with 2.5× speculation take about 20–30 ms; prefill of 50 new tokens against a cached 4k prefix takes a few milliseconds. The budget is achievable, and the dominant risks are network latency and queueing under bursts, which argues for keeping utilisation moderate (perhaps 50%) on this latency-critical tier. Trade-off. Cost per token is higher than for chat because batches are small and utilisation is deliberately kept low; the product value of instant suggestions justifies it, and the volume of cancelled work is the main thing to minimise.

30.8 Case study: offline batch inference over a billion documents

Prompt. "Classify and summarise one billion documents (average 1,000 tokens in, 100 tokens out) with an 8B model within a week, as cheaply as possible."

Reframe. There is no latency target per request, only a deadline for the whole job. This is the "offline" regime (§28.4): maximise throughput per dollar. Estimates. Total work: \(10^{12}\) input tokens and \(10^{11}\) output tokens. Prefill of \(10^{12}\) tokens at \(1.6\times10^{10}\) FLOPs each is \(1.6\times10^{22}\) FLOPs, about 7,500 H100-hours at 60% of BF16 peak, or about half that in FP8. Decode of \(10^{11}\) tokens at very large batch: with short contexts (1,100 tokens) an H100 in FP8 can run batches of 512 or more, around 12,000–20,000 tokens/s, so about 1,500–2,300 H100-hours. Prefill dominates; the total is roughly 5,000–10,000 GPU-hours, so a week (168 hours) needs about 30–60 GPUs.

Design. A distributed job scheduler shards the corpus and feeds engines with large, sorted batches (grouping similar lengths reduces padding and scheduling overhead); shared instructions are placed first and prefix-cached; FP8 throughout; outputs constrained by a grammar for the classification label (§4.7) to avoid parsing failures; checkpointing of progress so failed workers restart from the last completed shard. Use the cheapest capacity available, spot or preemptible instances or a provider's batch API at a discount, since preemptions only delay a shard. A smaller distilled model, validated on a sample against the 8B model, could halve the cost again.

Figure 30.6 sketches the pipeline.

Follow-ups. "How do you verify quality at this scale?" Paired evaluation of a random sample against a stronger reference, with confidence intervals (§28.6), plus monitoring of label distributions over shards to catch drift. "What if the deadline is a day?" Seven times the GPUs, or a smaller model, or both; the work does not change, only its parallelism.

30.9 Common failure modes and how answers are graded

The same mistakes recur in interviews, and most have appeared as lessons in earlier chapters. Ignoring the decode/prefill distinction, for example estimating latency as total FLOPs divided by peak, misses that decode is memory-bound and that output length, not prompt length, often dominates cost. Quoting throughput without latency (§28.1) or assuming 100% utilisation (§27.7) gives numbers that cannot be realised. Forgetting the KV cache, both its memory, which limits batch, and its bytes, which dominate long-context steps, is the most common technical gap. Over-parallelising, such as tensor parallelism across servers for a model that fits in one (§18.2), or disaggregating short-prompt chat traffic without justification (§13.6). Generic web architecture, load balancers and databases with no LLM-specific components, signals unfamiliarity with inference. And no operations: no metrics, no failure handling, no rollout plan.

Interviewers probe with follow-ups designed to test depth and adaptability: "Your traffic doubles overnight", "The model changes to an MoE", "Prompts grow to 100k tokens", "We must cut cost by half", "One GPU in each TP group fails every day". A strong candidate re-estimates, identifies which part of the design the change stresses, and adjusts, often naming a technique from a specific chapter: quantisation and distillation for cost, disaggregation and context parallelism for long prompts, expert parallelism for MoE, spares and smaller failure domains for reliability. Grading then follows the rubric of §30.1, with seniority setting the expectations: correct fundamentals for junior roles, ownership of the dominant trade-offs for senior ones, and a view of the system's evolution and economics for staff-level roles.

Two habits help more than any amount of memorisation. The first is to always compute: when in doubt, estimate, even crudely, because a number anchors the conversation and exposes errors that words hide. The second is to always ask what the bottleneck is, because every technique in this book is the answer to some bottleneck, and knowing which one binds tells you which technique to reach for. These are the habits the book has tried to build, chapter by chapter, from the first roofline to the last fleet.

Drill 30.1 — Respond to a changed constraint

In the chat case study, the interviewer says: "Product wants to add a reasoning mode for 10% of requests, with 8,000 thinking tokens each." What changes, quantitatively, and how do you respond?

Solution. Reasoning requests add \(0.1\times7000\times8000=5.6\times10^{6}\) decode tokens/s at peak, more than double the existing \(2.1\times10^{6}\): decode work rises to about \(7.7\times10^{6}\) tokens/s. Their contexts also grow to about 9k tokens, so their caches are four to five times larger, cutting the feasible batch per GPU for those sequences. At similar per-GPU throughput the decode fleet would rise from about 500 to about 1,800 GPUs, and higher with the reduced batch. Responses: route reasoning requests to a separate pool sized for long outputs (memory planning by final length, §26.5); use a distilled reasoning model or reasoning-effort budgets to cut thinking tokens; enable speculative decoding there, where long contexts keep it effective; price the mode accordingly. The point to make is that output tokens, not requests, are the unit of cost.

Drill 30.2 — Estimate in your head

Without a calculator: (a) the BF16 KV cache of a 128k-token context for a model with 80 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 128; (b) the batch-1 TPOT of that model (70B, FP8) on four H100s with TP = 4 at 128k context; (c) whether a 400 Gb/s network can carry its KV from a prefill to a decode node in under a second.

Solution. (a) \(2\times80\times8\times128\times2=327{,}680\) bytes = 320 KiB per token; × 131,072 ≈ 43 GB. (b) Per GPU: weights 70.6/4 ≈ 17.6 GB, cache 43/4 ≈ 10.7 GB, total ≈ 28 GB at 3.35 TB/s ≈ 8.5 ms, plus about 0.8 ms of all-reduces: ≈ 9–10 ms. (c) Each of the four GPUs sends its 10.7 GB share over its own 50 GB/s NIC: about 0.2 s, comfortably under a second (and overlappable with prefill layer by layer).

30.10 Coding questions in inference interviews

Many inference loops pair the design interview with a coding interview on the same material, and the questions are predictable because they test exactly the mechanisms this book has explained. The most common is attention with a KV cache: implement multi-head (or grouped-query) attention for one decode step, appending the new key and value to a cache and attending over it, with the causal mask applied correctly in prefill and a check that incremental decoding matches a full forward pass (§3.2). Close behind are sampling questions, implementing temperature, top-\(k\), top-\(p\) and min-\(p\) on a vector of logits with numerically stable softmax and correct handling of ties (Chapter 4), and online softmax, computing softmax or attention in one pass over blocks with running maxima and sums, the core of FlashAttention (§9.3).

Systems-flavoured questions follow the engine chapters. A paged KV allocator: maintain a free list of fixed-size blocks, block tables per sequence, reference counts for shared prefixes and copy-on-write when a shared block is appended to (Chapter 10). A continuous-batching scheduler: given arriving requests with prompt and output lengths, simulate steps with a token budget, admit, chunk prefills and preempt under a memory limit, and report TTFT and TPOT (Chapter 11). A prefix cache with hashing of token blocks and LRU eviction, or a radix tree over token sequences (§10.6). More mathematical variants ask for the speculative-sampling acceptance rule and its residual distribution (§20.2), a ring all-reduce simulated over ranks (§17.4), or a quantise–dequantise routine with per-group scales and a measurement of its error (§14.3).

The evaluation of such code mirrors the design rubric: correctness first, including edge cases (empty caches, end-of-sequence handling, numerical stability with large logits); then clarity and the ability to explain complexity in both FLOPs and bytes; then awareness of how the toy implementation differs from production, for example that a real paged-attention kernel gathers blocks inside the kernel rather than materialising the cache, or that a real scheduler must cope with unknown output lengths. Practising each of these with a small NumPy or PyTorch implementation, checked against a reference, is the most efficient preparation, and the drills and worked examples of the relevant chapters supply the test cases.

Looking ahead

This is the end of the book, but not of the subject. Hardware will keep changing the constants: Blackwell's FP4 tensor cores and NVL72 racks, HBM4, and accelerators from other vendors with different balances of compute, bandwidth and memory. Models will keep changing the structure: sparser mixtures of experts, hybrid and sparse attention, multimodal inputs and outputs, reasoning that grows ever longer. Serving systems will keep absorbing these changes, as they absorbed paging, continuous batching, disaggregation and speculation. What should not change is the method this book has tried to teach: write down the bytes and the FLOPs, find the bottleneck, ask what each technique changes in the equations, measure carefully, and let the numbers decide. With that method, the next technique, whatever it is, will make sense on the day it appears.

Design Design the serving system for a chat assistant with 10 million daily active users.

Requirements (assume and state): ~20 messages/user/day → 2×10⁸ requests/day, ~2,300/s average, ~7,000/s peak; ~1,000 input tokens (≈ 600 cached history/system prompt), ~300 output; P99 TTFT < 1 s, P99 TPOT < 25 ms; 70B-class quality. Estimates: peak decode 2.1M tokens/s; with FP8 TP = 4 at 25 ms (~6,000 tokens/s/GPU) ≈ 500 decode GPUs at 70% utilisation; uncached prefill 2.8M tokens/s ≈ 4×10¹⁷ FLOP/s, largely absorbed by chunked prefill; ~700–800 H100s at peak, ~250 at night. Architecture: gateway (auth, limits, metering), router (session affinity + two-choice load), FP8 TP = 4 engines with paged FP8 KV, prefix caching and chunked prefill, host-memory KV tier for idle conversations, forecast-driven autoscaler, SLO monitoring. Deep dive: decode is memory-bound; FP8 halves weight and cache bytes; prefix hits cut prefill 60%. Trade-offs: disaggregation not needed for short prompts; router to a smaller model could halve cost; speculation for a latency tier. Cost ≈ $0.45 per million output tokens.

Design Users ask questions about 200k-token documents and TTFT is too slow. Fix it.

Prefill dominates: 2Nn + 2Ldn² ≈ 8×10¹⁶ FLOPs for a 70B model, >2 min on one GPU, ~17 s on 8, ~5 s on 32 with context parallelism. The document is reused across questions, so: cache its KV (65 GB BF16, 33 GB FP8) in a tiered store (HBM, host DRAM, SSD) keyed by document hash; route follow-ups to workers holding the cache (KV-aware routing); place questions after the document. First question: disaggregated prefill pool with ring-attention context parallelism; KV sent to decode workers over RDMA. Follow-ups: fetch cache (~0.5 s from host memory) instead of recomputing; TTFT ≈ 1 s. Decode is cache-bound (33–65 GB per step per sequence): small batches, FP8 cache, speculative decoding (long contexts keep it effective). Alternatives: RAG or Self-Route hybrid for cost; sparse-attention models (DSA/NSA) to cut both prefill and decode by up to ~8×. Measure long-context quality with RULER-style tests.

Design Serve a 671B MoE (37B active, MLA) at the lowest cost per token.

Key fact: at moderate batch every step reads nearly all 671 GB while each expert sees B/32 tokens, so cheapness needs aggregate batches of thousands to tens of thousands per step. Design: FP8 throughout; disaggregated prefill (EP ~32, large batches, two-batch overlap, redundant hot experts) and decode (EP ~256–320, one or two routed experts per GPU, data-parallel MLA attention so all GPUs feed all experts, low-latency all-to-all with FP8 dispatch and node-limited routing, two-batch overlap, EPLB placement with replicas); MTP speculative decoding; prefix caching with a disk tier. Estimates: ~2.6 GB of expert weights per decode GPU leaves memory for hundreds of sequences; ~172 KB of all-to-all per token per MoE layer must be overlapped; target ≈ DeepSeek's ~14.8k output tokens/s per 8-GPU node, ~30–40 cents per million tokens. With few GPUs, quantise experts or use a smaller model.

Design Design inline code completion with a ~100 ms end-to-end budget.

Budget: ~20 ms network (regional serving), a few ms queueing, ~25 ms prefill, ~45 ms for ~20 tokens, render. Model: 1–7B code model in FP8/INT4. Prefix caching is decisive: consecutive keystrokes change only the end of the context, so session-affine routing keeps each user's context cached and only new tokens are prefilled. Decode at small batch with speculative decoding via prompt lookup (code copies identifiers) or a tiny draft. Client debounces and cancels in-flight requests on new keystrokes; server aborts them immediately. Keep utilisation moderate (~50%) to protect tails; stream the first line early; optional on-device model for trivial completions. Monitor P99 end-to-end latency, acceptance rate of suggestions, cancellation rate and cost per accepted completion.

Design Classify and summarise a billion documents with an 8B model within a week, cheaply.

No per-request latency, only a deadline: maximise throughput per dollar. Work: 10¹² input tokens (prefill ≈ 1.6×10²² FLOPs ≈ 7,500 H100-hours in BF16, half in FP8) and 10¹¹ output tokens (decode at batches of 512+ ≈ 1,500–2,300 GPU-hours). Core Walk through the back-of-envelope estimates you would make for any LLM serving design.

Weights \(Nb_w\) (fits on how many GPUs?); KV per token \(2Ln_{kv}d_hb_{kv}\) and per sequence at the context length (how many sequences fit?); decode step ≈ (weights + B·t·s_kv)/(TP·β) + communication (TPOT at a batch; max batch meeting TPOT); prefill ≈ (2Nn + 2Ldn²)/π_eff (TTFT; attention crossover N/(Ld)); throughput per GPU = B/(step×TP); GPUs = peak output tokens/s ÷ per-GPU throughput ÷ utilisation, plus prefill capacity and spares; cost per million = GPUs × price × 10⁶/(3600 × tokens/s × u). Add α–β all-reduce cost for TP, χ for KV transfer, speculative speed-up for latency, and memory headroom for growing outputs. Sanity-check every result against the hardware roofline.

Core What hardware numbers should an inference candidate know by heart?

H100 SXM: ~989 TFLOP/s dense BF16 (~1,979 FP8), 3.35 TB/s HBM, 80 GB; ridge ≈ 295 FLOP/B. H200: same compute, 4.8 TB/s, 141 GB. B200: ~2.25 PFLOP/s BF16 dense, ~8 TB/s, ~192 GB, FP4 tensor cores. NVLink (Hopper) 450 GB/s per GPU per direction (900 bidirectional), Blackwell doubles; NVL72 racks. Network ~50 GB/s per GPU (400 Gb/s), 800 Gb/s on newer systems. PCIe Gen5 ~64 GB/s. Achievable efficiencies: 60–80% of peak FLOP/s for large GEMMs, 70–90% of bandwidth for good decode kernels. Consumer: RTX 4090 ~1 TB/s, 24 GB; M4 Max ~546 GB/s; phones ~60 GB/s.

Core How would you explain to an interviewer why decode is memory-bound and what follows from it?

Each decode step reads every weight once (and each sequence's KV cache) but performs only ~2 FLOPs per weight per sequence, so arithmetic intensity is ~2B/b_w FLOPs per byte for batch B; the H100 ridge is ~295 FLOP/B in BF16, so below batch ~300 the step time is bytes/bandwidth. Consequences: batching amortises weight reads (throughput rises almost linearly with B until the cache or compute binds); quantisation (FP8, INT4) directly speeds decode by cutting bytes; the KV cache (memory and bytes) limits batch and dominates at long contexts, so GQA/MLA and FP8 caches matter; idle compute can be spent on speculative decoding or chunked prefill; TP reduces per-GPU bytes for latency at the cost of communication.

Core What are the most common failure modes in ML-systems design interviews?

Designing before clarifying requirements; no quantitative estimates, or estimates that never inform decisions; treating inference as a generic web service (no engine, KV cache, batching or routing specifics); confusing prefill and decode costs (e.g. latency = FLOPs/peak); quoting throughput without latency or assuming 100% utilisation; forgetting KV-cache memory and bytes; over-parallelising (TP across servers, unneeded disaggregation); presenting one solution without alternatives; ignoring operations (metrics, SLOs, failures, rollouts, cost); and failing to adapt when the interviewer changes constraints. Remedy: follow the framework, compute aloud, name the bottleneck.

Deep-dive The interviewer says traffic doubles overnight. How do you respond in the chat design?

Re-estimate: decode work doubles to ~4.2M tokens/s at peak; at ~6,000 tokens/s/GPU and 70% utilisation, decode needs ~1,000 GPUs; prefill doubles too. Short term: capacity is the constraint, so add replicas (cloud bursting, reserved headroom), apply graceful degradation (shorter max outputs, route easy queries to a smaller model, defer batch traffic, tighter rate limits on free tiers), and protect SLOs with early rejection rather than queueing to timeouts. Medium term: reduce work per request (router/cascade to smaller models, distillation, better prefix-cache hit rates, reasoning budgets), increase per-GPU throughput (newer GPUs, FP8 everywhere, larger batches if TPOT allows). Mention autoscaling lag (cold starts) and the need for forecasts.

Deep-dive The interviewer says "cut cost per token in half." What are the options and their trade-offs?

Write cost = GPUs × price/(tokens/s × utilisation) and attack each factor. Throughput per GPU: FP8 (or FP4 on Blackwell) weights and KV; larger batches with a slightly looser TPOT target; better kernels; GQA/MLA models; speculative decoding only where long contexts keep it free. Work per request: prefix caching improvements (prompt structure, session affinity), shorter outputs or reasoning budgets, routing easy queries to a distilled smaller model (often the biggest single lever). Utilisation: better autoscaling and forecasts, consolidating models, multi-LoRA instead of dedicated replicas, moving flexible traffic to troughs with a batch API. Price: newer hardware with better perf/$, reserved capacity, spot for batch. Each has quality or latency risks to validate with paired evaluation and load tests.

Deep-dive How do you decide between colocated chunked prefill and disaggregation in an interview?

State the colocation bound: on identical hardware, an ideal colocated engine with chunked prefill has at least the throughput of a disaggregated one, because it fills decode's idle compute with prefill. Disaggregation pays when that ideal is unattainable: tight joint TTFT and TPOT targets with bursty long prompts (interference hurts P99 TPOT), long prompts making prefill dominant, different hardware or parallelism per phase (wide-EP MoE decode, TP or CP prefill), or operational isolation and independent scaling. Check the KV-transfer cost χ (small with GQA/MLA on 400 Gb/s) and the pool ratio from prompt/output lengths. For short-prompt chat with relaxed TPOT, colocate; for long documents, reasoning-heavy or MoE at scale, disaggregate.

Deep-dive How would you handle a 10% reasoning-mode traffic share in an existing chat fleet?

Quantify: 10% of 7,000 req/s × 8,000 thinking tokens adds 5.6M decode tokens/s, more than doubling decode work, with contexts growing to ~9k tokens (caches 4–5× larger, smaller batches). Separate pool for reasoning requests sized for long outputs: admission by expected final length with KV headroom, FP8 cache, swap-based preemption, speculative decoding (effective at long contexts), streaming of reasoning summaries, looser TPOT. Reduce thinking tokens: reasoning-effort budgets, a distilled reasoning model, difficulty routing so only hard queries use it. Price the mode by output tokens. Monitor tokens per request, preemptions and cost per solved task.

Design Design a multi-tenant fine-tuning-and-serving platform for 5,000 customers on one base model.

Serve LoRA adapters on shared base-model replicas (S-LoRA/Punica-style multi-LoRA engines): base FP8 weights once per replica, adapters (~84 MB each for 8B rank 16) in host memory, paged into a unified GPU pool with KV blocks, SGMV kernels for mixed-adapter batches. Route customers to replica subsets by consistent hashing with bounded loads (adapter and prefix locality); cap distinct adapters per batch; merge the highest-traffic customers' adapters into dedicated replicas. Per-customer token buckets and tiers, batch API, VTC-style fairness. Autoscale on tokens and adapter load. Training pipeline produces adapters into a registry with evaluation gates. Cost accounting per customer; monitoring of per-adapter latency, page-ins and quality.

Core How do you structure a 45-minute design interview answer in time?

About 5 minutes clarifying requirements and writing them down (traffic, lengths, SLOs, quality, cost, constraints); 5–10 minutes of estimates (memory, KV, step times, prefill, GPUs, cost); about 10 minutes on the architecture and request flow with LLM-specific components; 10–15 minutes on a deep dive into the dominant bottleneck, guided by the interviewer; 5–10 minutes comparing alternatives and trade-offs; about 5 minutes on operations (metrics, SLOs, failures, rollout, cost). Reserve time for follow-ups that change constraints; when they come, revisit the estimates first. Think aloud and keep numbers visible.

Core Why should you always sanity-check your estimates against the roofline during an interview?

Because errors of an order of magnitude are easy (bits versus bytes, per-GPU versus aggregate bandwidth, forgetting the KV cache or the TP split) and they propagate into every decision. A quick check, e.g. 'implied bytes per step ÷ step time must be below aggregate HBM bandwidth', 'implied FLOPs per second below peak × efficiency', 'weights plus cache must fit in memory', catches them, and saying it aloud shows the interviewer disciplined reasoning. It also helps evaluate claims the interviewer presents ('a vendor says 120k tokens/s on 8 GPUs'): an impossible number is a useful discussion point.

Core In a coding interview you are asked to implement single-step decode attention with a KV cache. What do you check and discuss?

Implementation: project the new token to q, k, v per head (with GQA, map each query head to its KV head); append k and v to the cache for this layer; compute scores \(qK^\top/\sqrt{d_h}\) over all cached positions with a numerically stable softmax (subtract the maximum), weight the values and concatenate heads. Tests: incremental decoding over a prompt must match a full causal forward pass to floating-point tolerance; edge cases include the first token (empty cache), very large logits and batch elements of different lengths (padding masks or ragged layouts). Discussion: per step the cost is \(O(t\,d)\) FLOPs and the bytes are the whole cache, so the operation is memory-bound; production kernels use paged layouts, read blocks inside the kernel, split long contexts across thread blocks (flash-decoding) and fuse the softmax, and the cache's memory, \(2Ln_{kv}d_hb\) per token, limits batch size.
