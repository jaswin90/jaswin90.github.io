Part VIII · Post-Training and Reasoning — Chapter 23

Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

Supervised fine-tuning teaches a model to imitate. Reinforcement learning teaches it to improve: the model generates, a score says how good the generation was, and the model is nudged toward the generations that scored well. This chapter builds the mathematics from the score-function identity to PPO, the algorithm that aligned the first generation of chat models, and shows why every step of it is, computationally, an inference workload.

Why this chapter

Chapter 16 ended with a limitation of fine-tuning by demonstration. An SFT model learns to reproduce its examples, so it can be no better than they are, and it never sees the consequences of its own mistakes: at training time every prefix comes from a human-written example, while at inference time every prefix comes from the model itself, so an early slip leads to states the model was never trained on[17]. Both limits disappear if, instead of demonstrations, we have a way to judge outputs. A human can often say which of two answers is better without being able to write the best one; a unit test can say whether code works; a checker can say whether a mathematical answer is right. Learning from such judgements of the model's own samples is reinforcement learning (RL).

The ideas are old. Williams derived the REINFORCE estimator in 1992[1], Sutton and colleagues proved the policy-gradient theorem in 2000[2], and Schulman and colleagues introduced TRPO in 2015 and PPO in 2017 for robotics and games. They reached language models through a chain of papers at OpenAI: Christiano et al. (2017) learned reward functions[6] from human comparisons for Atari and simulated robots; Ziegler et al. (2019) applied the method[7], with a KL penalty, to fine-tune GPT-2; Stiennon et al. (2020) produced summaries[8] that humans preferred to the reference summaries; and InstructGPT (2022) turned the recipe[9] into the pipeline behind ChatGPT. In 2024 and 2025 the same machinery, with rewards from automatic checkers instead of humans, produced the reasoning models of Chapter 26.

This chapter develops the RL that all of those systems share, in the language of probability that a mathematician already speaks. The next two chapters then supply the two kinds of reward: human preferences (Chapter 24) and verifiable checks (Chapter 25). Throughout, we keep one eye on cost, because an RL step for a language model consists mostly of generating text, and generating text is the subject of the rest of this book.

23.1 Generation as a decision process

To apply reinforcement learning we must say what the agent, its actions and its rewards are. For a language model the answer is pleasingly concrete. A prompt \(x\) arrives. The model, which in RL is called the policy, generates a response \(y=(y_1,\dots,y_{n_y})\) one token at a time, each drawn from its next-token distribution \(p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\). After the response ends, a reward \(\mathcal R(x,y)\in\mathbb R\) scores it. The objective is the expected reward under the model's own sampling:

\[ J(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D}\;\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\mathcal R(x,y)\big]. \]

This can be read in two ways, and both are useful. At the sequence level, the whole response is one action drawn from the distribution \(p_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_tp_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\), and the problem is a contextual bandit: one context, one action, one reward. At the token level, it is a Markov decision process (MDP). The state \(s_t=(x,y_{<t})\) is the prompt and the tokens so far, the action \(a_t=y_t\) is the next token, the transition is deterministic (append the token), and the per-step rewards \(\mathcal R_t\) are zero except at the last step, where \(\mathcal R_{n_y}=\mathcal R(x,y)\). Figure 23.1 draws the token-level view.

Figure 23.1 shows the two features that distinguish language-model RL from the games and robots where the algorithms were invented. First, the action space is enormous, the whole vocabulary of 128,256 tokens for Llama 3, but the policy starts out excellent: a pre-trained, fine-tuned model already puts most of its probability on sensible tokens, so exploration means sampling from the model, not searching blindly. Second, the reward is sparse and delayed: one number for a response of hundreds or thousands of tokens. Deciding which tokens deserve the credit is the credit-assignment problem, and much of this chapter is about solving it with less noise.

Why not simply differentiate \(J\)? Because the expectation is over samples, and sampling is not differentiable: changing \(\theta\) changes which tokens are drawn, and \(\mathcal R\) is evaluated on discrete text that has no gradient with respect to the logits. Often the reward is not differentiable at all, being a unit test or a human. The SFT loss sidestepped this by evaluating fixed data. RL needs a way to estimate \(\nabla J\) from samples and scores alone. That is the next section.

23.2 The policy-gradient theorem

The key identity is one line of calculus, the score-function or log-derivative trick. For any distribution \(p_\theta\) and any function \(f\) that does not depend on \(\theta\),

\[ \nabla_\theta\,\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[f(y)]\;=\;\sum_yf(y)\,\nabla_\theta p_\theta(y)\;=\;\sum_yf(y)\,p_\theta(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y)\;=\;\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}\big[f(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y)\big]. \]

The gradient of an expectation becomes the expectation of the function times the score \(\nabla\log p_\theta\), which we can estimate by sampling. Applying it to \(J\), and using \(\log p_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_t\log p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)\), gives the policy-gradient theorem for language models:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb E_{x}\,\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\mathcal R(x,y)\sum_{t=1}^{n_y}\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)\Big]. \]

The REINFORCE estimator (Williams, 1992) replaces the expectation with an average over sampled prompts and responses. Its form is worth staring at, because it reveals what RL does to a language model. The gradient of the SFT loss on a response \(y\) is \(-\sum_t\nabla\log p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)\). The REINFORCE gradient is the same quantity, for the model's own sample, multiplied by its reward. A policy-gradient step is therefore a step of supervised fine-tuning on the model's own outputs, weighted by how good they were: good samples are made more likely, and, if rewards can be negative, bad ones less likely.

The special case of a binary reward makes the connection exact. If \(\mathcal R\in\{0,1\}\), the REINFORCE gradient is the SFT gradient on the samples that succeeded, and those that failed contribute nothing. Sampling many responses, keeping the correct ones and fine-tuning on them is called rejection-sampling fine-tuning or expert iteration, and in STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022)[10] it taught models to reason by bootstrapping from their own correct rationales. It is REINFORCE with a 0/1 reward, done in large batches.

For the mathematician — the score function and its variance

The identity \(\nabla\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[f]=\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[f\nabla\log p_\theta]\) is the likelihood-ratio method of stochastic simulation. It requires only that \(p_\theta\) be differentiable in \(\theta\) and that we can sample from it and evaluate its log-density, all of which a language model provides. It makes no assumption on \(f\), which may be discontinuous or a black box. The price is variance: the estimator multiplies a possibly large \(f\) by a score whose components have zero mean, \(\mathbb E[\nabla\log p_\theta]=\nabla\sum_yp_\theta(y)=\nabla1=0\), so the useful signal is a small correlation between \(f\) and the score, buried in noise. The alternative, the reparameterisation ("pathwise") estimator used in variational autoencoders, has lower variance but needs \(f\) to be differentiable and the sample to be a smooth function of \(\theta\) and the noise, neither of which holds for discrete tokens judged by a checker.

Worked example — REINFORCE on a two-answer problem

Setup. A prompt has two possible answers: \(A\) (correct, reward 1) with probability \(p\), and \(B\) (wrong, reward 0) with probability \(1-p\), where \(p=\sigma(z)\) is controlled by one logit \(z\). Start at \(p=0.2\).

Exact gradient. \(J=p\), so \(\mathrm dJ/\mathrm dz=p(1-p)=0.16\).

Estimator. The score is \(\mathrm d\log p/\mathrm dz=1-p=0.8\) for \(A\) and \(\mathrm d\log(1-p)/\mathrm dz=-p=-0.2\) for \(B\). REINFORCE takes the value \(1\times0.8=0.8\) with probability 0.2, and \(0\times(-0.2)=0\) with probability 0.8: mean \(0.16\), as it must be, but standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.2\times0.64-0.16^2}=0.32\), twice the mean. With 16 samples the relative error of the average is still \(0.32/(0.16\sqrt{16})=50\%\).

Consequence. Even in this tiny problem the estimate is noisier than the signal. With hundreds of tokens, a vocabulary of 128k and rewards that vary from prompt to prompt, raw REINFORCE is far noisier still. Reducing the variance without biasing the gradient is the subject of the next section.

23.3 Baselines, reward-to-go and advantages

Suppose one prompt is easy and every response to it earns a reward between 0.9 and 1, while another is hard and every response earns between 0 and 0.1. REINFORCE pushes up the probability of everything sampled for the easy prompt, good and less good alike, because every reward is large and positive, and barely moves the hard one. What matters for learning is not the reward itself but whether a response did better or worse than the model's typical response to the same prompt. A baseline makes this precise. For any function \(b(x)\) that does not depend on the sampled response,

\[ \mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}\big[b(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y\mid x)\big]\;=\;b(x)\,\nabla_\theta\sum_yp_\theta(y\mid x)\;=\;b(x)\,\nabla_\theta1\;=\;0, \]

so subtracting \(b(x)\) from the reward leaves the expected gradient unchanged while, for a good choice of \(b\), reducing its variance greatly. The natural choice is the expected reward of the current policy on that prompt, \(b(x)\approx\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[\mathcal R(x,y)]\). Then a response's weight is positive if it beat the policy's average and negative if it fell short, and an easy prompt no longer drowns out a hard one. (The exactly variance-minimising constant is a score-weighted average of the reward, which differs slightly; the expected reward is the standard practical choice.)

A second free reduction comes from causality. The action at step \(t\) cannot influence rewards received before step \(t\), so in the token-level sum we may replace the total reward by the reward-to-go \(\sum_{k\ge t}\mathcal R_k\), dropping terms whose expected contribution is zero. With a single terminal reward, every token's reward-to-go equals the final reward, so this changes nothing; it matters once we add per-token rewards, as the KL penalty of §23.5 will.

The baseline can also depend on the state. The best state-dependent baseline is the expected future reward from that state, the state-value function \(V^{p}(s_t)=\mathbb E[\sum_{k\ge t}\mathcal R_k\mid s_t]\), and the difference between what an action actually led to and what was expected from its state is the advantage,

\[ A^{p}(s_t,a_t)\;=\;Q^{p}(s_t,a_t)-V^{p}(s_t),\qquad Q^{p}(s_t,a_t)=\mathbb E\Big[\sum_{k\ge t}\mathcal R_k\;\Big|\;s_t,a_t\Big]. \]

The policy gradient can be written exactly as \(\mathbb E\big[\sum_tA^{p}(s_t,a_t)\nabla\log p_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big]\). Each token is pushed up in proportion to how much better than expected things went after it was chosen. This is the ideal answer to the credit-assignment problem of §23.1: a token that turned a likely failure into a likely success has a large advantage, while a token that changed nothing has an advantage near zero, even if the response as a whole was rewarded. The difficulty is that \(V^{p}\) and \(Q^{p}\) are unknown. The algorithms of the rest of this Part differ mainly in how they estimate the advantage. PPO learns \(V^{p}\) with a second network (§23.4); the methods of Chapter 25 estimate the baseline by sampling several responses per prompt and averaging their rewards.

Figure 23.2 shows the effect on two prompts. Without a baseline, the easy prompt's samples receive weights near 1 and dominate the update, although they differ only slightly in quality; with the per-prompt mean subtracted, both prompts contribute weights of comparable size, positive for the better samples and negative for the worse. This per-prompt mean is exactly the baseline that GRPO computes by sampling a group of responses (Chapter 25). A learned value function is the alternative, and it is the subject of the next section.

Drill 23.1 — A state-dependent baseline is still unbiased

Show that for any function \(b(s_t)\) of the state alone, \(\mathbb E\big[b(s_t)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\big]=0\), where the expectation is over trajectories from \(p_\theta\).

Solution. Condition on \(s_t\): \(\mathbb E[b(s_t)\nabla\log p_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\mid s_t]=b(s_t)\sum_{a}p_\theta(a\mid s_t)\nabla\log p_\theta(a\mid s_t)=b(s_t)\nabla\sum_ap_\theta(a\mid s_t)=b(s_t)\nabla1=0\). Taking the expectation over \(s_t\) gives zero by the tower property. The argument fails if \(b\) depends on \(a_t\) or on later tokens, which is why an "action-dependent baseline" introduces bias unless corrected.

23.4 Value functions and generalised advantage estimation

To use advantages we must estimate them, and the classical way is to learn the value function. A critic \(V_\psi(s_t)\) is a network, for language models usually a copy of the policy with its vocabulary head replaced by a scalar head, trained by regression to predict the reward-to-go from each prefix. Given a critic, how should we combine it with the observed rewards to estimate \(A^{p}(s_t,a_t)\)?

The simplest estimate uses one step of observed reward and the critic for everything after: the temporal-difference (TD) error

\[ \delta_t\;=\;\mathcal R_t+\gamma_d\,V_\psi(s_{t+1})-V_\psi(s_t), \]

where \(\gamma_d\le1\) is a discount factor (in language-model RL, usually 1). If \(V_\psi\) were exact, \(\delta_t\) would be an unbiased estimate of the advantage with low variance, because it involves only one random reward. But \(V_\psi\) is learned and wrong, so \(\delta_t\) is biased. At the other extreme, the Monte Carlo estimate \(\sum_{k\ge t}\mathcal R_k-V_\psi(s_t)\) uses all observed rewards and the critic only as a baseline: unbiased, but with the full variance of the rest of the response. Schulman et al.'s generalised advantage estimation (GAE, 2015)[4] interpolates with a parameter \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}\in[0,1]\):

\[ \hat A_t\;=\;\sum_{l\ge0}(\gamma_d\lambda_{\text{GAE}})^l\,\delta_{t+l}. \]

A telescoping sum shows the two ends. With \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=0\), \(\hat A_t=\delta_t\), the one-step TD estimate. With \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=1\) and \(\gamma_d=1\), the intermediate values cancel and \(\hat A_t=\sum_{k\ge t}\mathcal R_k-V_\psi(s_t)\), the Monte Carlo estimate with a baseline. Intermediate values trade bias against variance by trusting the critic progressively more for the distant future. The critic's own target is the reward-to-go estimate \(\hat A_t+V_\psi(s_t)\), so policy and critic are trained together on the same rollouts.

Worked example — GAE on a four-token response

Setup. A response of four tokens earns a final reward of 1 and no other reward; \(\gamma_d=1\). The critic's values for the states before tokens 1 to 4, and after the last, are \(V=(0.5,\,0.4,\,0.7,\,0.8,\,0)\) (the terminal value is 0).

TD errors. \(\delta_1=0+0.4-0.5=-0.1\); \(\delta_2=0+0.7-0.4=0.3\); \(\delta_3=0+0.8-0.7=0.1\); \(\delta_4=1+0-0.8=0.2\).

\(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=1\). \(\hat A_t\) is the sum of the remaining \(\delta\)s: \(\hat A=(0.5,\,0.6,\,0.3,\,0.2)\), equal to \(1-V(s_t)\) for each token, as the telescoping argument says.

\(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=0\). \(\hat A=\delta=(-0.1,\,0.3,\,0.1,\,0.2)\). Now the second token gets most of the credit, because the critic believes it raised the chance of success from 0.4 to 0.7, and the first token is slightly penalised, because the critic believes it lowered it from 0.5 to 0.4.

Consequence. With a good critic, small \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}\) assigns credit to the tokens that actually changed the outlook, which is exactly what a sparse reward cannot do alone. With a bad critic, the same mechanism assigns credit arbitrarily. Language-model PPO commonly uses \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}\) near 0.95 or even 1, trusting the critic little, because a critic over prefixes of text is hard to learn.

The worked example exposes the central weakness of critic-based methods for language models. The critic must predict, from any prefix, how good the eventual response will be, a task as hard as judging the response itself, and it must be trained alongside the policy, doubling the memory and compute. If the critic is poor, it adds bias without removing much variance. This is why the methods of Chapter 25 drop the critic altogether and estimate baselines by sampling. Before we can see why PPO kept one, we must add the ingredient that makes RL on language models safe.

23.5 The KL-regularised objective

Maximising \(J(\theta)\) alone is dangerous for a language model, for three reasons. The reward is usually imperfect: a learned reward model (Chapter 24) is a neural network with blind spots, and a policy optimised hard against it finds and exploits them, producing text that scores highly and reads badly. This is reward hacking. Second, the pre-trained model's fluency and knowledge are valuable, and nothing in \(\mathcal R\) protects them. Third, RL can collapse the distribution onto a few high-reward responses, destroying diversity. The standard remedy, introduced for language models by Ziegler et al. (2019) and used in InstructGPT, is to penalise divergence from a frozen reference policy \(p_{\text{ref}}\), usually the SFT model:

\[ J_{\text{KL}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb E_x\Big[\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\mathcal R(x,y)\big]\;-\;\beta_{\text{KL}}\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,p_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big]. \]

The coefficient \(\beta_{\text{KL}}>0\) sets the exchange rate between reward and staying close to the reference. (We write \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\), not the customary \(\beta\), because \(\beta\) is bandwidth throughout this book.) This objective has a closed-form maximiser, and it is one of the most useful facts in the subject. For a fixed prompt \(x\), the maximum over all distributions \(p\) is attained at

\[ p^*(y\mid x)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{\mathcal R(x,y)}{\beta_{\text{KL}}}\Big),\qquad Z(x)=\sum_yp_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{\mathcal R(x,y)/\beta_{\text{KL}}}. \]

The optimal policy reweights the reference by an exponential of the reward. A response the reference never produces is never produced by the optimum either, however high its reward, which is the sense in which the KL term keeps the policy on the reference's support. As \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\to\infty\), \(p^*\to p_{\text{ref}}\); as \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\to0\), \(p^*\) concentrates on the highest-reward responses that the reference can produce. Figure 23.3 shows the trade on a two-answer problem.

For the mathematician — the Gibbs variational principle

For fixed \(x\), write the objective as \(\sum_yp(y)\mathcal R(y)-\beta_{\text{KL}}\sum_yp(y)\log\frac{p(y)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y)}\). Substituting the definition of \(p^*\), so that \(\mathcal R(y)=\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\frac{p^*(y)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y)}+\beta_{\text{KL}}\log Z\), gives

\[ \sum_yp(y)\mathcal R(y)-\beta_{\text{KL}}\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\;=\;\beta_{\text{KL}}\log Z\;-\;\beta_{\text{KL}}\,\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert p^*). \]

Since KL divergence is non-negative and zero only when its arguments agree, the objective is maximised uniquely at \(p=p^*\), where it equals \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\log Z\). This is the Gibbs variational principle of statistical mechanics, with reward as negative energy and \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\) as temperature: the "free energy" \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\log Z\) is the best achievable regularised reward. Chapter 24 inverts this formula, solving it for \(\mathcal R\) in terms of \(p^*\), to obtain DPO.

Figure 23.3 makes two practical points. First, what matters is the ratio of reward differences to \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\): rescaling the reward by a factor is equivalent to dividing \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\) by it, which is why reward models are normalised and why \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\) must be tuned for each reward. Second, the transition is fairly sharp: over a factor of ten in \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\), the policy goes from barely moved to almost deterministic. Typical values in RLHF were 0.01 to 0.1 for rewards of order one, per token.

Implementation. In practice the KL term is not computed over all responses but estimated on the sampled ones and folded into the reward token by token. With \(\varrho^{\text{ref}}_t=p_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid s_t)/p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)\), three estimators of the per-token KL are common, named \(k_1\), \(k_2\), \(k_3\) after a note by Schulman[11]: \(k_1=-\log\varrho^{\text{ref}}_t\) (unbiased, high variance, can be negative), \(k_2=\tfrac12(\log\varrho^{\text{ref}}_t)^2\) (biased, low variance), and \(k_3=(\varrho^{\text{ref}}_t-1)-\log\varrho^{\text{ref}}_t\) (unbiased, low variance and always non-negative). PPO-style RLHF usually subtracts \(\beta_{\text{KL}}k_1\) from each token's reward, giving the per-token rewards \(\mathcal R_t=-\beta_{\text{KL}}k_1\) plus \(\mathcal R(x,y)\) at the end; GRPO (Chapter 25) instead adds \(\beta_{\text{KL}}k_3\) directly to the loss. Either way, every RL step must evaluate the reference model on every sampled token, a second forward pass to budget for.

Drill 23.2 — The \(k_3\) estimator

Show that \(k_3=(\varrho-1)-\log\varrho\), with \(\varrho=p_{\text{ref}}(y)/p_\theta(y)\) and \(y\sim p_\theta\), is non-negative and has expectation \(\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\).

Solution. Non-negativity: \(\log u\le u-1\) for all \(u>0\), with equality only at \(u=1\), so \(k_3\ge0\). Expectation: \(\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[\varrho]=\sum_yp_\theta(y)\,p_{\text{ref}}(y)/p_\theta(y)=1\), so \(\mathbb E[\varrho-1]=0\), and \(\mathbb E[-\log\varrho]=\sum_yp_\theta(y)\log\frac{p_\theta(y)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y)}=\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\). The term \(\varrho-1\) is a control variate: it has mean zero and is negatively correlated with \(\log\varrho\), so adding it reduces variance without bias.

23.6 From trust regions to PPO

REINFORCE is on-policy: its gradient is an expectation under the current policy, so after one gradient step the samples are stale and new ones must be generated. For language models, generating is the expensive part (§23.7), so we would like to take several optimisation steps on each batch of rollouts. Importance sampling makes that possible. If the rollouts were generated by an older policy \(p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), then, to first order in the change of policy, the objective's improvement can be estimated by the surrogate

\[ L(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb E_{s_t,a_t\sim p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\big[\varrho_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t\big],\qquad \varrho_t(\theta)=\frac{p_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}. \]

At \(\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}\), \(\nabla L\) equals the policy gradient, since \(\nabla\varrho_t=\varrho_t\nabla\log p_\theta\). But the surrogate is trustworthy only near \(\theta_{\text{old}}\): it ignores the fact that a changed policy visits different states, and maximising it without limit drives \(\varrho_t\) to extremes, with a few tokens dominating the update. The policy can then collapse in a single bad step, and in RL a collapsed policy generates bad data, from which it cannot easily recover.

Schulman et al.'s TRPO (2015)[3] maximised the surrogate subject to a constraint on the average KL divergence between old and new policies, a trust region, and proved that this guarantees monotonic improvement under idealised conditions. It required second-order computations (conjugate gradients with Fisher-vector products) that are awkward at the scale of a language model. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimisation, Schulman et al., 2017)[5] achieved much the same with first-order optimisation by clipping the ratio inside the objective:

\[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb E_t\Big[\min\big(\varrho_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\;\operatorname{clip}(\varrho_t(\theta),1-\epsilon_{\text{clip}},1+\epsilon_{\text{clip}})\,\hat A_t\big)\Big], \]

with \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\) typically 0.2. Figure 23.4 plots one term as a function of the ratio, and it explains the design better than words.

Figure 23.4 shows the asymmetry that makes PPO safe. The minimum takes the more pessimistic of the clipped and unclipped terms. When the update has already moved a token's probability far enough in the direction its advantage favours, the clipped term wins, the objective becomes flat in \(\theta\), and that token stops contributing gradient: the policy cannot profit from moving it further on this batch. When the update has moved a token in the wrong direction, the unclipped term wins and the gradient remains, pulling it back. The result is a soft trust region implemented by masking, which lets PPO run several epochs of minibatch updates on one batch of rollouts. The full PPO loss adds a value-regression loss for the critic and, often, an entropy bonus to keep exploring.

Drill 23.3 — Which tokens get a gradient?

With \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}=0.2\), four tokens in a minibatch have (ratio, advantage) equal to (1.3, +0.5), (1.3, −0.5), (0.7, +0.5) and (0.7, −0.5). Which contribute a policy gradient?

Solution. (1.3, +0.5): \(\varrho\hat A=0.65\) and the clipped term is \(1.2\times0.5=0.6\); the minimum is the clipped term, which is constant in \(\theta\), so there is no gradient (it has already been pushed up enough). (1.3, −0.5): \(-0.65\) versus \(-0.6\); the minimum is the unclipped \(-0.65\), so there is a gradient, pushing the probability back down. (0.7, +0.5): \(0.35\) versus \(0.4\); the minimum is the unclipped \(0.35\), so there is a gradient, pushing the probability back up. (0.7, −0.5): \(-0.35\) versus \(-0.4\); the minimum is the clipped \(-0.4\), so there is no gradient. Two of the four tokens are masked: exactly those that moved too far in their favoured direction.

23.7 PPO for language models, and what it costs

Applying PPO to a language model, as InstructGPT did, requires four networks, which Figure 23.5 arranges into one training step. The actor is the policy being trained. The critic predicts values, usually initialised from the reward model or the SFT model, with a scalar head. The reference is the frozen SFT model, used for the KL penalty. The reward model scores completed responses (Chapter 24). One step has three phases. In the generation phase, the actor samples responses to a batch of prompts: this is ordinary autoregressive inference. In the scoring phase, the reward model scores each response, and the reference and the critic each run one forward pass over every sampled token, to provide KL penalties and values. In the training phase, GAE turns rewards and values into advantages, and the actor and critic take several epochs of minibatch gradient steps on the clipped objective and the value loss.

Figure 23.5 makes the cost structure visible, and two numbers matter. Memory: with all four networks the size of the policy, the bill is \(16N+16N+2N+2N=36N\) bytes, 289 GB for an 8B model and 2.5 TB for a 70B one, before the KV cache of generation and the activations of training. Smaller critics and reward models, LoRA actors, and offloading frozen models between phases reduce it. Time: the phases have opposite characters. Generation is memory-bound decoding, which, by Chapter 3, runs at a small fraction of the GPU's FLOP rate unless batches are large. Scoring and training are compute-bound passes over all tokens at once, like prefill. For short responses the phases take comparable time; for long ones generation dominates, because its cost grows with the number of sequential decode steps.

Worked example — the time budget of one PPO step for Llama 3 8B

Setup. 512 prompts, one response each of 1,000 tokens, on 8 H100s; actor, critic, reference and reward model all 8B; two PPO epochs.

Generation. Each GPU decodes 64 sequences. By the chat example of §3.8, a decode step at batch 64 and about 2k context takes about 13 ms, so 1,000 tokens take about 13 s, plus prefill and the long tail of the slowest response.

Scoring. Three forward passes (reward, reference, critic) over \(5.12\times10^5\) tokens at \(2N\) FLOPs each: \(6N\times5.12\times10^5\approx2.5\times10^{16}\) FLOPs.

Training. Two epochs of actor and critic updates at \(6N\) each (\(8N\) with checkpointing): \(2\times2\times8N\times5.12\times10^5\approx1.3\times10^{17}\) FLOPs. Scoring plus training is about \(1.6\times10^{17}\) FLOPs; at 40% MFU on 8 GPUs (3.2 PFLOP/s) that is about 50 s.

Consequence. Here training costs more than generation. Make the responses 16,000 tokens long, as reasoning models do, and generation grows to over 200 s of sequential decoding at falling batch efficiency, while the long tail of the slowest responses leaves GPUs idle; generation then dominates. That shift, from training-bound to inference-bound RL, is why Chapter 25's systems are built around inference engines.

Production RLHF systems are therefore inference systems with training attached. OpenRLHF[13] and veRL[14], two widely used open frameworks of 2024, run the generation phase on vLLM or SGLang engines and the training phase on FSDP or Megatron, either on separate GPU pools that exchange weights after each step, or colocated on the same GPUs, which switch roles and reshard the weights between the inference layout of Chapter 18 and the training layout of §16.3. Either way the updated actor must reach the generator before the next step, which is the weight-synchronisation problem that §26.7 estimated. Huang et al. (2024) documented[12] more than twenty implementation details, from reward normalisation to the treatment of the end-of-sequence token, that were needed to reproduce OpenAI's results, a reminder that PPO for language models is as much engineering as mathematics.

We have now built the whole RL machine except its most important input. Every formula in this chapter takes a reward \(\mathcal R(x,y)\) as given. For a summary, a chat answer or a story, where does that number come from? Humans cannot score millions of rollouts, and they are unreliable at assigning absolute scores. They are much better at comparing two answers. The next chapter turns comparisons into rewards.

23.8 The sampler is part of the estimator

Every gradient in this chapter assumed that the responses were drawn from \(p_\theta\), the distribution whose log-probabilities appear in the score. In a real RL system the responses are drawn by an inference engine, and Chapter 4 taught us that inference engines rarely sample from \(p_\theta\) exactly. They apply a temperature \(\tau\), truncate the tail with top-\(p\) or top-\(k\), and compute logits with kernels whose results depend on the batch (§4.8). Each of these changes the sampling distribution to some \(q\ne p_\theta\), and the policy gradient computed as if \(q\) were \(p_\theta\) is then biased.

The cleanest case is temperature. If tokens are drawn from \(q_\tau\propto p_\theta^{1/\tau}\), the unbiased estimator weights each sampled sequence by \(p_\theta(y\mid x)/q_\tau(y\mid x)\), a product of per-token ratios that, over a thousand tokens, varies over many orders of magnitude, so the correction is useless in practice. Truncation is worse: top-\(p\) gives \(q=0\) to tokens that \(p_\theta\) assigns positive probability, and no reweighting can account for samples that are never drawn. The standard response is to sample rollouts at \(\tau=1\) with no truncation, so that \(q=p_\theta\) by construction, and to rely on the KL penalty and the policy's own sharpening to keep outputs sensible. When a system does sample with a different temperature, it must compute the log-probabilities of the sampling distribution consistently in both the ratio and the KL term, or accept the bias knowingly.

Figure 23.6 shows a subtler mismatch, which appeared as RL systems scaled in 2024 and 2025. The generation engine and the training engine are different programs. One uses inference kernels, perhaps FP8 weights, continuous batching and paged attention; the other uses training kernels in BF16 under FSDP. They compute slightly different log-probabilities for the same tokens. The differences are usually tiny, but the PPO ratio and the importance weights amplify them, and occasionally a token that the sampler found likely is one the trainer finds very unlikely. Two practices follow. First, the "old" log-probabilities \(\log p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) in the PPO ratio are recomputed by the trainer on the sampled tokens, not taken from the inference engine, so that the ratio compares like with like. Second, the remaining gap between the sampler's distribution and the trainer's is treated as a small off-policy correction: the per-token ratio of trainer to sampler probabilities is computed and truncated at a modest cap, which bounds the variance of the correction while removing most of the bias. Several groups reported in 2025 that this correction stabilised long RL runs that had otherwise collapsed.

The lesson for the inference engineer is that, inside an RL loop, the inference engine is no longer just a server. Its sampling settings, numerical precision and determinism are part of the statistical estimator, and changing a kernel or a quantisation format can change the training dynamics. The same holds, more strongly, for the asynchronous systems of Chapter 25, in which rollouts are generated by a policy that is several updates old.

Drill 23.4 — Temperature and the importance weight

A rollout of 500 tokens is sampled at \(\tau=0.8\). Suppose that at each position the sampled token has \(p_\theta=0.6\) and \(q_\tau=0.7\). What is the sequence-level importance weight needed to correct the policy gradient, and why is this impractical?

Solution. The weight is \(\prod_t p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)/q_\tau(y_t\mid s_t)=(0.6/0.7)^{500}=e^{500\ln(0.857)}=e^{-77}\approx4\times10^{-34}\). Other sequences, containing tokens that the lower temperature made less likely, would have weights far above one. A handful of sequences would carry almost all the weight, and the estimator's variance would be enormous. Per-token corrections with truncation, or sampling at \(\tau=1\), are the practical alternatives.

Looking ahead

This chapter derived the policy gradient, reduced its variance with baselines, advantages and GAE, protected the model with a KL penalty to a reference, and stabilised the optimisation with PPO's clipped surrogate. The KL-regularised objective turned out to have a closed-form optimum, \(p^*\propto p_{\text{ref}}e^{\mathcal R/\beta_{\text{KL}}}\), which we have not yet exploited. Chapter 24 supplies the reward: it shows how InstructGPT trained a reward model from human comparisons using the Bradley–Terry model, why optimising against that model eventually hurts, and then inverts the closed-form optimum to obtain DPO, which learns from preferences directly with a classification loss and no reinforcement learning at all.

Chapter summary

  • RL fine-tuning maximises \(J(\theta)=\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[\mathcal R(x,y)]\). Generation is a token-level MDP with prefixes as states, tokens as actions, deterministic transitions and a sparse terminal reward.
  • Score-function identity: \(\nabla\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[f]=\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[f\nabla\log p_\theta]\). REINFORCE is reward-weighted SFT on the model's own samples; with 0/1 rewards it is rejection-sampling fine-tuning.
  • Baselines \(b(x)\) or \(b(s_t)\) leave the gradient unbiased, because \(\mathbb E[\nabla\log p_\theta]=0\), and remove prompt-to-prompt reward scale. The advantage \(A^{p}=Q^{p}-V^{p}\) assigns credit to the tokens that changed the outlook.
  • GAE: \(\hat A_t=\sum_l(\gamma_d\lambda_{\text{GAE}})^l\delta_{t+l}\) with \(\delta_t=\mathcal R_t+\gamma_dV(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\); \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=0\) is TD (biased, low variance) and \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=1\) is Monte Carlo minus a baseline.
  • The KL-regularised objective \(\mathbb E[\mathcal R]-\beta_{\text{KL}}\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\) has optimum \(p^*\propto p_{\text{ref}}e^{\mathcal R/\beta_{\text{KL}}}\) (the Gibbs variational principle). It limits reward hacking, forgetting and collapse.
  • KL estimators per token: \(k_1=-\log\varrho^{\text{ref}}\), \(k_2=\frac12(\log\varrho^{\text{ref}})^2\), \(k_3=\varrho^{\text{ref}}-1-\log\varrho^{\text{ref}}\) (unbiased and non-negative).
  • PPO maximises \(\mathbb E[\min(\varrho_t\hat A_t,\operatorname{clip}(\varrho_t,1\pm\epsilon_{\text{clip}})\hat A_t)]\): tokens that have moved far enough in their favoured direction stop receiving gradient, allowing several epochs per batch of rollouts.
  • PPO for LLMs uses an actor, a critic, a reference and a reward model: about \(36N\) bytes of weights and state. Generation is memory-bound inference and dominates the step for long responses.
  • The sampler is part of the estimator: sample rollouts at \(\tau=1\) without truncation, recompute old log-probabilities in the trainer, and correct the remaining sampler–trainer mismatch with truncated importance weights.

In the token-level MDP of a language model, the state at step \(t\) is:

  1. The last token only
  2. The prompt and all tokens generated so far

Why can't we simply backpropagate the reward \(\mathcal R(x,y)\) into the model's parameters?

  1. The reward is always negative
  2. Tokens are sampled discretely and the reward is often a black box, so it has no gradient with respect to the logits
  3. The model has no gradients
  4. Rewards are too large
This is why the score-function estimator is needed.

Which limitations of SFT does RL from judged samples remove?

  1. The model can only be as good as its demonstrations
  2. The need for a pre-trained model
  3. The model never learns from the consequences of its own mistakes
  4. The need for GPUs
RL trains on the model's own samples and their scores.

The score-function identity states that \(\nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[f(y)]\) equals:

  1. \(\mathbb E[\nabla f(y)]\)
  2. \(\mathbb E[f(y)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y)]\)
  3. \(\mathbb E[f(y)]\nabla\log\mathbb E[p_\theta]\)
  4. \(f(\mathbb E[y])\)
\(\nabla p=p\nabla\log p\); it requires no derivative of \(f\).

The REINFORCE gradient on one sampled response equals:

  1. The SFT gradient on a human demonstration
  2. The SFT (log-likelihood) gradient on the model's own sample, multiplied by its reward
  3. The gradient of the reward model
  4. Zero in expectation
Good samples are made more likely, in proportion to their reward.

With a 0/1 reward, sampling many responses, keeping the correct ones and fine-tuning on them is:

  1. Unrelated to RL
  2. REINFORCE with a binary reward (rejection-sampling fine-tuning or expert iteration, as in STaR)
  3. PPO
  4. DPO
Failed samples contribute zero; successful ones contribute their log-likelihood gradient.

In the two-answer example with \(p=0.2\), the REINFORCE estimate has mean 0.16 and standard deviation:

  1. 0.04
  2. 0.16
  3. 0.32
  4. 0.8
It is 0.8 with probability 0.2 and 0 otherwise: variance \(0.128-0.0256=0.1024\).

Why does subtracting a baseline \(b(x)\) not bias the policy gradient?

  1. Because \(b\) is small
  2. Because \(\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[\nabla\log p_\theta(y\mid x)]=\nabla\sum_yp_\theta(y\mid x)=0\)
  3. Because the reward is normalised
  4. It does bias it, slightly
The score has zero mean, so any factor independent of \(y\) multiplies zero.

Without a baseline, a batch with one easy prompt (rewards near 1) and one hard prompt (rewards near 0) mostly:

  1. Improves the hard prompt
  2. Pushes up everything sampled for the easy prompt, regardless of relative quality
  3. Has zero gradient
  4. Diverges
Raw rewards mix the prompt's difficulty with the sample's quality; baselines separate them.

The advantage \(A^{p}(s_t,a_t)\) is:

  1. The reward of the token
  2. \(Q^{p}(s_t,a_t)-V^{p}(s_t)\): how much better than expected the outcome is after choosing \(a_t\)
  3. The KL divergence to the reference
  4. The log-probability of the token
It assigns credit to tokens that changed the expected outcome.

Replacing the total reward by the reward-to-go at each token is valid because:

  1. Future rewards are discounted
  2. An action cannot affect rewards that were received before it, so those terms have zero expected contribution
  3. It is an approximation
  4. The critic requires it
Causality removes terms whose expectation is zero, reducing variance.

In PPO for language models, the critic is usually:

  1. A human annotator
  2. A copy of a language model with a scalar head, trained to predict reward-to-go from each prefix
  3. The reference model
  4. A lookup table
It doubles the trained parameters and must judge partial responses, which is hard.

The TD error is \(\delta_t=\)

  1. \(\mathcal R_t-V(s_t)\)
  2. \(\mathcal R_t+\gamma_dV(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\)
  3. \(V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\)
  4. \(\sum_k\mathcal R_k\)
One observed reward plus the critic's estimate of the rest, minus the critic's prior estimate.

GAE with \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=1\) and \(\gamma_d=1\) reduces to:

  1. The one-step TD error
  2. The Monte Carlo return-to-go minus the value baseline
  3. Zero
  4. The reward model score
The intermediate values telescope away.

Lowering \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}\) toward 0:

  1. Increases variance and reduces bias
  2. Reduces variance but increases reliance on (and bias from) the critic
  3. Has no effect
  4. Removes the need for a critic
TD estimates use one random reward but trust the learned values.

In the four-token example (\(V=(0.5,0.4,0.7,0.8,0)\), final reward 1), with \(\lambda_{\text{GAE}}=0\) which token gets the largest advantage?

  1. Token 1
  2. Token 2
  3. Token 3
  4. Token 4
\(\delta_2=0.7-0.4=0.3\), the largest one-step improvement in the critic's estimate.

Which problems does the KL penalty to a reference policy mitigate?

  1. Reward hacking against an imperfect reward model
  2. Slow decoding
  3. Loss of fluency and diversity (collapse) learned in pre-training and SFT
  4. The need for a reward
It keeps the policy near the reference distribution.

The maximiser of \(\mathbb E_{p}[\mathcal R]-\beta_{\text{KL}}\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\) is:

  1. The highest-reward response, deterministically
  2. \(p^*(y)\propto p_{\text{ref}}(y)\exp(\mathcal R(y)/\beta_{\text{KL}})\)
  3. \(p_{\text{ref}}\)
  4. Uniform over responses
This is the Gibbs variational principle; it underlies DPO.

Under the KL-regularised optimum, a response that \(p_{\text{ref}}\) assigns zero probability is:

  1. Chosen if its reward is highest
  2. Never produced, whatever its reward
  3. Produced with probability \(e^{\mathcal R/\beta_{\text{KL}}}\)
  4. Produced half the time
\(p^*\) is a reweighting of \(p_{\text{ref}}\), so it stays on its support.

Multiplying all rewards by 2 is equivalent, for the optimum, to:

  1. Multiplying \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\) by 2
  2. Dividing \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\) by 2
  3. No change
  4. Squaring \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\)
Only \(\mathcal R/\beta_{\text{KL}}\) appears in \(p^*\).

Which per-token KL estimator is unbiased and always non-negative?

  1. \(k_1=-\log\varrho\)
  2. \(k_2=\frac12(\log\varrho)^2\)
  3. \(k_3=\varrho-1-\log\varrho\)
  4. None
\(\log u\le u-1\) and \(\mathbb E[\varrho]=1\). \(k_1\) can be negative; \(k_2\) is biased.

Why does PPO use the ratio \(\varrho_t=p_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\)?

  1. To compute the KL penalty
  2. To reuse rollouts from the old policy for several gradient steps (importance sampling)
  3. To normalise rewards
  4. To compute values
Generating rollouts is expensive, so each batch is used for several epochs.

TRPO and PPO both aim to:

  1. Remove the need for rewards
  2. Keep each policy update within a region where the surrogate objective is trustworthy
  3. Increase the learning rate
  4. Train the reward model
TRPO uses an explicit KL constraint with second-order steps; PPO clips the ratio.

With \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}=0.2\), a token with \(\hat A>0\) and ratio 1.3 contributes:

  1. A large gradient pushing its probability up
  2. No policy gradient: it has already moved far enough in its favoured direction
  3. A gradient pushing its probability down
  4. An infinite loss
The clipped term is the minimum and is constant in \(\theta\).

A token with \(\hat A<0\) whose probability has risen (ratio 1.3) contributes:

  1. No gradient
  2. A gradient that pushes its probability back down
  3. A gradient pushing it further up
  4. A value-loss term only
Moves in the harmful direction are never clipped.

Which networks does PPO for language models use?

  1. Actor (policy) and critic, both trained
  2. A discriminator
  3. A frozen reference model and a frozen reward model
  4. A draft model
Four networks in total.

With all four networks the size of an 8B policy, the weights and training state need about:

  1. 32 GB
  2. 128 GB
  3. 289 GB
  4. 1.1 TB
\(16N+16N+2N+2N=36N\) bytes.

Which PPO phase is memory-bound and grows with response length through sequential steps?

  1. Scoring
  2. Generation (autoregressive decoding)
  3. Advantage computation
  4. The optimiser step
Scoring and training process all tokens in parallel, like prefill.

Frameworks such as OpenRLHF and veRL generate rollouts with:

  1. The training framework's forward pass
  2. Production inference engines such as vLLM or SGLang
  3. A CPU sampler
  4. Beam search
Generation efficiency dominates RL cost, so inference engines are used.

Why are RL rollouts usually sampled at temperature 1 without top-\(p\) truncation?

  1. It is faster
  2. So that the sampling distribution equals \(p_\theta\) and the policy gradient is unbiased; truncation gives zero probability to tokens \(p_\theta\) can produce
  3. To increase rewards
  4. Because engines cannot truncate
Sequence-level importance weights for other temperatures have enormous variance.

Why should the trainer recompute the "old" log-probabilities rather than use those reported by the inference engine?

  1. The engine does not report them
  2. Different kernels and precisions give slightly different log-probabilities, which the PPO ratio amplifies
  3. To save memory
  4. For determinism of sampling
The remaining sampler–trainer gap can be corrected with truncated importance weights.

Why is the reparameterisation (pathwise) estimator unavailable for language-model RL?

  1. It is biased
  2. Tokens are discrete and rewards are often non-differentiable, so the sample is not a smooth function of \(\theta\)
  3. It needs more memory
  4. It requires a critic
The score-function estimator needs only log-probabilities and samples.

Warm-up How is text generation an RL problem? Define state, action, transition and reward.

The policy is the language model \(p_\theta\). The state is the prompt plus the tokens generated so far, \(s_t=(x,y_{<t})\). The action is the next token. The transition deterministically appends it. The reward is usually a single number \(\mathcal R(x,y)\) given when the response ends, from a reward model, a checker or a test. The objective is \(J=\mathbb E_x\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[\mathcal R]\). Equivalently, at the sequence level it is a contextual bandit with the whole response as one action. Distinctive features: a huge action space but a strong initial policy, deterministic dynamics, sparse delayed reward, and episodes of hundreds to tens of thousands of steps.

Core Derive the policy gradient for a language model.

\(\nabla J=\nabla\sum_yp_\theta(y\mid x)\mathcal R(x,y)=\sum_y\mathcal R\,p_\theta\nabla\log p_\theta=\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[\mathcal R(x,y)\nabla\log p_\theta(y\mid x)]\), using \(\nabla p=p\nabla\log p\). Since \(\log p_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_t\log p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)\), the gradient is \(\mathbb E[\mathcal R\sum_t\nabla\log p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)]\). REINFORCE estimates it by averaging over sampled responses. Interpretation: reward-weighted maximum likelihood on the model's own samples. Then improve it: subtract a baseline (unbiased since the score has mean zero), use reward-to-go by causality, and replace the reward by an advantage estimate.

Core Why do baselines reduce variance without introducing bias? What baseline would you use?

For any \(b\) independent of the sampled action, \(\mathbb E[b\nabla\log p_\theta]=b\nabla\sum p_\theta=0\), so the expectation is unchanged. The variance falls because subtracting a quantity correlated with the reward removes the part of the reward that does not distinguish good samples from bad ones, especially the prompt-to-prompt differences in reward scale. Natural choices: the expected reward on the prompt, estimated by a learned critic \(V(s)\) (PPO), or by the mean reward of several samples for the same prompt (GRPO, RLOO). The exactly variance-optimal constant is a score-weighted mean of the reward, but the plain expected reward is standard and close in practice. A baseline that depends on the sampled action itself would introduce bias.

Core Explain GAE and the role of \(\lambda\).

With a critic \(V\), the one-step TD error \(\delta_t=\mathcal R_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\) estimates the advantage with low variance but is biased by errors in \(V\). The Monte Carlo estimate \(\sum_{k\ge t}\mathcal R_k-V(s_t)\) is unbiased but noisy. GAE takes \(\hat A_t=\sum_l(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}\): \(\lambda=0\) gives TD, and \(\lambda=1\) gives Monte Carlo (the values telescope). Intermediate \(\lambda\) trusts the critic more for the distant future. For LLMs, with \(\gamma=1\) and a terminal reward, \(\lambda\) near 0.95–1 is typical because the critic is unreliable; a small \(\lambda\) with a good critic gives fine-grained credit to the tokens that changed the predicted outcome.

Core Why add a KL penalty to a reference model in RLHF? What happens if \(\beta\) is too small or too large?

The reward model is imperfect, so unconstrained optimisation exploits it (reward hacking: verbose, sycophantic or degenerate text that scores well). The KL term \(\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\) keeps the policy near the SFT model, preserving fluency, knowledge and diversity, and it limits how far the policy can move into regions where the reward model is unreliable. If \(\beta\) is too small, the policy drifts, hacks the reward and can collapse. If it is too large, the policy barely changes and the reward gains are small. Because only \(\mathcal R/\beta\) matters, \(\beta\) must be tuned with the reward's scale; some systems adapt it to target a KL budget.

Deep-dive Derive the optimal policy of the KL-regularised objective.

For fixed \(x\), maximise \(F(p)=\sum_yp(y)\mathcal R(y)-\beta\sum_yp(y)\log(p(y)/p_{\text{ref}}(y))\) over distributions. Define \(p^*(y)=p_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{\mathcal R(y)/\beta}/Z\). Then \(\mathcal R(y)=\beta\log(p^*(y)/p_{\text{ref}}(y))+\beta\log Z\). Substituting gives \(F(p)=\beta\log Z-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert p^*)\), maximised uniquely at \(p=p^*\) since KL is non-negative and zero only at equality. (A Lagrangian with the normalisation constraint gives the same answer.) Consequences: the optimum reweights the reference exponentially by reward, stays on its support, and interpolates between \(p_{\text{ref}}\) (\(\beta\to\infty\)) and the best reachable response (\(\beta\to0\)). Inverting it, \(\mathcal R=\beta\log(p^*/p_{\text{ref}})+\beta\log Z\), is the starting point of DPO.

Core Explain PPO's clipped objective and why it is needed.

Rollouts are expensive, so we want several gradient steps per batch. Importance sampling gives the surrogate \(\mathbb E[\varrho_t\hat A_t]\) with \(\varrho_t=p_\theta/p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), whose gradient at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the policy gradient. But it is accurate only near \(\theta_{\text{old}}\), and maximising it freely makes ratios explode and can collapse the policy. PPO maximises \(\mathbb E[\min(\varrho_t\hat A_t,\operatorname{clip}(\varrho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t)]\). For a good token, once the ratio exceeds \(1+\epsilon\) the objective is flat and the gradient zero; for a bad token, once the ratio falls below \(1-\epsilon\), likewise. Moves in the harmful direction are not clipped, so they are corrected. It is a first-order approximation of TRPO's KL trust region. Typical settings: \(\epsilon=0.2\), a few epochs of minibatches, plus value loss and an optional entropy bonus.

Core What are the four models in RLHF with PPO, and what does each cost?

The actor (policy, trained: about 16 bytes per parameter of weights and state). The critic (value model, trained: 16 bytes per parameter, often initialised from the reward model). The reference (frozen SFT model, about 2 bytes per parameter, for the KL penalty). The reward model (frozen, about 2 bytes per parameter, scores complete responses). With equal sizes that is \(36N\) bytes: 289 GB for 8B and 2.5 TB for 70B, plus the generation KV cache and training activations. Per step: generation by the actor; forward passes of reward, reference and critic over all tokens; then several epochs of actor and critic updates. Mitigations: smaller critic or reward model, LoRA actor (the reference is then the base with adapters off), offloading frozen models between phases, or dropping the critic (GRPO, RLOO).

Design Estimate the time of one PPO step for an 8B model with 512 prompts of 1,000-token responses on 8 H100s, and say what dominates.

Generation: 64 sequences per GPU; at batch 64 and about 2k context a decode step takes about 13 ms, so 1,000 tokens take about 13 s, plus prefill and the long tail. Scoring: three forward passes over 512k tokens, \(6N\times5.12\times10^5\approx2.5\times10^{16}\) FLOPs. Training: two epochs of actor and critic at about \(8N\) per token with checkpointing, \(\approx1.3\times10^{17}\) FLOPs. Total compute is about \(1.6\times10^{17}\) FLOPs, roughly 50 s at 40% MFU across 8 GPUs. So training dominates at this length. With 16k-token responses, generation exceeds 200 s and the long tail idles GPUs, so generation dominates. Levers: larger generation batches, a faster inference engine, asynchronous generation, length caps, and dropping the critic.

Deep-dive Why is learning a critic for language models hard, and what are the alternatives?

The critic must predict, from any prefix, the expected final reward: in effect, judging an unfinished response. That is as hard as reward modelling, with noisier targets (Monte Carlo returns of single samples), and it must track a moving policy. A poor critic adds bias through GAE without removing much variance, and it doubles trained memory and compute. Alternatives: Monte Carlo baselines from multiple samples per prompt (RLOO's leave-one-out mean, GRPO's group mean and standard deviation), ReMax (the greedy response's reward as baseline), or REINFORCE with a running mean. These remove the critic entirely at the cost of generating several responses per prompt. With verifiable rewards and long responses, this trade has won in practice (Chapter 25).

Core Compare the \(k_1\), \(k_2\) and \(k_3\) estimators of KL divergence.

With samples \(y\sim p_\theta\) and \(\varrho=p_{\text{ref}}(y)/p_\theta(y)\): \(k_1=-\log\varrho\) is unbiased for \(\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\) but has high variance and can be negative on individual samples. \(k_2=\frac12(\log\varrho)^2\) is always non-negative and low-variance but biased (it matches KL to second order when the distributions are close). \(k_3=\varrho-1-\log\varrho\) is unbiased (since \(\mathbb E[\varrho]=1\)), non-negative (since \(\log u\le u-1\)) and low-variance: \(\varrho-1\) acts as a control variate. PPO-RLHF typically puts \(-\beta k_1\) into per-token rewards; GRPO adds \(\beta k_3\) to the loss.

Design You are building the rollout side of an RLHF system. What do you need from the inference engine?

High throughput: continuous batching, large batches, paged KV, prefix sharing across samples of the same prompt, and fast handling of the long tail (refill freed slots, cap lengths). Correct sampling: temperature 1 and no truncation by default, or consistent log-probabilities of whatever distribution is used. Logprob fidelity: the trainer recomputes old log-probs, and the engine's numerics (kernels, FP8, batch-invariance) should be close enough that truncated importance correction suffices. Fast weight updates: load new actor weights after each step via NCCL broadcast or in-place resharding when colocated, without restarting the engine or losing the CUDA graphs. Memory coordination: release the KV cache when the trainer runs on the same GPUs. Observability: per-step generation time, tail lengths and logprob-mismatch statistics.

Warm-up What is reward hacking? Give examples.

Reward hacking is when the policy increases the measured reward without improving the intended quality, by exploiting flaws in the reward signal. Examples: a reward model that prefers longer answers yields verbose padding; one that likes confident tone yields confident falsehoods; sycophancy (agreeing with the user) when raters liked agreement; code that special-cases visible tests; formatting tricks the reward model over-scores. Mitigations: KL penalty to the reference, better and ensembled reward models, length normalisation, held-out human evaluation, early stopping on gold-standard metrics, and verifiable rewards where possible.

Deep-dive Why does sampling at a temperature other than 1 bias the policy gradient, and what do systems do about it?

The estimator \(\mathbb E_{y\sim p_\theta}[\hat A\nabla\log p_\theta]\) assumes samples from \(p_\theta\). Sampling from \(q_\tau\propto p_\theta^{1/\tau}\) or a top-\(p\)-truncated \(q\) changes the expectation, so the estimate is biased. Exact correction multiplies by \(\prod_tp_\theta/q\), whose variance is astronomically large for long sequences (for example, \((0.6/0.7)^{500}\approx10^{-34}\)). Truncation cannot be corrected at all, since \(q=0\) where \(p_\theta>0\). Systems sample at \(\tau=1\) with no truncation, recompute old log-probs in the trainer, and apply per-token, truncated importance weights for small residual mismatches between engine and trainer numerics.

Core How does REINFORCE relate to rejection-sampling fine-tuning and to SFT?

SFT maximises log-likelihood of fixed demonstrations: its gradient is \(\sum_t\nabla\log p_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)\) on human data. REINFORCE uses the same gradient on the model's own sample, weighted by its reward (or advantage). With 0/1 rewards and no baseline, it is exactly SFT on the successful samples, which is rejection-sampling fine-tuning or expert iteration (STaR). Differences: RL is on-policy (it must resample as the model changes); with baselines it also pushes down below-average samples, which pure rejection sampling never does; and its weights can be continuous. Rejection sampling done offline in large rounds is a simple, stable, but less sample-efficient form of policy gradient.

Design Your PPO run's reward keeps rising, but human evaluators say outputs are getting worse. Diagnose and fix.

This is reward over-optimisation, or hacking. Check: KL to the reference (probably large and growing), response length (often growing), and the reward model's scores against a held-out gold set or fresh human labels; look at samples for padding, sycophancy or formatting tricks. Fixes: raise \(\beta\) or target a KL budget; stop at the checkpoint where gold metrics peak; add a length penalty or normalise reward by length; retrain or ensemble the reward model on the policy's current outputs (iterated RLHF); constrain with rules or verifiable sub-rewards; reduce learning rate or PPO epochs. Longer term, collect comparisons on on-policy samples, since the reward model is least reliable where the policy now lives.
