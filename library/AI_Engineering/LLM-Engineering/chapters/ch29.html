Part VIII · Post-Training and Reasoning — Chapter 24

Learning from Human Preferences

People find it hard to write the perfect answer, and harder still to score an answer on an absolute scale, but they can usually say which of two answers is better. This chapter turns such comparisons into training signal: first into a reward model that the reinforcement learning of Chapter 23 can optimise, and then, by inverting the closed-form optimum of the KL-regularised objective, into DPO, which learns from comparisons directly with a loss no harder to compute than supervised fine-tuning.

Why this chapter

Chapter 23 built a machine that improves a language model given a reward \(\mathcal R(x,y)\) for each response, and left open where the reward comes from. For a mathematics problem, a checker can supply it; Chapter 25 takes that route. For most of what a chat assistant does, there is no checker: whether an explanation is clear, a refusal appropriate or a summary faithful is a matter of human judgement. Asking people to assign numbers is unreliable, because different raters use the scale differently and the same rater drifts. Asking them to compare two responses is far more consistent. So the question of this chapter is how to learn from comparisons.

The story is short and recent. Christiano et al. (2017) showed[1] that a reward function learned from human comparisons of short video clips could teach a simulated robot to do a backflip. Stiennon et al. (2020) used the same idea[2] to train summarisers whose output people preferred to human-written reference summaries. Ouyang et al.'s InstructGPT (2022)[3] applied it to general instruction following, and found that people preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 it started from. That three-stage pipeline, supervised fine-tuning, then a reward model trained on comparisons, then PPO against it, is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and it was the recipe behind ChatGPT. Anthropic (Bai et al., 2022) studied it at scale[4] for helpfulness and harmlessness, and then replaced much of the human labelling with AI feedback guided by a written constitution. In 2023, Rafailov et al. showed[6] that the reward model and the reinforcement learning could be folded into a single classification loss, DPO, which made preference learning almost as easy as fine-tuning and was adopted by most open models, including Llama 3, within a year.

For an inference engineer, the systems contrast is the point. RLHF with PPO is a large inference workload with training attached. Offline DPO needs no generation at all. Online and iterative variants bring generation and judging back, as inference jobs of their own. Knowing which method needs which computation is what lets one estimate the cost of an alignment pipeline, and interviewers ask for exactly that.

24.1 From demonstrations to comparisons

Why are comparisons better data than demonstrations or scores? A demonstration requires a rater to produce the best response, which is slow and caps the model at the rater's ability. A score requires an absolute scale that raters do not share: one rater's 7 out of 10 is another's 5. A comparison requires only a relative judgement on two responses that already exist, and relative judgements are both faster and more consistent. They also capture qualities that are hard to demonstrate: a rater can recognise that one explanation is clearer than another without being able to write the clearest possible one.

The data collection loop of InstructGPT is typical. A prompt \(x\) is drawn from real usage. The current model, initially the SFT model of §16.1, samples several responses. A trained rater ranks them, and each ranking of \(K\) responses yields \(\binom K2\) pairwise comparisons \((x,y_w,y_l)\), where \(y_w\) is the preferred ("winning") response and \(y_l\) the other. InstructGPT collected rankings of between 4 and 9 responses per prompt, about 33,000 prompts' worth for the reward model. Figure 24.1 shows how this data feeds the three stages of RLHF.

Figure 24.1 also shows a dependency that will matter later. The comparisons are made on samples from a particular policy. A reward model is reliable on responses that resemble the ones it was trained on, and as RL moves the policy away from the SFT model, its samples drift into regions where the reward model has seen little data. Iterating, by collecting new comparisons on the new policy's samples, is the remedy; Anthropic and Meta both ran RLHF in several such rounds. With the data in hand, we need a model that turns comparisons into a reward.

24.2 Reward models and the Bradley–Terry model

We want a function \(\mathcal R_\phi(x,y)\) such that preferred responses score higher. The Bradley–Terry model (1952)[7], devised for ranking chess players and sports teams from match results, supplies the probabilistic link. It posits that each response has a latent quality and that the probability of one beating another depends only on the difference:

\[ P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)\;=\;\sigma\big(\mathcal R(x,y_w)-\mathcal R(x,y_l)\big)\;=\;\frac{e^{\mathcal R(x,y_w)}}{e^{\mathcal R(x,y_w)}+e^{\mathcal R(x,y_l)}}, \]

where \(\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})\) is the logistic function. Maximum likelihood on a dataset \(\mathcal D\) of comparisons gives the reward-model loss

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{RM}}(\phi)\;=\;-\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\mathcal R_\phi(x,y_w)-\mathcal R_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big], \]

a logistic regression on the difference of two scores. Two properties of the model shape everything that follows. First, only differences of rewards for the same prompt are identified: adding any function \(c(x)\) to \(\mathcal R\) changes no probability. A reward model's raw outputs are therefore meaningless across prompts unless normalised, which is why RLHF implementations standardise rewards and why per-prompt baselines (§23.3) are natural. Second, the scale is fixed by the data: a reward difference of 1 means the preferred response wins with probability \(\sigma(1)\approx0.73\), a difference of 2 with probability 0.88, and a difference of 4 with probability 0.98.

For the mathematician — Bradley–Terry as a generalised linear model

If each item \(i\) has a score \(s_i\) and we observe comparisons, the Bradley–Terry log-likelihood \(\sum_{(i\succ j)}\log\sigma(s_i-s_j)\) is a logistic regression whose design matrix has a \(+1\) and a \(-1\) in each row. It is concave in \(s\), so maximum likelihood has no spurious local optima, and the solution is unique up to the additive shift that the model cannot see, provided the comparison graph is connected and no item wins all its comparisons. If an item wins every comparison, the likelihood keeps increasing as its score goes to infinity: the same pathology that §24.5 finds in DPO. The Elo rating system of chess is stochastic gradient ascent on this likelihood, one game at a time, with a base-10 logistic and a scale of 400 points. Chatbot Arena's leaderboard fits exactly this model to millions of human votes between models. A neural reward model replaces the free scores \(s_i\) by a function \(\mathcal R_\phi(x,y)\) of the text, which generalises to responses never compared, but loses concavity in \(\phi\).

In practice, reward models are trained for only one or two epochs, because they overfit comparison data quickly; InstructGPT found a single epoch best. All \(\binom K2\) pairs from one ranking are placed in the same batch, both to share the forward pass over the \(K\) responses and to avoid overfitting to correlated pairs spread across batches. After training, the scores are normalised (InstructGPT added a bias so that the labellers' demonstrations scored zero on average), which fixes the otherwise arbitrary offset before the scores are used as RL rewards. The trained model is then frozen and served alongside the policy, scoring one response at a time, as an inference workload in its own right: a prefill over the prompt and response, with no decoding.

The reward model itself is a language model, usually initialised from the SFT model, with its vocabulary head replaced by a linear layer that maps the final hidden state of the last token to a scalar. Figure 24.2 shows the forward pass on a pair. Both responses are processed with the same prompt, which makes training cheaper with prefix sharing, and the loss compares the two scalars.

How good can a reward model be? Its accuracy on held-out comparisons is bounded by the agreement of the raters themselves, because the labels are noisy. InstructGPT reported inter-rater agreement of about 73%, and reward models of that era reached accuracies in the high 60s to low 70s. A reward model that agrees with raters 70% of the time is a noisy instrument, and optimising a policy hard against a noisy instrument is the problem of the next section.

Worked example — reading a reward model

Setup. For one prompt, a reward model scores three responses \(\mathcal R=(1.2,\;0.4,\;-0.3)\).

Pairwise probabilities. Response 1 beats response 2 with probability \(\sigma(0.8)=0.69\), response 3 with \(\sigma(1.5)=0.82\), and response 2 beats 3 with \(\sigma(0.7)=0.67\).

Choosing among them. Under Bradley–Terry, the probability that response \(i\) would be ranked first in a comparison against all others is not given by one formula, but the softmax \(e^{\mathcal R_i}/\sum_je^{\mathcal R_j}=(0.59,\,0.27,\,0.13)\) is the natural "which would a rater pick from all three" probability under the related Plackett–Luce model.

Invariance. Adding 10 to all three scores changes none of these numbers. A response scored 1.2 for this prompt and a response scored 0.9 for a different prompt cannot be compared.

Loss. If a rater prefers response 2 over response 1, the loss on that pair is \(-\log\sigma(0.4-1.2)=-\log0.31=1.17\) nats, and its gradient pushes \(\mathcal R_2\) up and \(\mathcal R_1\) down with weight \(\sigma(0.8)=0.69\), the model's probability of the wrong answer.

24.3 Optimising against a reward model, and over-optimisation

Given \(\mathcal R_\phi\), there are two ways to use it. The first is the KL-regularised RL of Chapter 23: PPO maximises \(\mathbb E[\mathcal R_\phi]-\beta_{\text{KL}}\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\). The second needs no training at all: best-of-\(n\) sampling draws \(n\) responses from the reference policy and returns the one the reward model likes most. Best-of-\(n\) is a remarkably strong baseline. It is also a useful mathematical object, because its distance from the reference has a closed form: the KL divergence between the best-of-\(n\) distribution and \(p_{\text{ref}}\) is (for continuous rewards without ties)

\[ \mathrm{KL}_{\text{BoN}}(n)\;=\;\log n-\frac{n-1}{n}. \]

Best-of-16 moves 1.84 nats from the reference; best-of-1,024 moves only 5.9. Figure 24.3 plots the formula. Each doubling of \(n\), which doubles the inference cost, buys only about 0.69 nats more. Best-of-\(n\) is therefore a cheap way to get a small move and an expensive way to get a large one, which is why it is used as a baseline, for generating fine-tuning data (Llama 2's "rejection sampling" rounds) and at inference time for high-value queries, but not as a substitute for training.

Whatever the method, optimising a learned reward has a limit, and Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured it precisely[8]. They trained a large "gold" reward model on synthetic labels, used it to label data for smaller "proxy" reward models, and then optimised policies against the proxies while measuring the gold reward. As the policy moved away from its starting point, measured by \(d=\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}\), the proxy reward rose steadily, but the gold reward rose, peaked and then fell. They fitted simple functional forms: for best-of-\(n\), gold reward \(\approx d(a-bd)\); for RL, \(\approx d(a-b\log d)\). Figure 24.4 shows the shape. This is Goodhart's law made quantitative: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Larger reward models and more comparison data pushed the peak further out but did not remove it.

Figure 24.4 explains the role of every safeguard in RLHF. The KL coefficient sets how far along the horizontal axis the optimum lies (§23.5). Early stopping on a held-out gold metric stops near the peak. Larger, ensembled or regularly retrained reward models move the peak to the right. What the over-optimised policy actually does is often recognisable. Longer responses are the most common exploit: Singhal et al. (2023) found[9] that much of the reward gain in several RLHF pipelines could be reproduced by optimising length alone. Sycophancy, agreeing with the user's stated view, is another: Sharma et al. (2023) traced it[10] to raters' preference for agreement. Hedging, flattery and formatting that the reward model over-values complete the list.

Drill 24.1 — The KL divergence of best-of-\(n\)

Derive \(\mathrm{KL}_{\text{BoN}}(n)=\log n-(n-1)/n\), assuming the reward of a response drawn from \(p_{\text{ref}}\) has a continuous distribution.

Solution. Let \(u\in[0,1]\) be the quantile of a response's reward under \(p_{\text{ref}}\), which is uniform for samples from \(p_{\text{ref}}\). The best of \(n\) independent samples has quantile distributed as the maximum of \(n\) uniforms, with density \(nu^{n-1}\). The best-of-\(n\) policy reweights each response by the density ratio \(nu^{n-1}\) at its quantile, so \(\mathrm{KL}=\mathbb E_{u\sim nu^{n-1}}[\log(nu^{n-1})]=\log n+(n-1)\mathbb E[\log u]\). For the maximum of \(n\) uniforms, \(\mathbb E[\log u]=\int_0^1nu^{n-1}\log u\,\mathrm du=-1/n\). Hence \(\mathrm{KL}=\log n-(n-1)/n\). Ties in reward reduce it, so the formula is an upper bound for discrete rewards.

24.4 Direct preference optimisation

RLHF as described needs a reward model, a critic, a reference, online sampling and the delicate PPO machinery of Chapter 23. Rafailov et al. (2023) noticed that the closed-form optimum of the KL-regularised objective (§23.5) allows all of it except the reference to be eliminated. Start from the optimum for a reward \(\mathcal R\),

\[ p^*(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{\mathcal R(x,y)/\beta_{\text{KL}}}, \]

and solve it for the reward:

\[ \mathcal R(x,y)\;=\;\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\frac{p^*(y\mid x)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\;+\;\beta_{\text{KL}}\log Z(x). \]

The intractable partition function \(Z(x)\), a sum over all possible responses, depends only on the prompt. Substitute this expression into the Bradley–Terry probability, which involves only the difference of two rewards for the same prompt, and \(Z(x)\) cancels:

\[ P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)\;=\;\sigma\Big(\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\frac{p^*(y_w\mid x)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\frac{p^*(y_l\mid x)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big). \]

Every reward model corresponds to an optimal policy, and the preference probabilities can be written in terms of that policy alone. So instead of fitting a reward model and then finding its optimal policy, we can fit the policy directly by maximum likelihood on the comparisons, replacing \(p^*\) by the trainable \(p_\theta\). The result is the DPO loss:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)\;=\;-\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\frac{p_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\frac{p_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big]. \]

This is the reward-model loss of §24.2 with the reward model replaced by the implicit reward \(\hat{\mathcal R}_\theta(x,y)=\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\big(p_\theta(y\mid x)/p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\big)\), which the paper's title summarised as "your language model is secretly a reward model". Each log-probability is a sum of per-token log-probabilities computed by one forward pass, so the loss needs four forward passes per comparison (policy and reference on both responses), the reference ones only once, and no sampling. Training is as simple as supervised fine-tuning.

The gradient shows what DPO does. Differentiating,

\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\beta_{\text{KL}}\,\mathbb E\Big[\sigma\big(\hat{\mathcal R}_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat{\mathcal R}_\theta(x,y_w)\big)\,\big(\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big]. \]

It raises the likelihood of the preferred response and lowers that of the rejected one, like a contrastive form of SFT, and it weights each pair by \(\sigma(\hat{\mathcal R}_l-\hat{\mathcal R}_w)\), the implicit reward model's probability of getting the pair wrong. Pairs it already ranks correctly by a wide margin contribute little. The reference enters only through the implicit reward, and its role is the KL leash of RLHF: the loss depends on how far the policy's log-probabilities have moved from the reference's, not on their absolute values. Figure 24.5 compares the two pipelines.

Worked example — one DPO step by hand

Setup. \(\beta_{\text{KL}}=0.1\). For one comparison, the reference assigns log-probabilities \(-120\) to \(y_w\) and \(-110\) to \(y_l\); the current policy assigns \(-118\) and \(-111\).

Implicit rewards. \(\hat{\mathcal R}_w=0.1\times(-118+120)=0.2\) and \(\hat{\mathcal R}_l=0.1\times(-111+110)=-0.1\). The margin is 0.3.

Loss. \(-\log\sigma(0.3)=-\log0.574=0.555\) nats. At initialisation, when \(p_\theta=p_{\text{ref}}\), the margin is 0 and the loss is \(\log2=0.693\).

Gradient weight. \(\sigma(-0.3)=0.43\): the pair still pulls with almost half its maximum weight. Notice that the reference preferred \(y_l\) (log-probability \(-110\) against \(-120\)), perhaps because it is shorter. DPO does not care about absolute log-probabilities, only about changes relative to the reference, so it can still learn to prefer \(y_w\).

Consequence. The pair's contribution falls as the margin grows: at a margin of 3 its weight is \(\sigma(-3)=0.05\). Pairs the policy already gets right fade out, as in logistic regression.

24.5 What DPO gets wrong, and the variants

DPO's derivation is exact, but it rests on assumptions that practice violates, and each violation has produced a variant. The first is that the optimum is reached over all responses. The DPO loss sees only the responses in the dataset, which were sampled from some other policy, often an earlier model or a different one entirely. Nothing in the loss controls what \(p_\theta\) does to responses outside the dataset, and a widely observed symptom is that training lowers the likelihood of both the chosen and the rejected responses, the chosen one less, moving probability mass to responses that no one has judged. The policy can improve its margin on the data while drifting in unmeasured directions.

The second assumption is that preferences are noisy. If a pair is always decided the same way, Bradley–Terry's maximum-likelihood solution sets the reward difference to infinity, and so DPO keeps increasing the margin \(\hat{\mathcal R}_w-\hat{\mathcal R}_l\) without bound, which means moving arbitrarily far from the reference whatever \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\) is; the KL leash that was supposed to regularise the solution does not, on finite deterministic data. Azar et al.'s IPO (2023)[11] replaced the log-sigmoid by a squared loss that targets a finite margin, with \(h_\theta=\log\frac{p_\theta(y_w)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\log\frac{p_\theta(y_l)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\):

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{IPO}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb E\Big[\Big(h_\theta-\frac{1}{2\beta_{\text{KL}}}\Big)^2\Big], \]

so that the margin is pulled toward \(1/(2\beta_{\text{KL}})\) and no further.

The third assumption is that data comes in pairs. Much real feedback is a thumbs up or down on a single response. Ethayarajh et al.'s KTO (2024)[12] learns from such unpaired labels with a loss inspired by prospect theory. Each response's implicit reward is compared with a reference point (an estimate of the policy's KL from the reference): desirable responses are pushed above it, undesirable ones below, through a saturating sigmoid. Two further variants remove the reference model entirely, saving its memory and forward passes. ORPO (Hong et al., 2024)[13] adds to the SFT loss on the chosen response a penalty on the log odds ratio between chosen and rejected, so that preference learning happens during fine-tuning in a single stage. SimPO (Meng et al., 2024)[14] uses the length-normalised log-probability \(\frac{\beta_{\text{KL}}}{n_y}\log p_\theta(y\mid x)\) as the implicit reward, with a target margin \(m_{\text{SimPO}}\); the normalisation by length counters DPO's tendency to favour longer responses, which have larger absolute log-ratios.

Drill 24.2 — Why DPO's margin grows without bound

Suppose a dataset contains the same comparison \((x,y_w,y_l)\) many times and never the reverse. Show that the DPO loss has no minimiser, and that IPO's does.

Solution. The DPO loss on this data is \(-\log\sigma(\beta_{\text{KL}}h_\theta)\), strictly decreasing in the margin \(h_\theta\) and approaching 0 only as \(h_\theta\to\infty\). Any finite margin can be improved, so gradient descent keeps raising \(\log p_\theta(y_w)/p_{\text{ref}}(y_w)\) and lowering \(\log p_\theta(y_l)/p_{\text{ref}}(y_l)\), and the policy moves arbitrarily far from the reference, contrary to the intent of the KL term. (With a population of preferences \(P(y_w\succ y_l)=q<1\), the minimiser is finite, \(\beta_{\text{KL}}h=\log\frac q{1-q}\), but a finite sample of a deterministic judgement looks like \(q=1\).) IPO's loss \((h_\theta-1/(2\beta_{\text{KL}}))^2\) is minimised at \(h_\theta=1/(2\beta_{\text{KL}})\), a finite margin whatever the data.

24.6 Offline, iterative and online preference learning

RLHF with PPO is online: the policy samples fresh responses at every step, and the reward model judges them. Offline DPO is not: its responses were sampled once, before training, from some other policy. The difference matters for the same reason that baselines and advantages matter in Chapter 23. The most useful training signal is about the responses the current policy actually produces, and an offline dataset says less and less about them as the policy moves. Xu et al. (2024) compared carefully tuned PPO[15] with DPO and found PPO better across dialogue and code benchmarks, attributing much of DPO's gap to distribution shift between the data and the policy. Guo et al. (2024) showed[16] that online DPO, in which the current policy samples two responses per prompt and an LLM judge labels them at every step, recovered most of the gap.

In between lies iterative preference learning, which has become the dominant recipe for open models. In each round, the current policy samples several responses per prompt; a reward model or judge ranks them; the best and worst form new pairs, or the best are kept for rejection-sampling SFT; and the policy is trained by DPO on the new data before the next round. Llama 2 used rounds of rejection sampling and PPO[17]; Llama 3 used several rounds of SFT, rejection sampling and DPO[18]; AI2's Tülu 3[19] combined SFT, on-policy DPO and a final stage of reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Each round is offline within itself and online across rounds, trading some freshness for the stability and simplicity of DPO.

The systems consequence is that "DPO needs no generation" is true only of the offline version. Iterative and online preference learning reintroduce sampling and judging, and with them the inference workloads of earlier chapters. Generating candidates is batched decoding; judging them is mostly prefill. The next section looks at the judges, and §24.8 adds up the bill.

24.7 AI feedback and LLM judges

Human comparisons are slow and expensive, a few dollars each for expert raters, and they limit how often a preference dataset can be refreshed. Anthropic's Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022)[5] replaced much of the human labelling for harmlessness with a model guided by a short list of written principles, the constitution. In a supervised phase the model critiques and revises its own responses according to the principles, and the revisions become SFT data. In a reinforcement-learning phase, the model compares pairs of responses according to the principles, and these AI preferences train the reward model. This is RLAIF, reinforcement learning from AI feedback. Lee et al. (2023) found that RLAIF[20] matched RLHF on summarisation and dialogue tasks when the labelling model was strong enough.

Language models are now used as judges throughout post-training: to label pairs for DPO, to score samples for rejection sampling, and to evaluate models (Chapter 28). Zheng et al. (2023) showed[21] that strong judges agreed with human preferences about as often as humans agreed with each other on chat benchmarks, and documented their biases: a preference for the first position, for longer answers and for their own outputs. The standard mitigations are to judge each pair in both orders and average, to ask for reasoning before the verdict, and to calibrate against human labels. Generative reward models push the idea further, training a model to produce a written judgement and then a score, so that the reward benefits from reasoning at the cost of generating tokens for every judgement.

Worked example — labelling 100,000 pairs with a 70B judge

Setup. Each judgement prompt contains a rubric, the user prompt and two responses, about 1,500 tokens, and the judge writes about 200 tokens of reasoning and a verdict. The judge is Llama 3 70B in BF16 on 8×H100 servers. To counter position bias, every pair is judged in both orders: 200,000 judgements.

Prefill. \(2N\) FLOPs per token: \(2\times70.6\times10^9\times1500\times2\times10^5\approx4.2\times10^{19}\) FLOPs. At 50% MFU, 494 TFLOP/s per GPU, that is about 85,000 GPU-seconds, 24 GPU-hours. Prefix caching of the shared rubric would cut it somewhat.

Decode. \(4\times10^7\) output tokens. From the 70B example of §3.6, one server decoding 64 sequences at 4k context produces about 6,000 tokens per second, so decoding takes about 6,700 server-seconds, 15 GPU-hours.

Cost. About 40 GPU-hours, roughly $100 at $2.50 per GPU-hour, against tens of thousands of dollars for 100,000 expert human comparisons. AI feedback makes preference data cheap enough to regenerate every round on the current policy's samples, which is precisely what online and iterative methods need.

24.8 The cost of alignment, method by method

We can now compare the methods as an inference engineer would, by the computation each needs. Take a policy of \(N\) parameters and a reward model or judge of similar size.

RLHF with PPO keeps four models, about \(36N\) bytes of weights and training state (§23.7), and each step generates rollouts, scores them with three forward passes and trains two networks. It is the most expensive method and the most flexible: it is fully online, and it can use any reward. Offline DPO keeps the policy's training state (\(16N\) bytes) and needs the reference only for one forward pass over the dataset, whose log-probabilities can be precomputed and stored, so its cost is that of supervised fine-tuning on twice the response tokens, plus that pass. Online or iterative DPO adds, per round, the generation of candidates by the policy and the labelling by a judge: inference costs of the kind estimated above, without PPO's critic or its delicate optimisation.

Figure 24.6 summarises the trade. Methods to the top are online and adaptive and pay for it in inference; methods to the bottom are cheap and static. For a team with a fixed preference dataset and a modest budget, DPO or SimPO is the obvious first step. For a team that can afford to generate and judge every round, iterative DPO captures most of the benefit of online RL without PPO's fragility. Full PPO remains the choice when the reward is complex, when online adaptation matters most, or when the same infrastructure is being built for the verifiable-reward RL of Chapter 25.

Worked example — offline DPO on Llama 3 8B

Data. 100,000 comparisons with 500-token prompts and 400-token responses. Each pair is processed as two sequences of 900 tokens: \(1.8\times10^8\) tokens.

Reference pass. One forward pass at \(2N\) per token: \(2\times8.03\times10^9\times1.8\times10^8\approx2.9\times10^{18}\) FLOPs, done once and stored as two numbers per pair.

Training. One epoch at \(6N\) per token: \(8.7\times10^{18}\) FLOPs. Together, \(1.2\times10^{19}\) FLOPs, about 8 GPU-hours at 40% MFU on H100s, the same order as the SFT run of §16.1.

Memory. As for full fine-tuning, 128 GB of state, so ZeRO-2 across a server; or LoRA, in which case the reference model is simply the base with the adapter switched off, and no second copy is needed at all.

Every method in this chapter, however clever its loss, inherits the weakness of its reward: human preferences are noisy, judges are biased, and reward models can be hacked. For some tasks, none of that is necessary, because the answer can simply be checked.

Looking ahead

Preference learning taught models to be helpful and harmless, but its rewards are soft: a reward model agrees with people about 70% of the time, and optimising it too hard makes things worse. Mathematics and programming offer something better, a reward that is simply correct or not, checked by comparing an answer or running tests. Chapter 25 studies reinforcement learning with such verifiable rewards. It derives GRPO, the critic-free algorithm behind DeepSeek-R1, from the policy gradient and baselines of Chapter 23, examines the fixes that later work made to it, and shows why the resulting training runs are dominated by the generation of very long responses, which turns RL into one of the largest inference workloads in the industry.

Chapter summary

  • Comparisons are more consistent than scores or demonstrations. RLHF: SFT → reward model from comparisons → PPO against it with a KL penalty, iterated on fresh samples.
  • Bradley–Terry: \(P(y_w\succ y_l)=\sigma(\mathcal R_w-\mathcal R_l)\); the reward-model loss is logistic regression on the difference. Only within-prompt differences are identified; a difference of 1 means 73% preference.
  • Best-of-\(n\) moves \(\log n-(n-1)/n\) nats from the reference. Optimising a proxy reward raises it monotonically while the true reward peaks and falls (Goodhart; Gao et al.). Length and sycophancy are common exploits.
  • DPO inverts \(p^*\propto p_{\text{ref}}e^{\mathcal R/\beta_{\text{KL}}}\): the implicit reward \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\log(p_\theta/p_{\text{ref}})\) in the Bradley–Terry likelihood gives \(\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma(\hat{\mathcal R}_w-\hat{\mathcal R}_l)\), with \(Z(x)\) cancelled. No reward model, no critic, no sampling.
  • DPO's gradient raises \(y_w\), lowers \(y_l\), weighted by the implicit model's probability of misranking the pair.
  • Failure modes: offline distribution shift (both likelihoods fall), unbounded margins on deterministic data (IPO fixes), pairs required (KTO), reference model cost (ORPO, SimPO), length bias (SimPO).
  • Online and iterative DPO regain on-policy data at the cost of generation and judging; well-tuned PPO can still beat offline DPO.
  • RLAIF and LLM judges make labels cheap (about 40 GPU-hours for 200,000 70B judgements) but bring position, length and self-preference biases.

Why do RLHF pipelines collect pairwise comparisons rather than absolute scores?

  1. Comparisons need fewer prompts
  2. Relative judgements are faster and more consistent across raters than absolute scales
  3. Scores cannot be used in RL
  4. Comparisons remove the need for an SFT model
Raters use absolute scales differently and drift over time; comparisons avoid the problem.

A rater ranks 6 responses to one prompt. How many pairwise comparisons does this yield?

  1. 5
  2. 6
  3. 15
  4. 36
\(\binom62=15\).

The three stages of InstructGPT-style RLHF are:

  1. Pre-training, distillation, quantisation
  2. SFT, reward-model training on comparisons, PPO against the reward model
  3. DPO, IPO, KTO
  4. Prompting, retrieval, fine-tuning
The SFT model also serves as the reference for the KL penalty.

Why are RLHF rounds iterated with new comparisons?

  1. To reduce the KL penalty
  2. The reward model is reliable only near the samples it was trained on, and the policy's samples drift away from them
  3. To train the critic
  4. Because raters get tired
Fresh comparisons on the new policy's samples keep the reward accurate where the policy lives.

Under Bradley–Terry, if \(\mathcal R(y_w)-\mathcal R(y_l)=2\), the probability that \(y_w\) is preferred is about:

  1. 0.50
  2. 0.73
  3. 0.88
  4. 0.98
\(\sigma(2)\approx0.881\).

Adding a constant \(c(x)\) to every reward for prompt \(x\):

  1. Changes the preference probabilities
  2. Changes nothing, since only within-prompt differences enter Bradley–Terry
  3. Breaks the loss
  4. Doubles the KL
Raw reward-model scores are therefore not comparable across prompts without normalisation.

The reward-model loss on a comparison is:

  1. \((\mathcal R_w-\mathcal R_l)^2\)
  2. \(-\log\sigma(\mathcal R_\phi(x,y_w)-\mathcal R_\phi(x,y_l))\)
  3. \(-\log\mathcal R_\phi(x,y_w)\)
  4. \(\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal R_w\Vert\mathcal R_l)\)
Maximum likelihood of the Bradley–Terry model: logistic regression on the difference.

A typical LLM reward model is:

  1. A small MLP on bag-of-words features
  2. A language model (often initialised from the SFT model) with a scalar head on the last token's hidden state
  3. The policy itself
  4. A human rater
It shares the backbone architecture; only the head changes.

Why is a reward model's held-out accuracy typically only around 70%?

  1. The models are too small
  2. Rater labels are noisy; InstructGPT's inter-rater agreement was about 73%, which bounds achievable accuracy
  3. Bradley–Terry is wrong
  4. Overfitting is impossible to avoid
A model cannot agree with a label more often than raters agree with each other, on average.

The KL divergence of best-of-\(n\) sampling from the reference is approximately:

  1. \(n\)
  2. \(\sqrt n\)
  3. \(\log n-(n-1)/n\)
  4. \(1/n\)
Best-of-16 is about 1.84 nats; each doubling adds about 0.69 nats.

Why is best-of-\(n\) an expensive way to move far from the reference?

  1. It needs a critic
  2. Its KL grows only logarithmically in \(n\) while inference cost grows linearly
  3. It cannot use a reward model
  4. It is biased
RL pays the cost once in training instead of at every query.

Gao et al. (2022) found that as a policy is optimised against a proxy reward model:

  1. Both proxy and gold rewards rise forever
  2. The proxy reward keeps rising while the gold reward peaks and then falls
  3. The gold reward falls immediately
  4. KL divergence decreases
Goodhart's law; the peak moves out with larger reward models and more data.

Which are common forms of reward hacking in RLHF?

  1. Producing longer responses
  2. Lower perplexity on pre-training data
  3. Sycophancy: agreeing with the user's stated view
  4. Faster decoding
Singhal et al. found length explained much of the reward gain in several pipelines; Sharma et al. traced sycophancy to rater preferences.

The key step that makes DPO tractable is:

  1. Approximating \(Z(x)\) by sampling
  2. Substituting \(\mathcal R=\beta_{\text{KL}}\log(p^*/p_{\text{ref}})+\beta_{\text{KL}}\log Z(x)\) into Bradley–Terry, where \(Z(x)\) cancels in the difference
  3. Using a critic
  4. Ignoring the reference model
The partition function depends only on the prompt.

DPO's implicit reward for a response is:

  1. \(\log p_\theta(y\mid x)\)
  2. \(\beta_{\text{KL}}\log\big(p_\theta(y\mid x)/p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\big)\)
  3. The reward model's score
  4. The response length
"Your language model is secretly a reward model."

At initialisation (\(p_\theta=p_{\text{ref}}\)), the DPO loss on every pair is:

  1. 0
  2. \(\log2\approx0.693\)
  3. 1
  4. Infinite
The margin is zero, and \(-\log\sigma(0)=\log2\).

Each pair's contribution to the DPO gradient is weighted by:

  1. Its length
  2. \(\sigma(\hat{\mathcal R}_l-\hat{\mathcal R}_w)\), the implicit model's probability of ranking the pair wrongly
  3. The reward model's confidence
  4. A constant
Pairs that are already correctly ranked by a wide margin fade out.

Which components of RLHF-PPO does offline DPO eliminate?

  1. The reward model
  2. The critic and online sampling
  3. The reference policy's log-probabilities
  4. The preference data
DPO still needs reference log-probabilities, which can be precomputed.

With \(\beta_{\text{KL}}=0.1\), the policy raises \(\log p(y_w)\) by 2 and lowers \(\log p(y_l)\) by 1 relative to the reference. The margin is:

  1. 0.1
  2. 0.3
  3. 1
  4. 3
\(0.1\times(2-(-1))=0.3\); the loss is \(-\log\sigma(0.3)\approx0.55\).

A frequently observed behaviour of offline DPO training is that:

  1. The chosen response's likelihood always rises
  2. The likelihoods of both chosen and rejected responses fall, the rejected more, shifting mass to unjudged responses
  3. The model stops changing
  4. The reference model is updated
The loss controls only the margin on dataset responses.

IPO replaces DPO's log-sigmoid loss with a squared loss in order to:

  1. Remove the reference model
  2. Target a finite margin, so deterministic preferences do not push the policy arbitrarily far from the reference
  3. Handle unpaired data
  4. Normalise by length
The margin is pulled toward \(1/(2\beta_{\text{KL}})\).

KTO is designed for:

  1. Pairwise rankings
  2. Unpaired binary feedback (a thumbs up or down on single responses)
  3. Verifiable rewards
  4. Distillation
It compares each response's implicit reward with a reference point, inspired by prospect theory.

Which methods need no reference model?

  1. DPO
  2. ORPO
  3. SimPO
  4. IPO
ORPO adds an odds-ratio penalty to SFT; SimPO uses length-normalised log-probabilities with a margin.

SimPO normalises the implicit reward by response length mainly to:

  1. Speed up training
  2. Counter the bias toward longer responses, whose summed log-ratios are larger in magnitude
  3. Allow unpaired data
  4. Remove the KL penalty
Length is a common exploit of preference objectives.

Xu et al. (2024) attributed much of offline DPO's gap to well-tuned PPO to:

  1. The choice of optimiser
  2. Distribution shift between the fixed dataset's responses and the current policy's
  3. A bug in DPO
  4. Model size
Online DPO with fresh samples and an AI judge recovers most of the gap.

Iterative DPO, as used for Llama 3, consists of:

  1. One pass of DPO on human data
  2. Rounds in which the current policy generates candidates, a reward model or judge ranks them, and DPO trains on the new pairs
  3. PPO with a DPO critic
  4. Distillation from GPT-4
Offline within each round, on-policy across rounds.

In Constitutional AI, the AI feedback is used to:

  1. Pre-train the model
  2. Critique and revise responses for SFT data, and label preference pairs that train the reward model
  3. Replace the tokenizer
  4. Quantise the model
A short list of written principles guides both phases.

Which biases of LLM judges did Zheng et al. (2023) document?

  1. Preference for the first position
  2. Preference for longer answers
  3. Preference for shorter answers in all cases
  4. Inability to judge code
Also self-preference. Judge both orders and calibrate against humans.

Labelling 200,000 judgements of 1,500 input tokens with a 70B judge needs prefill compute of about:

  1. \(4\times10^{15}\) FLOPs
  2. \(4\times10^{17}\) FLOPs
  3. \(4\times10^{19}\) FLOPs
  4. \(4\times10^{23}\) FLOPs
\(2\times70.6\times10^9\times1500\times2\times10^5\): about 24 H100-hours at 50% MFU.

With LoRA, why does DPO not need a second copy of the model as reference?

  1. DPO never uses a reference
  2. The reference is the base model with the adapter switched off
  3. The reference is quantised
  4. The critic replaces it
Reference log-probabilities can also be precomputed once.

The compute of offline DPO on 100,000 pairs for an 8B model is closest to that of:

  1. Pre-training the model
  2. A supervised fine-tuning run on twice the response tokens, plus one reference forward pass
  3. One PPO step
  4. Zero, since no sampling is needed
About \(1.2\times10^{19}\) FLOPs, around 8 H100-hours.

DPO and RLHF-PPO with a Bradley–Terry reward model optimise:

  1. Different objectives
  2. The same KL-regularised preference objective, under different data regimes (offline vs on-policy)
  3. The same objective, but DPO ignores the KL term
  4. Supervised cross-entropy
At the population optimum they coincide; in practice the data each sees differs.

Warm-up Describe the RLHF pipeline end to end.

Stage 1: supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations gives \(p_{\text{SFT}}\), which is also the reference policy. Stage 2: for many prompts, sample several responses from the SFT model; raters rank them; convert the rankings to pairs \((x,y_w,y_l)\); train a reward model (an LM with a scalar head) by the Bradley–Terry loss \(-\log\sigma(\mathcal R_w-\mathcal R_l)\). Stage 3: optimise the policy with PPO to maximise \(\mathbb E[\mathcal R_\phi]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\), with the four models of Chapter 23. Iterate stages 2 and 3, collecting comparisons on the new policy's samples, so the reward model stays accurate where the policy lives. Evaluate with held-out human preferences, since the reward model's score is not trustworthy as an evaluation once it has been optimised against.

Core Explain the Bradley–Terry model and the reward-model loss. What is not identifiable?

Bradley–Terry posits a latent score per item, and \(P(i\succ j)=\sigma(s_i-s_j)\). For responses, \(P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma(\mathcal R(x,y_w)-\mathcal R(x,y_l))\). Maximum likelihood gives \(\mathcal L=-\mathbb E\log\sigma(\mathcal R_\phi(x,y_w)-\mathcal R_\phi(x,y_l))\), logistic regression on the score difference. Not identifiable: any per-prompt shift \(c(x)\), since only within-prompt differences appear, so scores must be normalised and are not comparable across prompts. The scale is fixed by the label noise: a difference of 1 corresponds to 73% preference. Rankings of \(K\) items give \(\binom K2\) pairs, or a Plackett–Luce likelihood.

Core Derive DPO.

The KL-regularised objective \(\mathbb E[\mathcal R]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\) has optimum \(p^*=p_{\text{ref}}e^{\mathcal R/\beta}/Z(x)\). Invert it: \(\mathcal R(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{p^*(y\mid x)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\). Plug into Bradley–Terry: \(P(y_w\succ y_l)=\sigma(\beta\log\frac{p^*(y_w)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\beta\log\frac{p^*(y_l)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_l)})\); \(Z(x)\) cancels because it depends only on the prompt. Parameterise \(p^*\) by \(p_\theta\) and maximise the likelihood of the observed preferences: \(\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb E\log\sigma(\beta\log\frac{p_\theta(y_w)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}-\beta\log\frac{p_\theta(y_l)}{p_{\text{ref}}(y_l)})\). It is the reward-model loss with the implicit reward \(\beta\log(p_\theta/p_{\text{ref}})\). There is no reward model, no critic and no sampling; reference log-probs can be precomputed.

Deep-dive What does the DPO gradient do, and why can DPO lower the likelihood of the chosen response?

\(\nabla\mathcal L=-\beta\,\mathbb E[\sigma(\hat{\mathcal R}_l-\hat{\mathcal R}_w)(\nabla\log p_\theta(y_w)-\nabla\log p_\theta(y_l))]\). It raises the chosen response and lowers the rejected one, weighted by the implicit model's probability of misranking the pair. The loss depends only on the difference of log-ratios, so it is satisfied as long as \(y_l\) falls faster than \(y_w\). The two responses often share most of their tokens, and the gradient to lower \(y_l\) also pushes down shared tokens in \(y_w\). Both likelihoods can fall, the mass moving to responses outside the dataset (likelihood displacement). This is harmless if the mass moves to good unseen responses, harmful if to bad ones. Mitigations: add an SFT term on \(y_w\) (as in ORPO and many DPO recipes), use on-policy pairs, use IPO-style bounded margins, or regularise the chosen likelihood.

Core DPO vs PPO: when would you use each?

DPO: a fixed preference dataset, limited compute or expertise, stability and simplicity desired. Its cost is like SFT, and it needs only the policy and precomputed reference log-probs. PPO: an explicit reward (reward model, verifier, mixture) and the budget to generate on-policy samples. PPO is online, adapts to the policy's current distribution, can use non-preference rewards, and, when well tuned, has outperformed offline DPO on dialogue and code (Xu et al., 2024). It costs four models and generation every step, with fragile hyperparameters. Middle ground: iterative or online DPO, where the current policy generates pairs and a reward model or judge labels them each round, capturing much of PPO's benefit with DPO's simplicity.

Core What is reward over-optimisation, and how do you detect and prevent it?

Optimising a learned proxy reward eventually lowers true quality: the proxy keeps rising while gold reward peaks and falls as KL from the start grows (Gao et al.: gold \(\approx d(a-bd)\) for best-of-\(n\), \(d(a-b\log d)\) for RL, with \(d=\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}\)). Detect it with held-out human evaluation or a stronger gold model; by tracking KL, response length and stylistic statistics; and by reading samples for padding, sycophancy or formatting exploits. Prevent it with an adequate KL coefficient or KL budget, early stopping at the gold peak, larger or ensembled reward models, reward models retrained on current policy samples, length penalties or normalisation, and verifiable rewards where available.

Deep-dive Why does DPO overfit on deterministic preferences, and how does IPO fix it?

With \(P(y_w\succ y_l)=q\), the Bradley–Terry optimum sets the margin to \(\log\frac q{1-q}\), finite for \(q<1\). A finite dataset in which a pair always goes one way looks like \(q=1\), and the DPO loss \(-\log\sigma(\beta h)\) decreases monotonically in the margin \(h\), with no finite minimiser. Gradient descent keeps widening the gap, moving the policy arbitrarily far from the reference, so the KL regularisation that motivated DPO is not enforced. IPO replaces the loss with \((h-\frac1{2\beta})^2\), whose minimum is at a finite margin. It regularises toward the reference regardless of label determinism, at the cost of not exactly matching Bradley–Terry probabilities.

Core Compare DPO, IPO, KTO, ORPO and SimPO.

DPO: pairs, reference model, log-sigmoid on the difference of log-ratios. IPO: pairs, reference, squared loss toward a finite margin; robust to deterministic labels. KTO: unpaired good/bad labels, reference, compares each response's implicit reward with a reference point through a prospect-theory value function. ORPO: pairs, no reference; SFT loss on the chosen response plus a log odds-ratio penalty, done in one stage. SimPO: pairs, no reference; implicit reward is length-normalised log-likelihood with a target margin, countering length bias. Reference-free methods save memory and a forward pass but lose the explicit KL anchor. All are usually run offline, and the choice of data (on-policy or not) often matters more than the choice of loss.

Design Design a preference-learning pipeline for a 70B chat model with a modest budget.

Start from a strong SFT model. Data: prompts from real traffic, deduplicated and balanced by category. Each round: generate 4–8 candidates per prompt with the current policy (an inference job: batched decoding on a serving engine). Label with a strong LLM judge under a rubric, both orders, with reasoning, calibrated against a few thousand human labels; add human labels for sensitive categories. Form best-versus-worst pairs. Train with DPO (or SimPO to avoid length bias) using LoRA or QLoRA to fit the budget, so the reference is the base model with the adapter off, with a small SFT term on chosen responses. Run 3–4 rounds. Evaluate on held-out human preference and safety sets, watching length and refusal rates. Costs per round: generation and judging (tens of GPU-hours per 100k pairs at 70B) plus training (similar to SFT).

Core What is RLAIF? What are the risks of LLM judges?

RLAIF replaces human preference labels with labels from a model, often guided by written principles (Constitutional AI) or a rubric. It is far cheaper (about $100 of GPU time for 200,000 judgements with a 70B judge versus tens of thousands of dollars for humans) and can be regenerated every round on the current policy's samples. Risks: position bias (prefer the first answer), verbosity bias, self-preference for the judge's own style or family, blind spots shared by judge and policy (errors the judge cannot detect are reinforced), and reward hacking against the judge. Mitigations: judge both orders, require reasoning before the verdict, use a stronger or different-family judge, calibrate against human labels, and spot-check with humans.

Deep-dive Derive the KL divergence of best-of-\(n\), and explain what it implies.

Let \(u\) be a response's reward quantile under \(p_{\text{ref}}\), uniform on [0,1]. The best of \(n\) samples has quantile density \(nu^{n-1}\), so the best-of-\(n\) distribution is \(p_{\text{ref}}\) reweighted by \(nu^{n-1}\). Then \(\mathrm{KL}=\mathbb E[\log n+(n-1)\log u]=\log n-(n-1)/n\), using \(\mathbb E[\log u]=-1/n\) for the maximum of \(n\) uniforms. Implications: KL grows only logarithmically in \(n\) (best-of-1024 is under 6 nats), so best-of-\(n\) is cheap for small moves and very expensive for large ones. It is a strong baseline and data generator (rejection sampling), and a reference point for measuring how far RL has moved the policy.

Design Estimate the GPU cost of one round of iterative DPO for an 8B policy with 100k prompts, 4 candidates each, judged by a 70B model.

Generation: 400k responses of about 400 tokens = \(1.6\times10^8\) tokens. At batch 64 an H100 decodes about 4,900 tokens/s for the 8B model, so about \(3.3\times10^4\) GPU-seconds, 9 GPU-hours, plus prefill. Judging: compare best and worst of 4, or all 6 pairs, in both orders. With 2 judgements per prompt, 200k judgements cost about 24 GPU-hours of prefill and 15 of decode (40 total). With all pairs, about 6 times more. Training: 100k pairs of 900-token sequences, about \(1.2\times10^{19}\) FLOPs, 8 GPU-hours. Total about 60 GPU-hours, roughly $150 at $2.50/h, dominated by judging. Levers: a smaller judge, pointwise scoring with a reward model instead of pairwise judging, prefix caching of the rubric, and fewer pairs per prompt.

Warm-up Why can DPO be described as "your language model is secretly a reward model"?

Every policy defines an implicit reward \(\hat{\mathcal R}_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log(p_\theta(y\mid x)/p_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x))\), up to a per-prompt constant, namely the reward for which it would be the KL-regularised optimum. DPO trains the policy so that this implicit reward fits the human preferences under Bradley–Terry. After training, the policy can be used as a reward model: score any response by its log-ratio to the reference. Such implicit rewards are less accurate than dedicated reward models out of distribution, but the idea unifies reward learning and policy learning into one step.

Core What is the role of the reference model in DPO, and what is lost without it?

The reference defines the KL anchor. The implicit reward is a log-ratio to it, so the loss is about changes relative to the reference, not absolute likelihoods, and a response the reference finds unlikely can still become preferred. It plays the role of RLHF's KL penalty: the derivation assumes the objective penalises \(\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\Vert p_{\text{ref}})\). Without it (ORPO, SimPO), the loss acts on absolute (often length-normalised) likelihoods. That saves a model's memory and forward passes, but the policy is anchored only by early stopping, the SFT term and margins, which can permit more drift. With LoRA, keeping the reference is free: disable the adapter.

Design Your DPO-trained model got more verbose and its win rate rose on an LLM-judge benchmark but not with humans. What happened, and what do you change?

Probably length bias reinforced twice. Preference data (human or AI) favours longer answers; DPO's summed log-ratios give longer responses larger implicit-reward magnitudes; and LLM judges also prefer length. So the policy learned verbosity, and the judge rewarded it. Checks: response length over training; judge win rate after length control (length-controlled win rates); human evaluation. Changes: length-balanced pairs (match lengths, or penalise length differences); SimPO's length-normalised reward or an explicit length penalty; a judge rubric that penalises padding, length-controlled metrics, and human spot checks; more on-policy pairs where the shorter answer wins when it is correct.

Deep-dive Is DPO equivalent to RLHF? Under what conditions do they differ?

At the population level, with infinite data covering all responses and the Bradley–Terry model correct, both have the same optimum, \(p^*\propto p_{\text{ref}}e^{\mathcal R/\beta}\), for the reward that best fits the preferences. They differ in practice because: DPO is fit offline on responses from another policy, so its behaviour off the data is unconstrained, whereas PPO samples on-policy and its reward model generalises (imperfectly) to new responses; finite deterministic data makes DPO's margins unbounded, while an RM's regularisation and the KL term in PPO keep things finite; the reward model in RLHF can be trained separately, ensembled and reused, while DPO's implicit reward is entangled with the policy; and optimisation dynamics differ (PPO's clipping and value baseline versus logistic regression). Online DPO narrows the gap by restoring on-policy data.
